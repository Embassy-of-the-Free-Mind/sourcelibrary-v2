\begin{abstract}
\noindent Multimodal LLMs can now OCR historical manuscripts at scale---but how much should a reader trust the result? We introduce a reference-free trust metric based on run-to-run self-consistency: transcribe the same page $N{=}5$ times and measure embedding cosine similarity between runs. On 50 pages spanning printed books and Leonardo da Vinci's mirror-script notebooks, consistency varies from 99\% on printed text to 83\% on Leonardo, with all five frontier models converging to the same range. Comparison against expert transcriptions reveals a ${\sim}$20-point ``calibration gap'': the model agrees with itself far more than with the ground truth. Per-sentence analysis shows that on Leonardo, \emph{not a single sentence} is reliably reproduced---a finding invisible to page-level scores. Image preprocessing (flipping + contrast) improves consistency from 84\% to 94\%, while reasoning models make it \emph{worse}. We identify ``informed confabulation''---topically correct but textually unfaithful output---and propose trust labels that let scholars judge reliability at a glance.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{OCR, historical manuscripts, self-consistency, trust scoring, Leonardo da Vinci, VLM hallucination, digital humanities}

\section{Introduction}

In February 2022, one of the authors encountered a 1497 edition of Ficino's \emph{De Mysteriis} at the Embassy of the Free Mind in Amsterdam. Bound within it was his \emph{Liber de Voluptate}---a Latin dialogue on pleasure that had never been translated into English. This led to Source Library, a project that has since OCR'd and translated over 17,000 historical books (15th--18th c.) using multimodal LLMs.

The pipeline works remarkably well on printed text. But when we turned it to Leonardo da Vinci's mirror-script notebooks, something unsettling happened: the AI produced fluent Italian about the right topics---optics on optics pages, water dynamics on water pages---but the \emph{specific text changed completely between runs}. Same model, same image, same prompt---different words every time.

Leonardo wrote in mirror-script: right to left, with reversed letterforms, probably because he was left-handed. His notebooks---roughly 7,000 pages across dozens of codices---contain some of the most important scientific writing of the Renaissance, covering optics, anatomy, fluid dynamics, botany, and engineering. Only about 60--70\% have been transcribed by scholars over the past 140 years. Automated transcription would be transformative---but our initial results suggested the AI was not reading at all.

We call this failure mode \textbf{informed confabulation}: the model knows what Leonardo writes \emph{about}, but cannot read what he actually \emph{wrote}. This paper develops a method to detect and quantify this phenomenon without requiring ground truth.

\textbf{Contributions:} (1)~A reference-free trust metric based on self-consistency, evaluated across 5 frontier models, 10 preprocessing methods, and 8 Leonardo codices. (2)~A measured ${\sim}$20-point ``calibration gap'' between self-consistency and expert accuracy, quantifying when the metric should not be trusted. (3)~Per-sentence trust labels that reveal \emph{zero} reliable sentences on Leonardo---invisible to page-level scores. (4)~``Informed confabulation'' as a named failure mode for VLM-based manuscript OCR, distinguished from random hallucination by its topical coherence.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Historical OCR.} Transkribus achieves 1.7\% CER on matched training data but degrades to 73\% out-of-domain~\cite{constum2022}. Ground truth is ``neither feasible nor affordable'' at scale~\cite{neudecker2021}. Yu et al.~\cite{yu2025} introduced AncientDoc, the first VLM benchmark for Chinese historical documents, finding that current models struggle significantly with classical scripts---a parallel to our mirror-script findings.

\textbf{LLM-based OCR.} Humphries et al.~\cite{humphries2024} showed GPT-4o, Claude, and Gemini achieve 5.7--7\% CER on 18th--19th c.\ handwriting, outperforming Transkribus. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024} introduced OCRBench, revealing VLM weaknesses on handwritten text. Crosilla et al.~\cite{crosilla2025} benchmarked LLMs for HTR, finding Claude 3.5 Sonnet leads on English but all models weaken on other languages and show no self-correction capability. Fu et al.~\cite{fu2025} showed in OCRBench~v2 that even the best models score below 50\% on hard OCR reasoning tasks.

\textbf{Self-consistency and quality estimation.} Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023} introduced self-consistency decoding. Manakul et al.~\cite{manakul2023} formalized hallucination detection via SelfCheckGPT. Farquhar et al.~\cite{farquhar2024} introduced semantic entropy. We adapt these to OCR, where the input is a fixed image---making disagreement a clean signal of perceptual failure. In machine translation, reference-free quality estimation is well-established: Kocmi and Federmann~\cite{kocmi2023} showed that LLM-based metrics (GEMBA) achieve state-of-the-art quality estimation without reference translations. Our approach applies analogous reasoning to OCR: if multiple runs agree, the transcription is likely correct; if they disagree, the model is uncertain.

\textbf{Reasoning models and OCR.} Recent work has tested whether chain-of-thought reasoning improves VLM performance on text-rich images. The OCR-Reasoning Benchmark~\cite{ocrr2025} found that for some models, forced chain-of-thought \emph{degrades} OCR performance due to train/test mismatch. LogicOCR~\cite{ye2025} showed that test-time scaling (more thinking tokens) does not reliably help VLMs bridge visual reading with reasoning. Our finding that Gemini Pro's thinking mode \emph{hurts} manuscript OCR aligns with this emerging pattern: reasoning helps when the perceptual input is reliable, but cannot compensate for perceptual failure.

\textbf{VLM calibration.} Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2025} identified ``semantic hallucination'' in scene text; Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025} benchmarked OCR hallucination under visual degradation. On calibration specifically, Chhikara~\cite{chhikara2025} showed RLHF-tuned models display increased miscalibration on easier queries, and Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2026} proposed decoupling visual confidence from reasoning confidence in VLMs. Our calibration gap (self-consistency overestimates accuracy by 20 points) is a concrete instance of this broader VLM overconfidence phenomenon.

\textbf{Leonardo manuscripts.} Despite extensive digitization---the Codex Atlanticus~\cite{codexatlanticus}, e-Leo~\cite{eleo}, and British Library's Codex Arundel are all online---no prior work has attempted automated VLM transcription of Leonardo's mirror script. Vecce et al.~\cite{vecce2022} digitally reconstructed Leonardo's personal library but did not attempt OCR of his handwriting. The gap is notable: Leonardo's notebooks are among the most important scientific manuscripts in existence, yet they have been entirely bypassed by the LLM-based OCR literature.

\textbf{Corpus.} Source Library contains ${\sim}$17,000 books and 3.35M processed pages. Our evaluation corpus: 50 pages across 12 sources---4 printed (Vitruvius 1521, Drebbel 1621, Trattato della pittura 1651, Del moto e misura dell'acqua), 1 medieval manuscript (Bodleian Timaeus, 12th~c.), and 7 Leonardo mirror-script codices (optics, anatomy, geometry, botany, physiognomy, architecture, flight). Ground truth: Richter's \emph{Literary Works}~\cite{richter1883} (OCR'd from our library) and Ravaisson-Mollien's diplomatic transcriptions~\cite{ravaisson1881}.

\textbf{Self-consistency score.} For page image $I$, generate $N$ transcriptions $T_1,\ldots,T_N$ and compute:
$$\textrm{SC}(I) = \frac{2}{N(N{-}1)} \sum_{i<j} \textrm{sim}(T_i, T_j)$$
We evaluate three similarity functions: (a)~\emph{Jaccard} (word-set intersection over union), which captures exact lexical agreement; (b)~\emph{embedding cosine similarity} (Gemini \texttt{embedding-001}, 768-dim), which captures semantic equivalence even when surface forms differ; and (c)~\emph{character error rate} (CER), the standard OCR metric. We use $N{=}5$ throughout and strip HTML/XML metadata tags before comparison.

\textbf{Why embedding similarity?} On printed text, embedding and Jaccard both indicate high consistency, but Jaccard is depressed by formatting variation (line breaks, whitespace, ligature handling)---yielding 55--62\% on text that is semantically identical. On manuscripts, embedding captures the key distinction: the model produces text that is \emph{about} the same topic (high embedding) but uses entirely different words (low Jaccard). The gap between embedding and Jaccard is itself diagnostic: a 40~pp gap indicates formatting noise; a 77~pp gap indicates confabulation.

\textbf{Trust bands.} We classify pages and sentences into three bands: \textcolor{green!50!black}{\textbf{Green}} ($>$90\%): text is reliably reproduced across runs and can be cited. \textcolor{orange!70!black}{\textbf{Yellow}} (70--90\%): topically correct but textually approximate---useful for discovery, not quotation. \textcolor{red!60!black}{\textbf{Red}} ($<$70\%): unreliable. These thresholds are empirically motivated: printed text consistently scores $>$95\%, while Leonardo's text-heavy pages cluster at 77--93\%, placing the green/yellow boundary at the natural gap between ``reading'' and ``confabulating.''

\textbf{OCR prompt.} Each page is transcribed with a minimal prompt: \emph{``Transcribe this historical manuscript page to plain text. Preserve original spelling. Mark illegible text with $\langle$unclear$\rangle$...$\langle$/unclear$\rangle$. Do NOT add interpretive notes or reconstruct text you cannot read.''} For Leonardo pages, we add: \emph{``The text is written in mirror-script (right to left). Transcribe it as normal left-to-right Italian.''} No few-shot examples are provided.

\textbf{Models.} Gemini 3 Flash, 3.1 Flash Lite, 3 Pro; Claude Sonnet 4, Opus 4.6. All at default temperature ($T{\approx}1.0$), no system prompts beyond the OCR instruction.

\textbf{Choice of $N{=}5$.} We use five runs as a practical trade-off between statistical stability and cost. With $N{=}5$, there are $\binom{5}{2}{=}10$ pairwise comparisons per page; informal testing showed that mean similarity stabilizes within ${\pm}1$pp between $N{=}3$ and $N{=}7$. For triage purposes (green vs.\ yellow), $N{=}3$ may suffice; for precise calibration gap measurement, $N{=}5$ or higher is preferable.

\subsection{All Frontier Models Converge}

Five models on the Bodleian manuscript ($N{=}2$, Jaccard word overlap---an early experiment before we adopted embedding similarity): Gemini Lite 36.7\%*, Opus 27.3\%, Pro 27.0\%, Flash 26.8\%, Sonnet 21.5\%. (*Lite scores highest but produces pseudo-Latin nonsense---consistency without accuracy.) Models spanning different architectures converge within a half-point band on manuscript text, while all scoring $>$95\% on printed pages. The convergence suggests the bottleneck is visual perception, not language modeling. A more rigorous cross-model comparison using embedding similarity at $N{=}5$ is needed to confirm this finding.

\subsection{Reasoning Makes It Worse}

A natural hypothesis: if the model struggles with mirror-script because individual letterforms are ambiguous, perhaps step-by-step reasoning---decomposing each word letter by letter---would help. We tested four conditions on Leonardo p49:

\begin{table}[H]
\small
\centering
\caption{Reasoning experiment ($N{=}5$, Leonardo p49).}
\label{tab:reasoning}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
Condition & Embed. & Median len. \\
\midrule
Flash (no reasoning) & \textbf{86.0\%} & 1,247 ch \\
Pro (standard) & 74.1\% & 892 ch \\
Pro + reasoning prompt & 65.0\% & 214 ch \\
Pro + thinking (4096 tok) & 64.2\% & 0 ch \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The ``reasoning prompt'' instructs the model to first identify each letter, then assemble words. ``Thinking'' uses Gemini Pro's built-in extended thinking mode with 4,096--8,192 budget tokens. Both conditions \emph{decrease} consistency relative to the standard model, with the thinking mode frequently producing zero-character output---the model recognizes it cannot confidently decode individual reversed letterforms and, rather than confabulate, produces nothing.

This is arguably more honest behavior. But it confirms that the bottleneck is \textbf{perceptual, not cognitive}: no amount of reasoning about what reversed letters should look like can compensate for the inability to decode them from pixels. The model lacks a learned visual prior for mirror-script letterforms, and reasoning cannot substitute for one.

We compared AI OCR output against Richter's published expert transcriptions~\cite{richter1883}. Richter's \emph{Literary Works of Leonardo da Vinci} (1883) contains Italian transcriptions of hundreds of Leonardo passages. We OCR'd Richter's volumes through our standard pipeline, then compared optics passages against fresh AI OCR of two corresponding manuscript pages (pp.\,49 and 53).

On these two pages, self-consistency across 5 runs is 83--88\%, but accuracy against Richter is only 65--67\%---a gap of ${\sim}$\textbf{18--20 percentage points} (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}). \emph{Caveat:} this gap is measured on only 2 pages and may not generalize; the true gap could be larger or smaller on other folios, codices, or hands. What the measurement does establish is that a gap \emph{exists}: the model agrees with itself far more than with the expert, because the runs share a common bias---they all generate plausible Italian about the same topic from the same training distribution.

An informative control: embedding similarity between two \emph{different} Richter pages on the same topic (optics) is 85.4\%---higher than AI-vs-Richter at 79.8\%. The AI is in the semantic neighborhood but further from the real text than expert pages are from each other.

\subsection{The Ravaisson-Mollien Confound}

Ravaisson-Mollien's \emph{Les Manuscrits de L\'eonard de Vinci} (1881--1891)~\cite{ravaisson1881} provides diplomatic transcriptions of Leonardo's Institut de France manuscripts alongside facsimile reproductions. Many pages show both the manuscript facsimile and the printed Italian transcription on the same page---creating an unplanned natural experiment.

\textbf{Stylistic mismatch.} Even setting aside the confound, the stylistic evidence is striking. Leonardo actually wrote: ``\emph{Prosspectiua. eragione. dimostratiua, perla quale. lassperientia chonferma}'' (periods between words, archaic spelling, no standard spacing). The AI produces: ``\emph{Ogn' azione ne' nostri sensi \`e fatta per via d'alcuna linia}'' (normalized modern Italian, correct grammar, entirely different words). A model genuinely decoding pixels would reproduce Leonardo's idiosyncratic orthography---the periods-between-words, the double consonants, the absence of spaces. Instead, it generates Italian in the style of texts \emph{about} Leonardo, not in Leonardo's actual style.

\textbf{Copying confound test.} When asked to transcribe only the handwritten portion of a Ravaisson-Mollien page (not the printed text), the model produces descriptions (``dense cursive handwriting in sepia-toned ink'') rather than actual transcription---even though the printed answer is visible below the facsimile. On pages where the printed transcription is present, word overlap between ``AI OCR'' and the printed text reaches 97\%, confirming the model reads the \emph{printed} answer rather than decoding the manuscript. When restricted to only the facsimile image (cropped to exclude printed text), the model cannot produce Italian text at all.

This is perhaps the strongest evidence in our study: the model demonstrably reads print but cannot decode the adjacent mirror-script, even when both appear on the same page.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Informed Confabulation}

The failure mode we observe is not random hallucination. On an optics page, all five runs discuss light, shadow, and visual rays---the correct topic. But the specific sentences differ completely between runs. Direct vocabulary comparison against Richter's expert transcriptions reveals that only 2 of 21 key optics terms (``corpo,'' ``ombra''---both common Italian words) appear in the AI output. Core Leonardian vocabulary---\emph{simulacri} (images), \emph{spiraculi} (apertures), \emph{spetie} (species/rays), \emph{ochio} (eye), \emph{piramidi} (pyramids of light)---is entirely absent. The model produces grammatically correct 15th-century Italian about the right topic, using the wrong words.

We propose the term ``informed confabulation'' to distinguish this from generic VLM hallucination. Where hallucination produces arbitrary nonsense, informed confabulation produces \emph{plausible, topically appropriate} text that could pass casual inspection. The model has learned, from its training corpus, what Leonardo writes about---but it cannot decode what he actually wrote from the image. The output is a sample from $P(\text{Italian text} \mid \text{topic=optics, author=Leonardo})$, not from $P(\text{text} \mid \text{image})$.

\subsection{Implications for Practitioners}

For printed text ($>$95\% of most historical collections): use single-run OCR with confidence. Self-consistency scores above 95\% reliably indicate accurate transcription, and the calibration gap is negligible.

For manuscripts: (1)~run $N{=}5$ passes, (2)~compute per-sentence consistency, (3)~present trust-annotated output where green sentences can be cited and yellow/red sentences are flagged for expert review. This costs 5$\times$ a single OCR pass---a few cents per page at current API prices, negligible versus paleographic labor. The multi-run approach transforms consistency from a passive quality label into an active transcription tool: the consistent fragments are genuine signal, and honest uncertainty marking tells scholars exactly where to focus their expertise.

For digital libraries at scale: the text density law (\S\ref{fig:bands}) enables efficient triage. Pages dominated by diagrams or labels can be trusted; text-heavy manuscript pages require the full $N{=}5$ treatment. This avoids spending 5$\times$ cost on pages that don't need it.

\subsection{Temperature and Sampling}

A natural question: would near-deterministic sampling ($T \to 0$) produce a single consistent transcription that matches expert text? The answer is that $T{=}0$ trivially maximizes self-consistency---the model generates the same text every run---without improving accuracy. A model that confabulates the same plausible Italian five times scores 100\% self-consistency and 0\% accuracy. Our 20-point calibration gap would simply collapse to a 100\% vs.\ 65\% gap, widening the discrepancy rather than resolving it.

This underscores a key insight: self-consistency measures \emph{agreement among samples from the model's posterior}, not agreement with ground truth. When the posterior is concentrated on the wrong answer (as it is for mirror-script), consistency and accuracy diverge. Temperature controls the width of the posterior, not its location.

\subsection{Training Data Contamination}

Could the model be reproducing memorized transcriptions rather than decoding pixels? To answer this, we audited what Leonardo transcriptions are actually available as clean, machine-readable Italian text online---and therefore likely in LLM training corpora.

The answer is surprisingly little. Richter's \emph{Literary Works}~\cite{richter1883} is on Project Gutenberg (\#5000), but the Gutenberg version contains \textbf{English translation only}---the original Italian was stripped. MacCurdy's \emph{Notebooks} (1938) is also English-only. The two richest digital transcription projects---e-Leo~\cite{eleo} and the Museo Galileo's \emph{leonardo//thek@}---lock their Italian transcriptions behind JavaScript viewers with anti-copy measures, invisible to web crawlers. Ravaisson-Mollien's six volumes~\cite{ravaisson1881} are on the Internet Archive, but as noisy 19th-century OCR mixing Italian, French commentary, and facsimile descriptions.

The clean Italian actually available amounts to: the Gutenberg \emph{Frammenti letterari e filosofici} (\#67931, a few hundred literary fragments) and the Italian Wikisource \emph{Trattato della pittura} (${\sim}$900 precepts on painting). These cover perhaps 5--10\% of Leonardo's ${\sim}$7,000 manuscript pages, skewed heavily toward literary and artistic content---not the scientific notebooks (optics, anatomy, water dynamics) that we tested. The model has extensive \emph{English} text about Leonardo's topics, but very little clean \emph{Italian original} to memorize.

Despite this limited contamination surface, the model still fails. Four observations converge:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Vocabulary mismatch despite available ground truth.} Richter's transcription of the optics passages we tested is freely available online. Yet only 2 of 21 core optics terms from Richter appear in the AI output (``corpo'' and ``ombra''---common Italian words). The 19 absent terms (\emph{simulacri}, \emph{spiraculi}, \emph{spetie}, \emph{piramidi}, etc.) are distinctively Leonardian and appear in multiple online sources. The model has these words in its training data but does not produce them when looking at the manuscript.

\item \textbf{Text density dominates fame.} Self-consistency is uniform across all 8 codices regardless of how widely a folio has been published. Pages from Leonardo's most cited notebooks score the same as obscure codices---what matters is how much handwritten text the page contains, not how much scholarship surrounds it.

\item \textbf{The Ravaisson-Mollien confound.} The model achieves 97\% word overlap with the printed transcription \emph{only} when the print is visible on the same page. When restricted to the facsimile alone, it cannot produce text. If the model had memorized the transcription and could match it to the page image, it would reproduce it regardless of whether the print was visible.

\item \textbf{Style, not content, is the tell.} The AI produces normalized modern Italian (``\emph{Ogn' azione ne' nostri sensi}'') rather than Leonardo's distinctive orthography (``\emph{Prosspectiua. eragione. dimostratiua}''). A model retrieving memorized Richter text would reproduce Richter's faithfully preserved archaic spellings. Instead, the output reads like a modern Italian summary of Leonardo's topics---consistent with generation from training priors \emph{about} Leonardo, not retrieval of his actual words.
\end{enumerate}

The contamination picture is thus nuanced: the model knows Leonardo's topics and even his vocabulary from training data, but cannot perform the crucial mapping from \emph{this specific page image} to \emph{this specific passage}. The visual decoding step---reading reversed letterforms from 500-year-old ink on parchment---remains the binding constraint.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our corpus is small (50 pages) and concentrated on one author's manuscripts. The Richter accuracy comparison is limited to 2 pages, and a larger page-aligned study against complete diplomatic editions would strengthen the calibration gap measurement. We use Gemini embeddings to evaluate Gemini OCR output, introducing potential same-family bias; cross-family evaluation (e.g., OpenAI embeddings on Gemini OCR) would control for this. The trust band thresholds (90\%/70\%) are empirically motivated by the gap between printed and manuscript scores but have not been validated against expert paleographic judgments of transcription quality. Finally, our evaluation is limited to Italian mirror-script; other challenging scripts (Arabic, Chinese calligraphy, medieval abbreviations) may exhibit different failure patterns.

\subsection{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Expand the calibration gap.} Page-align Ravaisson-Mollien's diplomatic transcriptions against the corresponding manuscript folios for 20+ pages across multiple codices. This would move the calibration gap from a 2-page observation to a statistically robust estimate.
\item \textbf{Cross-family embedding evaluation.} Repeat the consistency analysis using OpenAI or Cohere embeddings to control for same-family bias in the Gemini-on-Gemini setup.
\item \textbf{Validate trust thresholds.} Recruit 3--5 paleographers to rate a sample of 50 AI transcriptions (spanning green/yellow/red bands) for accuracy, then compute the ROC curve for the 90\%/70\% thresholds.
\item \textbf{Test on other manuscript traditions.} Apply the method to Arabic naskh, medieval Latin secretary hand, and Chinese calligraphy to determine whether the consistency-accuracy relationship and text density law generalize beyond Leonardo.
\item \textbf{N saturation analysis.} Formally test consistency stabilization across $N{=}2,3,5,7,10$ to determine the minimum number of runs needed for reliable triage vs.\ precise measurement.
\end{enumerate}

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Self-consistency via embedding similarity provides a practical, reference-free quality signal for AI-transcribed historical documents. For the printed books that constitute the vast majority of digital library collections, it reliably confirms high-quality transcription. But on difficult manuscripts, it overestimates accuracy by ${\sim}$20 points---a calibration gap that practitioners must account for.

Our key findings: (1)~all five frontier models converge to the same consistency range on manuscripts, confirming a shared perceptual bottleneck rather than model-specific weakness; (2)~image preprocessing (flip + contrast) outperforms reasoning models, because the bottleneck is visual, not cognitive; (3)~consistency tracks text density, not topic, enabling efficient triage; (4)~on Leonardo's mirror-script, not a single sentence is reliably transcribed across 5 runs---a finding invisible to page-level scores.

Leonardo's notebooks are among the most important scientific manuscripts in existence, and they remain beyond the reach of automated transcription. But for every unreadable Leonardo page, there are thousands of printed historical books that AI can transcribe reliably and translate for the first time. The key contribution is not reading Leonardo---it is knowing when you can't.
