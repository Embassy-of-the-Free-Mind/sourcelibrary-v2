# Draft Letter to Patrick Collison — March 2026

---

Hello Patrick,

A quick update on Source Library, since things have moved fast.

**We're now at 4,305 complete translations — and we'll hit 10,000 by month's end.** Since my last letter we've nearly doubled the collection, and we're currently translating over 40,000 pages per day. The pipeline works: book scans come in from Internet Archive, Gallica, the Bavarian State Library, and other repositories; AI reads every page in the original language; then translates it into English with full facing-page images of the source.

As of today, Source Library is the world's largest library of translated ancient texts. We exceed the combined holdings of the Loeb Classical Library, the Perseus Digital Library, Sacred-texts.com, and the Internet Classics Archive. The collection spans Latin, German, Greek, Chinese, Sanskrit, Sumerian, Syriac, Hebrew, French, Italian, Dutch, and more — from cuneiform tablets to Enlightenment treatises.

Next week we'll put out a press release announcing this milestone as part of our beta launch. We're framing it around our core mission: **translating the Renaissance**.

**The economics are striking.** The validated cost to fully translate a book — OCR, translation, metadata, indexing — is $1.54. That's a 16th-century Latin alchemical treatise, translated into English, with every page image preserved, for the cost of a cup of coffee. A professional Latin translator would charge $15,000–$50,000 for the same book. We've spent about $20,000 in hard costs to get here, most of it in the past month as we've scaled.

**The opportunity ahead:** About 150,000–250,000 premodern books are already digitized and freely available via IIIF from European research libraries. We could translate all of them. Our estimate is $250K gross to reach 100,000 translated books by June — essentially the entire digitized Renaissance. (NAF and the Embassy take 35% for overhead and fiscal sponsorship, so that's roughly $162K in direct costs, which tracks with our $1.54/book unit economics.)

**However, less than a fifth of the Renaissance has been scanned at all.** The real long-term challenge isn't translation — it's digitization. Scanning requires physical access, conservation assessment, and equipment. That's a fundamentally different and more expensive effort. It's what we'll raise for next.

**What we need right now:** Another $50K would keep us scaling through the next 3 months. Specifically:
- AI processing (~$30K) — sustaining 40,000+ pages/day
- Infrastructure (~$5K) — database, hosting, compute
- Legal advice (~$5K) — IP, licensing, nonprofit structure
- PR for beta launch (~$5K)
- First hire or contractor (~$5K) — editorial/scholarly review

Your initial support got us from a proof of concept to the world's largest collection. This next phase gets us from 10K to 100K — and from a project to an institution.

We're now putting serious time into fundraising to find founding donors for Source Library at the Embassy of the Free Mind. These are people who believe that the intellectual heritage of the Renaissance should be accessible to everyone — and who want to be recognized for making that happen.

**Could we recognize you as a founding donor?** You were the first person to support this work, and it would mean a great deal to have your name associated with it as we go public.

I'd also welcome any advice on the fundraising approach, legal structure, or anything else. You've seen more of these trajectories than I have.

I hope you continue to find interesting things at [SourceLibrary.org](https://sourcelibrary.org). The Leonardo da Vinci collection keeps growing, and we recently imported a beautiful Greek manuscript combining the Corpus Hermeticum with Hero of Alexandria's Pneumatica — the esoteric and the engineering, side by side, just as they were in the original.
