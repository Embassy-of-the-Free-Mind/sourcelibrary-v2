What nine million pages taught us

Source Library · a case study · figures read from the database on 7 September 2026
Open. We run a library of early printed books that machines read and translate. You have just seen the site, so this is not a demo. It is what we learned: four moments the machine was wrong and we only knew because we measured, and the questions that raises for anyone holding a collection. Behind me: 320 of the most-read books.

Limit

Fluent is not the same as right

Dicaearchus, Estienne 1589, page 34. Real page, real repair.
Collapse. Twenty lines of Latin and Greek. The first translation, in full: "continued from previous page… structure". Fluent, confident, missing the page. We checked 4.25M pages with one cheap signal, English far shorter than source. 51,227 flagged; half was our own measurement error, so about 25,000 real, 0.59%. 556 repaired in published books, prior versions kept. The cheap model collapses 11 times more than the better one on Latin; on Tibetan the transcription itself was looping. A cheap signal triages, a read calibrates. Repair non-destructively. Keep the checker independent of the checked.

The machine cannot see its own mistakes

Same leaf, two scans, two readings. 32 pages in nine million have a human reference transcription.
OCR. Left: the same page from two scans of the same book, read independently; differing words marked. We cannot measure accuracy at scale, so we measure self-agreement: 163,495 pages read twice in August. Right: disagreement by language. English 2%, Latin 15%, Greek 23%, Arabic 60%, Persian 70%. Non-Latin script: budget for the better model or a person. Our own correction: we reported plate misreads at 30%; the true rate was 0.34%.
Difficult choice

What counts as a first translation

100 badged books, checked. And what it took to check one.
First translations. These are real covers from the library, each one badged "First Translation". We told funders we had about 5,800. In a stratified study of 462 badged books: 46 in 100 were a clean first, 18 had already been translated, 30 were not one work at all, bundles like the Articella where one binding holds a dozen texts, 6 could not be resolved. We also found firsts we had never badged, in roughly equal number. Best estimate about 5,000, range 4,000 to 6,500. The cheap verifier fabricated 63% of its "a prior translation exists" answers. 75,927 verification attempts, 811 by a person. A first translation is not a property of a book. It is an event that happens to a work.
Human oversight

Where the humans are

Every action at each stage. Blue by the machine, yellow by a person.
Where the humans are. Read left to right. Choosing what to digitise: 78 acquisition campaigns, all human. Reading: 438,329 machine revisions against 32 human reference pages. Translating: 4.9M pages against five logged editor corrections. Verifying claims: 75,116 machine attempts, 811 human verdicts. Attribution: 133 hand checks. Publishing: 93 curator sessions, 962 volunteers, 461 feedback messages. Nobody reads every page. Oversight here means provenance on every field, prior versions kept, and human time spent on samples and on the measurements that decide where to look. That is defensible. It is not "a human checked this", and no badge should imply it.
Why bother

How much of the written past can anyone read today?

The gap, three steps; arrow right advances. Step one, Dutch: 82,651 distinct works printed in Dutch before 1700 in the Universal Short Title Catalogue. 1.2 percent have a scan we could find. 0.56 percent have an English translation on record. That is the province's own printed past. Step two, Europe: 1.39 million works; Latin alone 444,000 at 1.95 percent translated; German 0.14 percent. Across all languages about 0.9 percent, a figure the site's own translation-gap study stress-tested four ways; it cannot exceed 3.8 percent. Step three, beyond Europe: the catalogues we can measure, Tibetan 30,437 works at 2.7 percent, Islamicate 6,531 pre-1900 works at 5.3 percent, the Siku Quanshu canon of 3,461 Chinese works at one to three percent. Those are catalogues, not the whole of each tradition, so the true circles are larger and the rates lower. The choice is not perfect versus quick. It is quick and labelled, or nothing for another century.
For a collection-holding institution

What we would tell you

Do

  • Keep your own copy of every scan and every output
  • Stamp every field: model, prompt, date
  • Measure a rate before you fix anything. Re-measure.
  • Cheap signal first, then read a sample
  • Budget dial in front of the pipeline
  • Pay for the checking. That is the new job.

Don't

  • Let a badge imply a human read it
  • Use the model to check the model
  • Overwrite. Ever.
  • Assume the cheap model can read your script
  • Report records fixed. Report the rate.
  • Forget that the models will read what you publish
