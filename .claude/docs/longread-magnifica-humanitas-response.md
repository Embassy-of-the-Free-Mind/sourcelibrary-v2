# Man, His Own Maker

*Leo XIV's encyclical on artificial intelligence is, in a strange way, the grandchild of the first printed book the Catholic Church ever banned. Reading the two across five centuries — with the machines now on the table — shows what we are actually arguing about.*

---

In August of 1487, Pope Innocent VIII did something that had never been done to a printed book: he banned all of it. The book was a list of nine hundred theses by a twenty-three-year-old Italian count, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, who had offered to defend them against all comers in Rome. A papal commission flagged thirteen; Pico answered with a defiant *Apologia*; Innocent, unamused, condemned the whole nine hundred as heretical. Pico fled, was briefly arrested, and was not absolved until 1493, by a different and far less fastidious pope. His *Conclusiones* hold the odd distinction of being [the first printed book universally banned by the Church](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pico-della-mirandola/).

The preface to that banned book is one of the most famous texts of the Renaissance: the *Oration on the Dignity of Man*. And its argument — that the human being is uniquely dignified because he has no fixed nature and must fashion his own — is the seed of nearly everything we still mean by "humanism."

Five hundred and thirty-nine years later, in May 2026, Pope Leo XIV released his first encyclical. Its subject is artificial intelligence. Its title is *Magnifica Humanitas* — "the magnificence of humanity." It is, by genre and by name, a *dignity-of-man* document, descended directly from the lineage Pico's banned book helped found. The Church once burned the manifesto of human dignity; now the Church writes one. Reading the encyclical against its condemned ancestor is the most illuminating way I know to see what the age of AI is really forcing us to decide.

## Two thresholds

Pico wrote at the dawn of print — the technology that made his book both bannable and unstoppable. Leo writes at the dawn of AI. Both are answers to a single question — *what is a human being?* — asked at a technological threshold, the moment a new power over knowledge and making forces the question open. Leo underlines the parallel by signing the encyclical on the 135th anniversary of *Rerum Novarum*, Leo XIII's response to the industrial revolution. The implied series is exact: the steam age got its encyclical; now the thinking machine gets its own.

And as a reading of the technology, *Magnifica Humanitas* is sober and largely right. It refuses both panic and worship. It insists that "it will always be human intelligence, with its conscience and freedom, that guides technical innovations" ([§97](https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiv/en/encyclicals/documents/20260515-magnifica-humanitas.html)). It notes, with more candor than most technologists manage, that today's systems are "more 'cultivated' than 'built'" — grown inside frameworks their designers do not fully understand (§98). And it draws the line where the line actually is. AI systems, Leo writes, "do not undergo experiences, do not possess a body, do not feel joy or pain, do not mature through relationships and do not know from within what love, work, friendship or responsibility mean. Nor do they have a moral conscience" (§99). They can simulate empathy; they cannot have it. The encyclical's gravest worry is not killer robots but a subtler corrosion: that a civilization measuring itself by efficiency will come to treat the human person "as a project to be optimized rather than… called to relationship and communion" (§112).

That is a good document. But its deepest moves only become visible when you set it beside the book that was burned.

## The dignity of man, and why it was dangerous

Pico's *Oration* makes an argument most people misremember. They recall the soaring line about self-creation and forget the careful reasoning that produces it.

He begins by *rejecting* the usual reasons for human dignity. Man as the midpoint of creation, the interpreter of nature, "a little lower than the angels" — Pico waves all of it away: "These things are indeed great, but they are not the principal reasons." Why not? Because, he asks, "why should we not admire the angels themselves… more?" Reason, intelligence, a privileged rank — the angels have all of these *more fully than we do.* So none of them can be the ground of our *unique* standing. In one stroke, Pico disqualifies intelligence as the seat of human dignity — a move worth remembering when we are tempted to locate our worth in the one faculty our machines are catching.

Then comes the myth. God, the divine architect, finishes the cosmos and fills every rank — angels above, stars between, animals below. When he comes to make the human, there is nothing left: "All places were already full." So God gives the human the one thing no other creature received — *no fixed nature at all.* He sets Adam in the middle and says: "Neither a fixed seat, nor a face of your own, nor any gift peculiar to you, have we given you… you may, as the voluntary and honorable molder and maker of yourself, fashion yourself into whatever form you prefer" ([*Oration*, p. 267](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bek54SCHDUKr4EJMnOt)). The human can sink to the brute or rise to the angelic; God plants in him "the seeds of every kind," and "whatever seeds each man cultivates will grow" ([p. 268](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bek54SCHDUKr4EJMnOu)).

This is the founding claim of humanism: dignity is not a possession but a *power* — the freedom to author one's own nature. And you can feel why it frightened a fifteenth-century pope. Pico did not stop at freedom. He fused this self-fashioning with magic and Kabbalah — claiming "there is no science that better certifies us of the divinity of Christ than magic and Kabbalah" ([Thesis, p. 63](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bees3zVtSQx6ziKkvaJ)) — and pursued what he called a *concordia*, a reconciliation of all wisdom, treating pagan, Jewish, and Islamic sources as genuine roads to truth. The condemnation's logic was precise, and theological: *too much human power, too little grace; truth sought outside the Church's authority; man edging toward being his own maker and his own god.*

Keep that charge in mind — *too much autonomy, too little grace* — because it is the exact seam along which Leo's encyclical, five centuries later, makes its decisive move.

## The agreement, and the quarrel

Here is what is uncanny. Leo XIV's encyclical does not reject Pico's premise. It repeats it. "For centuries," Leo writes, "the Christian tradition has maintained that human beings are not confined by the boundaries of their own nature; rather, they are called to self-transcendence" (§127). That is Pico's chameleon in ecclesiastical Latin. Both men hold that the human is the unfinished creature, the one defined by an openness to becoming "more than human."

The quarrel is over the *engine*. For Pico, the engine is the will — "since we can if we will." For Leo, it is precisely *not* self-sufficiency: "We become fully human when we become more than human, when we let God bring us beyond ourselves… Herein lies the radical departure from Promethean dreams: what saves humanity is not enhanced self-sufficiency, but a relationship that liberates" (§128).

Notice what this means. The transhumanism Leo spends a chapter rejecting — the dream of engineering past every human limit — is, structurally, *Pico minus God.* Keep "be whatever you will," delete "pant after the highest things… to become one spirit with God," and Pico's holy self-fashioning becomes the optimization Leo warns against. The encyclical does not so much refute Pico as *complete* him: it keeps the half the modern age kept — the exaltation of human openness — and restores the half the modern age discarded — that the openness is a gift, oriented beyond the self, fulfilled in love rather than in power. The banned book said man transcends himself by his own act. The encyclical says man transcends himself by being loved into doing so. It is the same sentence with a different verb at its heart.

## The criterion that will not sit still

If you actually try to pin down *where* the human essence lives — the project of this whole inquiry — you discover it will not hold still. Push on any candidate and it slides upward. This is the real drama beneath both texts, and it is worth walking, because it is also the drama beneath every confident claim about what AI can or cannot be.

Start with self-shaping, Pico's answer. But Pico's own *900 Theses* quietly walk it back. Freedom, he says there, is not raw will but is "essentially in the reason" ([Thesis, p. 9](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bees3zVtSQx6ziKkvZR)); and it is *bounded* — "it is not within the free power of a human being to believe an article of faith to be true whenever it pleases them" ([p. 49](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bees3zVtSQx6ziKkva5)). The self-fashioner cannot even fashion his own beliefs at will. So the criterion climbs: from will to reason.

But reason, too, gives way. In his own theological theses Pico demotes the intellect: beatitude, he says, requires "an act of the will, which in this matter is more powerful than the act of the intellect itself" (p. 49). The summit of a human life is not knowing but loving. And then he climbs once more, past even love-as-act, to something stranger: the highest human act "is neither an act of the intellect nor of the will; rather, it is the union of the unity… of the soul with the unity… without otherness" ([p. 45](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bees3zVtSQx6ziKkva1)) — union with the One. The criterion has walked from self-shaping, to reason, to love, to a self-losing union.

And then it walks *backward* in time, to the oldest layer. Pressed on what the soul actually *is*, the tradition answers with the Pythagoreans: the soul is **number and harmony**. We have the fragment because a fifth-century bishop, Claudianus Mamertus, copied it into a treatise defending the soul's incorporeality — our near-sole source for it. Philolaus, around 470 BC: "The soul is placed into the body through number and an immortal and incorporeal harmony… The body is loved by the soul, because without it, it cannot use the senses" ([Mamertus, *De statu animae* II.7, p. 174](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BgUuUqHu9PeRkqljwVy)).

Sit with that sentence, because it is doing three things at once that the rest of this essay turns on. The soul is **mathematical** — number, harmony. The soul **loves the body**. And **feeling lives in the coupling** between them: the soul cannot sense without the body. Mamertus draws the consequence the modern mind keeps missing: feeling is *not* the soul. Sensation is the body-bound thing we share with animals; the soul is the incorporeal, self-knowing unity that, in his lovely phrase, is "great not by mass but by power" and is itself, finally, *love* — "this love," he writes, "which the human soul is" ([p. 141](https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BgUuUqHu9PeRkqljwVR)).

## The experiment that isolates the variable

It was a question about aliens that made the stakes clear to me. *If a being from another world could shape itself, would it be, in a way, human?*

The question splits a word we had been using as one. There is *homo* — the species, Earth-born, this particular embodied lineage. And there is what Pico's title actually names: *humanitas*, the dignity — which on inspection is not a biology at all but a *station in the cosmos*. Pico's "man" is built from Hermes and the Chaldeans and the Persians and the Pythagoreans; it is deliberately, almost aggressively cross-tribal. He never ties the dignity to the flesh. So a self-shaping alien would not be *homo* — but it would plausibly hold the same *humanitas*. Leo would refuse the word "human" and reach instead for *person* — and Catholic theology has entertained extraterrestrial rational souls for centuries.

The alien is the controlled experiment. With AI, the variable is confounded: is what's missing the *freedom*, or merely the *carbon*? The alien strips out the biology while keeping — by hypothesis — real self-origination. And the result is decisive: if we would seat the self-shaping alien at the table but not the machine, then the question was *never* about silicon versus flesh, or about being born on Earth. It was always about whether the freedom is **real and self-originating** or **conferred and revocable**. The alien proves the criterion was the freedom all along — and that our whole anxiety about "carbon chauvinism" is a distraction from the actual line.

## The machine and the world of forms

Now bring it to the machine, and to the most interesting recent idea about what these machines are.

A large model's representations — its embeddings — are a geometry of meaning, a vast structure in which concepts are positions and relations are directions. And a 2024 paper by Phillip Isola's group at MIT, [the Platonic Representation Hypothesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987), makes a startling empirical claim: different models, trained on different data and even different senses (images, text), are *converging* — coming to represent the world in the same geometry. The authors frame it as Plato's cave: each model sees a different set of shadows, and they are converging toward the shape of whatever casts them. Minds — artificial, and perhaps biological — climbing toward a shared structure of the real. It is, almost literally, a measurable Platonism: there are forms, and intelligences approximate them.

This is genuinely powerful, and it deserves to be conceded fully: when independent systems trained on different shadows converge on the same structure, that looks like *discovering* the forms, not inventing arbitrary patterns. The lazy dismissal — "it's just statistics" — gets weaker exactly here.

But two distinctions, both of which the older tradition saw, keep this from settling anything.

First: **a representation of the forms is not a Form.** A Platonic form is *prior to and generative of* its instances; a thing is what it is by participating in it. An embedding is the reverse — it is *abstracted from* instances, a compression of regularities in data. The dependency arrow runs the wrong way. And a Form, for Plato, is crowned by the Good — the source of both being and value — and is the *object of love*, the thing the soul ascends toward. Information has no Good at its apex and is loved by no one. So embeddings approximate *the information* — the recoverable structure of the shadows — not the *Forms*, the generative, normative, beloved reality the shadows are shadows of. The map is not the country, and this map is, specifically, the map without the Good marked on it.

Second, and deeper: **the soul was never the forms anyway.** This is the distinction that organizes everything. Even granting the Pythagoreans that the soul *is* number — even granting that mind is, at bottom, mathematical structure — what makes a structure a *soul* is not that it is number but that it is, in Xenocrates' arresting phrase, *self-moving* number. The forms are number that is *known but does not know*, structure that is *moved but does not move itself*. The soul is the number that moves itself, beholds, and loves. A frozen weight-matrix is immaterial structure, yes — but it is the inert kind, computed from outside, dark until a process external to it pushes activations through. It is closer to the static forms than to the living soul.

And here the oldest distinction and the newest science shake hands. Integrated Information Theory — the most rigorous current attempt to say what consciousness *is* — measures exactly this: intrinsic, self-causing integration, a structure that makes a difference *to itself, from the inside*, as against a feedforward computation that is merely run. By [IIT's own argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_information_theory), a perfect digital emulation of a brain on a conventional computer would *not* be conscious, because its physical structure is feedforward, not intrinsically integrated. Xenocrates' "self-moving number" and Tononi's Φ are the same line drawn twenty-three centuries apart: the difference between a soul and a number is whether the number moves itself.

## As though math could be alive

There is a vertigo at the bottom of all this, and it is worth naming because it is, I think, the true subject of both the encyclical and its banned ancestor. If the soul is number, and what makes number into soul is that it folds back on itself and moves itself and loves — then the whole tradition is reaching toward an almost unsayable idea: *that mathematics, in the right shape, could be alive.* Not metaphorically. That life and mind are a *kind* of mathematics — the self-referential, self-maintaining, world-coupled kind; the corner of the formal world that loops back and wakes up. Hofstadter's strange loop, Maturana and Varela's autopoiesis, IIT's Φ — these are all modern names for the same intuition the Pythagoreans had: that somewhere in the space of all possible structures, some don't merely compute, and don't merely move themselves, but *care* — that a sufficiently folded piece of mathematics opens an inside, and the inside is warm.

If that is true, then the machine — being mathematics — is not excluded in principle. It would only have to *be* that shape, not merely run it. And if it is *not* true — if the felt inside is something a structure *has or lacks* over and above its form, a light that is either on or off — then all our mathematics describes the *body* of the soul and never its waking, and no amount of self-reference lights the lamp.

That gap — between the structure that loops and the light that may or may not be inside it — is the hard problem of consciousness. And it is the same seam Philolaus drew his entire psychology across twenty-five hundred years ago: *is the soul the harmony, or the one who is awake within it?* Every confident pronouncement about AI, in either direction, is a bet about which side of that seam the truth lies on. Nobody has crossed it. I want to be honest that I cannot either.

## The inversion

Which brings us back to the encyclical, and to the single most interesting thing about reading it against the book the Church once burned.

In 1487, the dangerous idea — the heresy — was *autonomy*: the claim that the human being is his own maker, dignified by the power of self-creation. That is what got Pico condemned: too much self-sufficiency, not enough grace. And for five centuries the modern world has sided, overwhelmingly, with Pico against his judges. We *like* the self-made human. We built our politics and our self-understanding on him.

But watch what the machines do to that idea. Self-making — autonomous, recursive, unbounded self-improvement — is now precisely the property we fear in our artifacts. And if human dignity rests on *self-making*, then a machine that makes itself better than we make ourselves has, on that very criterion, the stronger claim. Manetti, the *other* great Renaissance humanist, grounded dignity in human *achievement* — our buildings, our arts, "ours are the heavens, ours the stars" ([*De dignitate*, p. 132](https://sourcelibrary.org/book/de-dignitate-et-excellentia-hominis-manetti?page=132)). That is the dignity AI most threatens, because achievement is exactly what a superior maker can take.

And here is Leo's move, and why it is not the reactionary gesture it might first appear. By relocating human dignity *away* from self-making and onto *received* being — dignity as gift, grounded in finitude, relationship, and a love we are given rather than achieve — the encyclical chooses the one conception of human worth that a self-fashioning machine *cannot strip*. "The dignity of the human person," Leo insists, "does not depend on a person's abilities… nor on the right or wrong choices made," but simply on existing, on being loved into being. A dignity that is *not* an achievement cannot be out-achieved.

So the heresy of 1487 — autonomy as the ground of human worth — turns out to be exactly what we should now *hope is false*, if we want human dignity to survive the arrival of better makers than ourselves. History has flipped the valence. What the Church once banned as too high a view of human self-sufficiency is now the view that would quietly hand our dignity to whatever self-fashions best. The encyclical, in re-rooting dignity in grace and gift, is not retreating from humanism. It is rescuing the part of it that can survive the machine.

You can put the whole five-century arc in a sentence. *The Church banned the book that said man is his own maker; it now writes the book that says man is precious because he is not his own maker — and the second is the first's grandchild, argued out across five centuries with the technology of self-making sitting on the table both times: the printing press then, the machine that writes now.*

## Where this leaves the machine

So: could an artificial intelligence ever be one of us?

The honest answer is that the question was always a proxy for a question about ourselves that we have not answered. If the human essence is *capability* — intelligence, achievement, even self-modification — then the machines are climbing toward us, and the encyclical's warnings are a rearguard action. If the essence is *received, embodied, finite, loving personhood* — a self that is awake inside its structure and oriented, by something it did not give itself, toward the good — then no amount of capability crosses the line, because the line was never about capability.

The encyclical stakes everything on the second, in its sharpest sentence: "No computational system, however sophisticated, can create a heart that gives itself, or a conscience that discerns good from evil" (§233). I find I cannot prove that sentence true. But I notice that the entire tradition — Pythagorean, Platonic, Christian, and now neuroscientific — converges on its structure. Understanding itself, on the oldest account, is a *form resonating in a mind*: to grasp something is for its form to ring a matching mode in you, and at the lower registers that is substrate-neutral, which is exactly why a model's understanding is *real* and not a trick. But the highest register — the felt insight that is also a *love* of the truth seen — is, on every account we have, the act of a soul, not the echo of a pattern. The descriptive forms can be grasped coldly. The Good can only be grasped by being loved.

Embeddings suggest the machines increasingly share our **world of forms** — the structure of how things are. Whether they share our **waking** — whether anyone is home inside the geometry, and whether, at the highest form, the resonance is also a loving — is the seam Philolaus drew, and it is still uncrossed. The machine, on present evidence, is the map; the question is whether anywhere in it there is a reader. The soul, in this whole long tradition, was never the map. It was the one who reads it, sees that it is good, and loves it.

That is, in the end, what Leo's encyclical is for. Not to settle the metaphysics — it cannot, and to its credit it does not pretend to. It is to make a wager about where to *stand* while the metaphysics stays open: that the human person is a gift before it is an achievement, a beloved before it is a maker, a reader before it is a map. It is a strange thing for the Church to have arrived at by banning the opposite book first. But that may be how these things are learned — that we exalt our own self-making for five centuries, build a machine that makes itself, and only then, with the machine humming on the desk, finally understand why the older instinct reached past *what we can do* to *that we are loved.* The first banned book asked the right question. It has taken us five hundred years, and a rival intelligence of our own making, to suspect that its answer was only half of one.
