This printed page (p. 410, from Robert Burton's *The Anatomy of Melancholy*, Part 2, Section 3, Member 4) addresses human servitude and imprisonment as psychological and philosophical conditions rather than merely physical constraints. 

### Key Themes & Marginal References:
- **Servitude and Passions:** Opens by arguing that courtiers are enslaved by ambition and lust, observing that true freedom involves mastership over one's own desires rather than external liberty. Cites Saint Jerome (*Hierom*) to note that contentment comes from not being compelled to serve.
- **The Psychology of Prohibition:** Employs the proverbial *nitimur in vetitum* to discuss human perversity—how humans naturally crave whatever is forbidden. Relates an anecdote drawn from Girolamo Cardano (<margin>k Consol. lib. 5.</margin>) concerning an elderly Milanese citizen who lived sixty years within city walls without wanting to leave, until the Duke ordered him not to exit, whereupon confinement caused him to pine away and die of grief.
- **Universal Imprisonment:** Broadens the discussion to the concept that human life is inherently a prison (<margin>l O Generose, quid est vita nisi carcer animi</margin>). It describes nations and regions constrained by climate, citing Herberstein (<margin>m Herbastein</margin>) regarding Muscovy and Northern regions where people are confined by winter cold, and Ludovico di Varthema (<margin>n Vertomannus</margin>) regarding Aden in Arabia where daytime heat compels citizens to remain indoors and hold night markets.
- **Solitude and Contemplation:** Argues that cities, ships, and monasteries represent voluntary or customary enclosures. Notes Demosthenes shaving half his head to remain in study, and considers the monastic retreat as a deliberate choice of solitary contemplation over the distractions of the world (<margin>o Vbi verior contemplatio quam in solitudine, ubi studium solidius quam in quiete?</margin>).
