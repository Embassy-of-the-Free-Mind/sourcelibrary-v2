Courtiers to lust and ambition, and all slaves to our affections, who is free? Why then dost thou repine? *Satis est potens, Hierom* saith, *qui servire non cogitur.* Thou carriest no burdens, thou art no prentise, no drudge, and thousands want that liberty, those pleasures which thou hast. Thou art not sicke, & what wouldst thou have? but *nitimur in vetitum*, we must all eat of the forbidden fruit. Were wee enjoyned to goe to such and such places, we would not willingly goe: but being barred of our liberty, this alone torments our wandering soule that we may not goe. A cittezen of ours, saith <margin type="printed">k Consol. lib. 5.</margin> *k Cardan*, was 60 yeares of age, and had never beene forth of the walls of the citty of *Millan*, the Prince hearing of it commanded him not to stirre out, being now forbidden that which all his life he had neglected, he earnestly desired, & being denied, *dolore confectus mortem obijt*, he died for griefe.

### Overview & Summary of Page 410 (*The Anatomy of Melancholy*, Part 2, Sec. 3, Memb. 4: "Cure of Melancholy")

* **Subject & Theme:** The passage reflects on themes of servitude, human desire, confinement, and imprisonment as conditions inherent to human life rather than unique misfortunes.
* **Key Arguments & Anecdotes:**
  * **The Illusion of Complete Freedom:** The author argues that no one is entirely free; even courtiers are enslaved to ambition and passion. Those who are not subject to hard manual labor or illness should not complain of mild constraints.
  * **Desire for the Forbidden (*Nitimur in vetitum*):** The text cites an anecdote from Jerome Cardan regarding an elderly citizen of Milan who had never left the city walls in sixty years. When ordered by the prince never to leave, the sudden restriction induced such distress and longing for forbidden liberty that he reportedly died of grief.
  * **Universal Imprisonment:** The discussion turns to imprisonment, asserting metaphorically that all people are prisoners—whether on an island, confined by geography and the narrowness of the seas, or restricted by severe climates (such as harsh northern winters in Muscovy and Scandia forcing people indoors, or extreme heat in Aden keeping people confined by day).
  * **Solitude and Contemplation:** The author examines voluntary confinement (ships, monasteries, scholars shaving their beards to remain indoors to study) to argue that physical confinement can be turned toward positive contemplation and quiet study.
* **Bibliographic & Marginal References:** Mentions of Jerome (*Hierom*), Cardan (*Consol. lib. 5*), Herberstein (*Herbastein*), Ludovico de Varthema (*Vertomannus*), and Demosthenes.
