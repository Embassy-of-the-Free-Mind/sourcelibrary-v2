There are three things that he treats in the whole epistle. In the beginning, he corrects in the Corinthians those vices that seemed light to them and not at all worthy of notice; for this reason, they had not sought the judgment of the Apostle concerning these. Because these were most grave, he proposes them in the first place, such as dissensions and sects on account of teachers, the vain persuasion by which they sought eloquence in the ministers of the Lord, fornication, incest, and the habit of litigating before unbelieving judges. They cared little for these and similar things. These matters are treated up to the seventh chapter. In the second part, he responds to the questions of the Corinthians, which had arisen in good part from the contention of the teachers and had rendered the listeners ambiguous. Such are the questions moved concerning marriage in chapter 7, concerning the choice of foods, namely concerning things sacrificed to idols <note>original: "idolothytis"</note>, concerning idols, and in chapter 11 concerning ecclesiastical assemblies <note>original: "cœtibus"</note>, concerning the avoiding of women. Also that they should not teach publicly, indeed that they should not have any right of asking questions, concerning the Supper of the Lord, concerning the use of spiritual gifts, concerning the excellence of the prophetic office, and concerning the resurrection of the dead, and similar things up to chapter 16. In the third place, he adds an appendix concerning private matters in the last chapter: concerning the collection, concerning his own arrival, concerning Timothy, and similar things which are private and did not greatly pertain to the subject matter of the writing. # On Lettuce. Chapter XXII.

Lettuce, as Isidore says, is so named from the exuberance of its milky humor, or because it fills nursing women with milk. In men, it restrains the movement of Venus <note>sexual desire</note>. Its species is twofold: namely, domestic and wild, which we call <term>feralia</term> <gloss>wild/brutal</gloss>, because its back has hard prickles like iron, as Isidore says in book 17, in the last chapter. Regarding this, Pliny says in his *Dietary*: the wild lettuce is similar in form to the domestic, but it has longer, finer, rougher, and less green leaves on account of the scarcity of its moisture, and therefore it has much bitterness and astringency compared to the one that is domestic, because it is of greater heat and dryness. For this reason, many say that it provokes menstruation and expels raw humors, as Dioscorides says. 

Lettuce, for its part, is cold and moist in the second degree, and in neither quality does it exceed the measure, and therefore its use is of great medicine. It is subtle and moderate and, if it is fresh, it is quickly digested; it calms the burning and biting of the stomach and red bile, cools the ebullition of blood, and pacifies sleeplessness. It heals headache and choleric fumes, multiplies milk in women, and generates and increases semen in men. This occurs on account of the goodness of the blood that it generates in perfect quantity and at a perfect age. But when it becomes old and hard, its moisture diminishes and it turns bitter, and through its use, very bad blood is generated. The animal virtue <note>the capacity of the soul to govern the body through the senses</note> fails and is weakened; the cause of this is the mortification of the animal sense, because with its astringency, it suffocates the natural spirit. Therefore, such lettuce is most harmful to those wishing to generate, for it congeals the material and coagulates the seminal fluid, especially if its seed is consumed often in food or drink. This is the doctrine of Isaac in his *Dietary*. 

Pliny, however, in book 20, chapter 8, says about lettuce: there is a species of lettuce growing spontaneously which they call <term>caprina</term> <gloss>goats’ lettuce</gloss>, which, if thrown into the sea, kills fish that are nearby. With its stalk and obtuse leaves and crude salt, incised nerves are healed. Another species of lettuce grows in fields, whose leaves, crushed and applied with polenta, heal ulcers, and this the Greeks call <term>ysopon</term> <gloss>hyssop</gloss>. A third species grows in forests, which they call <term>steraton</term> <gloss>wild lettuce</gloss>, whose crushed leaves heal festering wounds; with the root and leaves, they mitigate holy fire <note>erysipelas</note>. There is also another species of this lettuce, round and short, which many call <term>beratia</term> <gloss>likely a variant of verrucaria/wart-wort</gloss>, whose juice hawks, by scratching the herb, extract and, by staining their eyes with it, dispel obscurity and cataracts when they grow old. The juice of this herb heals eye ailments, especially when mother's milk is mixed with it. It cures the bites of serpents and the stings of scorpions if its juice is drunk with wine and the crushed leaves are applied to the wound as a plaster. It also dispels and suppresses every swelling. The seed of lettuce, both wild and domestic, suppresses the imaginations of lust in sleep, and does not allow the body to be polluted if it is consumed frequently in food. Frequent and excessive use of it, however, causes obscurity in the eyes. Pliny attributes many other praises to it in the same chapter, but these shall suffice.

# On Burdock. Chapter XXIII.

Burdock is an herb having broad and thick leaves around the ground, having at the tops of its stalks certain thorny and prickly little heads, but recurved, which are accustomed to adhere to the garments of those passing by. Hence Isidore says in book 17: burdock is so called because it has stalks arranged toward the ground, and it is called <term>philatropos</term> <gloss>man-loving</gloss> by the Greeks, because it adheres to the clothes of men on account of its roughness. It grows near walls, and it is named <term>philatropos</term>, that is, loving man, because it seems to adhere to men's clothes with a certain affection. 

The species of burdock are twofold: sharp and round, arranged in the likeness of a horse's foot, wherefore by many it is called <term>vngula caballina</term> <gloss>horse-hoof</gloss>. The leaves of both are of a horrible odor and taste alike, as Dioscorides says. But Pliny, in book 26, says there are many species of burdock and all are medicinal. For they cure the stings of scorpions, and they do not strike a man if he has been anointed with its juice. The decoction of its root aids the teeth and also strengthens them if held warm in the mouth. Its seed consumed within cures stomach ailments, and to those vomiting blood, it is beneficial and aids in dysentery; for its root with wine stops the flow of the bowels, but taken alone, it helps those with stones. The whole herb crushed with lard is valuable for scrofula. The doctrine of Pliny according to Plate: burdock or dock is a hot and dry herb having the virtue of dissolving, relaxing, opening, and attenuating. Therefore, it is valuable against mange, itching, <term>ferpigine</term> <gloss>herpes/rash</gloss>, and impetigo. It removes intercutaneous humors, and dissolves the hardness and swelling of abscesses. It is valuable against cold dropsy and obstruction of the spleen. Its juice, mixed with rue, purges the brain and rheumatic superfluity if in a moderate quantity
