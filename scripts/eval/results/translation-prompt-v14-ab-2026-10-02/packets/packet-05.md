PREFACE

Most recently, G. Koertius <note>Georg Koerting</note> began a study on that part of the <term>Cynegeticus</term> <gloss>On Hunting</gloss> which is called technical, though he was intercepted by death before he could bring it to an end. He first explained that not only is the arrangement inconvenient, but the instruction itself is so incomplete and obscure that it could hardly be useful to hunters. And it must be admitted that he rightly contended this, but it confirms our conjecture regarding the origin of this little book. For the heir of Xenophon, a man certainly not unversed in rhetorical studies, but little skilled in writing, kept the series of facts intact just as he had found them written out in the volume handed down to him, nor did he dare to add anything from his own knowledge to the rules that might clarify them or arrange them in a suitable order.

But many of the precepts in this part of the work, Koertius says, are so false that they could in no way have been written by a man truly skilled in hunting. The fates prevented him from explaining and establishing this opinion with a full number of examples. But I would say that some things, at least, which he criticized or was about to criticize, are controversial rather than false. For the writer of the <term>Cynegeticus</term> advises the hunter to hand the hare over to be torn apart by the puppies once it has been caught by them. **S**Objection must also be made against those who speak of the nature of gold, which alone, according to <term>Hermes</term>, is that in which no disease appears. Similarly, its quicksilver has two substances purified to their extremes. Likewise, the watery element is also subtilized by the same method of repeated subtilization; and when glass is thus present, it subtilizes subtly. After being honoured with the rank of protospatharius this same Ktenas lived two years and then died; and he received a stipend of one pound for each of the two years.
