The poetic tradition of the Eastern Kingdom <note>Korea</note> differs from that of the Central Kingdom <note>China</note>. The trends in the Central Kingdom flow daily toward elaborate craftsmanship and flowery artifice. The Eastern Kingdom still prizes the ancient ways. Although there are times when the rhyme and tone do not harmonize or the word choice seems forced, its innocent and natural quality remains, and this does not detract from its excellence. In the Gyeongja year <note>1780 or 1840</note>, I met with the brothers Yi Chun-gye and Niam-jae to discuss this in a book. Now,

Puwon has brought this piece to show me. Its sentiments and thoughts are transcendent and vast, what one might call the marvelous emerging from ancient strangeness, unfolding with a singular beauty. At times it reveals a fresh and spirited presence that is enough to startle the eye, letting one know that the store of talent within is such that it cannot be matched by the present age. How could one not be moved? The rise of Puwon is different from the common run of men. If there were no gentlemen in Lu, where would one find them? Truly, the Eastern Kingdom has many men of virtue. Opening this scroll, I cannot bear to put it down. It is truly as the saying goes: reading new poems until the green mountains turn to dusk. Yet, verses like "floating clouds and tree colors," "Jialing <note>a river in China</note>, the heart falling with the setting sun," and "the river's sound, the lapel and rhyme reaching ten thousand leagues" evoke Master Yu <note>Yu Ji, a Yuan dynasty poet</note>. In the past, the frost of the fifth watch would enter one's robes. Autumn is born in the Yu Garden. The chickens return, few guests arrive at the high tower, and the butterflies speak. Much of this reflects the shame of the Du pavilion, and it is difficult to reach the heights of Cen <note>Cen Shen, a Tang dynasty poet</note>. There is not a place that is lacking.
