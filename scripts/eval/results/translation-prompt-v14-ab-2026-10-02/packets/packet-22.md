Of Lettuce. Chapter XXII.
Lettuce, as Isidore says, is so named from the exuberance of its milky humor, or because it fills nursing women with milk. This plant restrains the movement of <term>venus</term> <gloss>sexual desire</gloss> in men. Its species is twofold: namely, domestic and wild, which we call feral because its back has thorns hard like iron, as Isidore says in book 17, last chapter. Concerning which Pliny says in the dietetics: wild lettuce is similar in form to the domestic, but it has longer, thinner, rougher, and less green leaves because of the scarcity of its moisture. And therefore, there is much bitterness and astringency in respect to that which is domestic, because it is of greater heat and dryness. And so, many say that it provokes menstruation and expels raw humors, as Dioscorides says. Lettuce, regarding the domestic kind, is cold and moist according to the degree and does not exceed the limit in either quality. And therefore its use is a serious matter of medicine, <unclear>subtilibus subam et marie qui erit recens</unclear> <note>This Latin phrase is corrupted in the OCR; "subam" and "marie" likely refer to obscure preparation methods or abbreviations that cannot be confidently translated.</note>. And for that reason, it is quickly digested and calms the burning and biting of the stomach and red bile. It cools the boiling of the blood, calms insomnia, and heals headache and choleric smoke. It increases milk in women and generates and increases semen in men. And this happens because of the goodness of the blood that it generates in perfect quantity and in perfect age, but when it becomes old and hard, its moisture is diminished and it becomes bitter, and through its use, very bad blood is generated. The vital power therefrom fails and is weakened; the cause of which is the mortification of the animal sense, which, with its astringency, suffocates the natural [heat]. And therefore, such lettuce is most harmful to those wishing to generate, for it congeals the material and coagulates the seminal fluid, especially its seed if it is frequently consumed in food or drink. Isaac [says this] in the dietetics. Pliny, however, in book 20, chapter 8, says about lettuce: There is a species of lettuce growing spontaneously which they call goat-lettuce; when it is placed in the sea, it kills the fish that are nearby. Its stem and leaves, with their obtuse thorns, heal incised nerves with coarse salt. There is another species of lettuce that grows in the fields, the leaves of which, when crushed with pearl barley and applied as a poultice, heal ulcers; the Greeks call this <term>ysopon</term> <gloss>hyssop/plant-type</gloss>. The third species is that which grows in the woods, which they call <term>steraton</term> <gloss>a type of wild lettuce</gloss>; its leaves, when crushed with a decoction, heal wounds. Of Burdock. Chapter XXIII.
Burdock is a herb having broad and thick leaves around the ground, having on the tops of the stems certain spiny and aculeated little heads, but they are curved and are accustomed to adhere to the clothes of passersby. Whence Isidore says in book 17, burdock is so called because it has a stalk disposed on the ground, and by the Greeks it is called <term>philatropos</term> <gloss>man-lover</gloss>, because it adheres to the clothes of men due to its roughness. It grows near walls, and [it is called] <term>philatropos</term>, i.e., loving man, because with a certain affection it seems to adhere to the clothes of man. The species of burdock is twofold, pointed and round, disposed in the likeness of a horse's foot; by many it is called <term>vngula caballina</term> <gloss>coltsfoot</gloss>. The leaves of both are of a horrible odor and taste alike, as Dioscorides says. But Pliny says in book 26 that there are many species of burdock and all are medicinal. For they heal the stings of scorpions, and they do not strike a man if he has been anointed with its juice. The decoction of its root helps the teeth and also confirms them if held tepid in the mouth. Its seed taken internally cures stomach ailments. To those vomiting blood, it is beneficial and assists with dysentery, for the root with wine constricts the belly, but when given alone it aids those with stones. The whole herb crushed with lard is good for swellings. According to Pliny, via Platearius, burdock or dock is a hot and dry herb having the power of dissolving, relaxing, opening, and attenuating. And therefore it is good against scabies, itching, <term>ferpigine</term> <gloss>a skin eruption</gloss>, and impetigo. It removes humors from the skin, and dissolves the hardness and tumor of abscesses. It is good against cold dropsy and obstruction of the spleen. Its juice, mixed with rue, purges the brain and [is good for] rheumatic flow if [used] in a small quantity.

A target sixteen fingers wide and of that same height, made of iron, is to be fashioned firm, like an elephant. 44
One should tie these firmly between the branches of two pillars, at the height of a man’s chest. 45
After piercing them and coming out the other side, and having penetrated the ground at the front, one should strike it skillfully so that the arrow remains exactly as it is. 46
When the arrow is held with the thumb at the side and joined by the index finger, with the bow held in the fist and the nock set level, it is called even-nocking. 47
In even-nocking from a chariot, the string-pull should be downward; therefore, a high-nocking is desired for upward shooting. 48
For a target situated at a great height, a low-nocking technique is appropriate; for a low target, an upward one; and for an even target, an even nocking is required. 49
A target sixteen fingers in circumference is called a gross target; one of two fingers is called subtle, and one with a five-finger crown is a pointed target. 50
A target inferred by sound is called a secondary target. Thus, the five types of targets should be presented for target practice. 51
Moving sideways, walking, rotating, and jumping: their locations are defined in the sky, on the earth, and in water. 52
The excellent distance is declared to be two hundred units, the medium one hundred and fifty, and the lowest is one hundred and twenty, when shooting on flat ground. 53
One should master the excellent distance by gradually using four arrows: even-feathered, thick and thin, and those without feathers, which are the urban style. 54
The king should demonstrate his agility by shooting one arrow into the falling target, one into the hand, and five into the sky, thus managing the targets. 55
