VII. Q. *Whether Genus, taken in common, is predicated univocally of the Genus of real being and of the being of reason?* I answer. These two Genera, according to their material parts, agree with each other only analogically; because Being can only be predicated analogically of real being and of the being of reason. But if they are taken in the being of a genus, and as they are of logical consideration, since the logical artifice and concept of both is absolutely the same, there is no doubt that they agree with each other univocally.

VIII. Q. *Since the highest Genus is as distant from the lowest as the lowest Genus is from the Species; why is the lowest Genus a different universal than the species, and the highest Genus not different from the lowest genus?* I answer. That this diversity is not to be sought from the greater or lesser distance that universals have in the predicamental line; but from their quidditative <note>pertaining to the essence or 'whatness' of a thing</note> concept. Since, therefore, both the highest Genus and the lowest Genus are predicated as a material part, while the species is predicated as the whole essence, there can be no doubt that the species differs more from the lowest Genus than the latter does from the highest.

IX. Q. *Is the concept defining a signed Genus <term>Genus signatum</term> <gloss>Genus as a conceptual category</gloss> the same concept that formally constitutes an exercised Genus <term>Genus exercitum</term> <gloss>Genus as it functions in practice</gloss>?* I answer. Since the concept making a Genus in exercised act must also make a universal in exercised act; and since a universal cannot be made by a simple concept that does not abstract from a multitude; and since the concept defining a signed Genus does not abstract from a multitude; just as it is a simple concept, so it is not a concept that makes a Genus in exercised act. Therefore, through the definition of a Genus, the Genus is defined in a signed manner, but it is not made in an exercised manner.

THE NATURAL THIRST. He demonstrates that there may be an innate thirst and cupidity in man to know. And because the cognition of divine things is that: through which we are blessed.
