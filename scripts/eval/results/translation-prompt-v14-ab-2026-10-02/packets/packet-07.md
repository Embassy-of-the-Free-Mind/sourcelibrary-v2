These are the words of Strabo: In Crete, the sacred rites of Jove are performed with orgiastic rituals. They called the Curetes certain youths who danced in armor. They also add a fable concerning the NATAL RITES OF JOVE, that Saturn was accustomed to swallow his children as soon as they were born. Rhea, however, attempted to hide the pains of childbirth, to carry the newborn infant away from the scene, and to save him as much as possible. For this purpose, she had the aid of the Curetes, who, armed and dancing with a tumult around the Goddess, terrified Saturn by using drums and other instruments that produced a din, and by deceiving him, they stole the son away. By this same action, it is handed down that the infant Jove was nourished by them. It is thought that they were called Curetes as if they were <term>curatores</term> <gloss>guardians/caretakers</gloss>, either because they performed the <term>kourotrophian</term> <gloss>the rearing of a boy</gloss>, that is, the feeding of Jove, or because they served him. This is what Suidas explains when he asserts that the Curetes were the foster-fathers and guards of Jove.

Arnobius, in his *Against the Nations*, book 3, commemorates those Curetes who are said to have hidden the wailing of Jove at one time with bronze, just as the Greeks indicate that the five Samothracian fingers are called Idaean Dactyls. And elsewhere: "Was Jove not saved from destruction by the favor of the Curetes? Whom they then carried away to the hidden places of Mount Ida: Hence Crete is called the island of the great Jove." The commentary of Servius, on book 4 of the *Georgics*, p. 149, is relevant to our matter: Ops, the wife of Saturn, gave Jove to the Curetes, the Corybantes, and the Idaean Dactyls to be guarded, on the Dictaean mountain of Crete, lest Saturn consume him as he was accustomed to do with all his sons.

# PHILOSOPHICAL ASSERTIONS.

XXXIII. Regarding imaginary time, we judge the same as we do regarding imaginary space: namely, that outside of the intrinsic durations of things, which found various relations of distance and proximity, it is in reality nothing at all.

XXXIV. Finally, duration is either created or uncreated. The uncreated is proper to GOD alone and is called Eternity. It is defined by <term>Boethius</term> <gloss>Roman philosopher, c. 480–524</gloss>: *The simultaneous and perfect possession of interminable life.* Created duration is either temporary or perpetual. Temporary is that which has both a beginning and an end. Perpetual is that which, at least, does not have an end. Such is the duration of the Angels and the rational soul, which is also called an <term>aevum</term> <gloss>age/duration of spirits</gloss>, and is commonly defined as: *The interminable permanence of a thing in a state once received.*

### ON QUANTITY.

XXXV. Aristotle describes a <term>quantum</term> <gloss>quantity</gloss> here in Book 5, text 18: *That which is divisible into things that are present within it, of which each one is a certain "one," and this "something" is apt to be.* That is (as Father <term>Suarez</term> <gloss>Francisco Suárez, 1548–1617, Spanish Jesuit philosopher</gloss> interprets), those things which are each apt by their own nature to remain as a certain whole, separated from others, and which is divisible according to the force of quantity—namely, through physical and natural divisibility—by the force of which one body naturally expels another, or its parts, from the place they previously occupied.

XXXVI. There is an opinion, as Father <term>Suarez</term> says, sufficiently common among the Thomists, that the primary formal effect of quantity is the distinction of integral parts, such that without quantity neither prime matter nor any other material entity has any integral parts. Following the Thomists from our own ranks is Father <term>Vasquez</term> <gloss>Gabriel Vázquez, 1549–1604, Spanish Jesuit theologian</gloss>, Volume 3, in part 3, distinction 190, from number 20. But I argue to the contrary: 1. Just as every thing is an entity through itself, so it is one through itself, and consequently divided and distinct from any other thing. 2. Because otherwise one would have to proceed to infinity. For if one thing is distinct from another by a third entity, then it must again be distinguished from that third entity by a fourth, and so on to infinity. 3. Because matter is presupposed to quantity with its entire entity. Therefore, it cannot receive parts from it; for since a whole is nothing other than all its parts, that which is presupposed with its entire entity must also be presupposed with its parts. Therefore, the primary formal effect of quantity cannot consist in the distinction of parts.

THE NATURAL THIRST. He demonstrates that there can be an innate thirst and cupidity in man for knowledge. And because the cognition of divine things is that through which we are blessed, and we cannot have this perfectly through our own industry, nor through a natural path, if we are not illuminated by the Holy Spirit: which Christ noted in the gospel of Saint John, Chapter IV, when asking the Samaritan woman for a drink at the well of Jacob, and she denying it to him, alleging that Jews do not use Samaritan things, he added: "If you knew who it is that asks you for a drink, you would ask him, and he would give you water from a spring that leaps into eternal life." The young woman, therefore, after many words asked him, and Christ filled her with the Holy Spirit. For which reason the poet most wisely demonstrates at present that the natural thirst, i.e., the natural cupidity for knowing and understanding, is never sated except with the water for which the young Samaritan woman asked for grace, i.e., the grace of the Holy Spirit. WAS TROUBLING ME. It is a metaphor from grain already threshed, which, so that it may remain purged and clean, is "worked," i.e., it must be passed from sieve to sieve; thus our soul, wishing to find the truth, is purged from time to time. AND HASTE WAS GOADING ME demonstrates that at one and the same time the soul was eager to investigate the cause of the shaking of the mountain and of the song of the souls, and the body had to accelerate in following its journey. But Statius, appearing, declares that which neither Danthe, i.e., the sensuality, nor Virgil, i.e., the human intellect, although illuminated by much doctrine, could understand by themselves. But Statius, who is accustomed to such things, declares it. And he puts Statius for the human intellect, which extends itself to comprehend that which cannot be comprehended through the path of reason. And by comparison he demonstrates according to what Luke writes in the 24th chapter.
