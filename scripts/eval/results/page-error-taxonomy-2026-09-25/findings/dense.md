- page-num / sig / catchword invented or truncated: 3 (Concoregio 76, 77; Spinoza 488)
- spread not split / image does not match the transcribed half: 2 (Poliphile 104, 105)
- marginalia dropped, misplaced or misassigned: 5 (Spinoza 488, 489; Browne 246; Kircher 316, 317)
- raw or broken markup visible: 3 (Poliphile 104, Browne 246, Spinoza 489)
- inconsistent OCR convention on adjacent pages: 3 books (Poliphile, Browne, Kircher)
- Hebrew garbled or dropped: 2 (Kircher 316, 317)
NEW classes proposed:
- convention-flip between neighbours: adjacent pages of one book transcribed under different conventions (ſ kept / ſ→s / ſ→f; reflowed vs line-broken). Invisible on any single page; visible to search and to anyone quoting across a break.
- footnote-key drift: superscript key letters in the translation shift by N places against the printed marginal key, so every gloss after the drift point points to the wrong term.
- reader-annotation transcription: a later reader's handwritten marks transcribed as if they were printed text (<insert>em H</insert>).
- stamp-as-content: a library ownership stamp mislabelled "printer's mark" and shown to the reader.
- fluent-omission: a skipped OCR line fuses two sentences, and the translator produces a fluent, false sentence ("milk within, and it will become copper"). Undetectable from the English alone.
- invented apparatus: a page number, signature or catchword that is not on the leaf.
Surprises: the page break is where the harm concentrates. 4 of 6 pairs had a defective join (leak plus duplicate, doubled catchword, mistranslated broken word), and each one traces back to how the OCR handled the catchword or the last broken word. The English "translation" of Browne is a modernising copy that silently repairs a ſ→f OCR, which hides that the searchable layer is bad. The worst single defect (the skipped line in Kircher) cannot be seen without the image.
