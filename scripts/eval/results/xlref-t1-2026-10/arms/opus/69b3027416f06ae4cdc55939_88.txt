For no command to which some reason is attached is accustomed, or able, to bind anyone beyond that reason of the command. *Be subject*, <term>hypotagete</term> <note>original Greek: "ὑποτάγητε"</note>, that is, if you look at the force of the word, subordinated, or lawfully subject; for <note>original Greek: "ἡ δὲ τάξις νόμος"</note> "order is law," says *Aristotle*. *Be subject for the Lord's sake*. Why? Because both king and governor are appointed by God for the punishment of evildoers and the praise of those who do good. *For such is the will of God*. That is, that we should obey such rulers as are described here; of others there is not a word here. You see how excellently the reason of this command holds together. He adds in verse 16, *as free men*: not, therefore, as slaves. What if, the other way round, they reign for the torment and ruin of the good, for the impunity, praise and rewards of the wicked? Shall we be subject forever, not only private men, but the leading men, all the magistrates, and finally the Senate itself? Is it not called a human ordinance? Why then shall human power be strong enough to establish what is good and wholesome for men, yet not strong enough to remove what is evil and ruinous to them? But, you say, the king to whom they are commanded to be subject was at that time at Rome the tyrant Nero; therefore we must be subject even to tyrants. But I reply, first, that even this is doubtful, whether Nero or Claudius held power at that time <note>Milton questions the date of First Peter, implying it might belong to Claudius's reign</note>; and that those who are commanded to be subject were strangers, scattered, private persons, not consuls, not praetors,

<summary>Milton answers Salmasius's appeal to 1 Peter 2:13-17, arguing that the command to be subject applies only to rulers who punish evil and reward good, and that it bound scattered private strangers, not senators or magistrates. He denies that the passage obliges a free people to submit to tyrants such as Nero.</summary>
