Therefore the gods are the rulers of all species; these are followed by gods whose ruler is <term>ousia</term> <gloss>essence, being</gloss>. These are the sensible gods, alike to both their origins, who make all things through sensible nature, each one illuminating his own work, one through another. Of heaven, or whatever is contained under that name, the <term>ousiarch</term> <gloss>ruler of essence</gloss> is Jupiter. For through heaven Jupiter gives life to all things. Of the sun the ousiarch is light. For the good of light is poured into us through the orb of the sun. Of the thirty-six (whose name is Horoscopes) <note>The thirty-six "decans," star groups each governing ten degrees of the zodiac in Egyptian astrology.</note> among the stars that are always fixed in the same place, the ousiarch or ruler is the one they call Pantomorphos, or Omniform, who makes different forms in different species. The seven spheres that are called wandering <note>The seven planets known to antiquity, including the sun and moon.</note> have their ousiarchs, that is, their rulers, which they call Fortune and <term>Heimarmene</term> <gloss>Fate</gloss>, by which all things are changed, through the law of nature, with the firmest stability, and varied by everlasting motion. The air, however, is the instrument or machine of all things, through which all things come to be. Its ousiarch is the second <note>The text appears to break off here; most editions mark a gap at this point.</note> mortal things to mortals, and things like these. These things, then, being so, moving from the lowest to the highest, all things are so linked together, belonging to one another by nature, that mortal things are bound to mortal, and sensible things to sensible; but the summit of government obeys that highest Lord, so that they are not many, but rather one. For all things hang from one, or rather flow down from it; when they are seen as apart, they are believed to be many and separate, but united they are one, or rather two: that from which all things come to be, and that by which they come to be. <summary>In this passage of the Asclepius, Hermes describes the hierarchy of gods and their "ousiarchs," or rulers of essence: Jupiter rules heaven, light rules the sun, Pantomorphos rules the thirty-six decans, and Fortune and Fate rule the seven planets. He concludes that all things, linked from lowest to highest, depend on one supreme Lord and are ultimately one, or rather two: matter and the divine will.</summary>
