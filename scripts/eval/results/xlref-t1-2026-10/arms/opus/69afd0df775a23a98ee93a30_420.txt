Great too is the use of the hare for women. Its dried lung, drunk, helps the womb. Its liver, drunk in water with Samian earth <note>a medicinal clay from the island of Samos</note>, helps fluxes. Its rennet expels the afterbirth; women avoid the bath on the day before. Applied also with saffron and leek juice in wool, it expels dead fetuses. If women eat hares' wombs in their food, it is believed that males are conceived. This is also achieved with the hare's testicles and its rennet. A hare's unborn young, taken from the womb, brings renewed fertility to women who have stopped bearing. But for conception the <term>magi</term> <gloss>Persian wise men, Pliny's usual name for practitioners of magical medicine</gloss> also give the fluid of a hare to drink, both to the woman and to the man. Likewise to a girl nine pellets of its dung, so that her breasts may stay firm forever. Its rennet does the same. For that reason they smear the blood with honey on places where they do not want plucked hairs to grow back.

For swelling of the womb it is beneficial to apply the dung of a boar or of a pig with oil. It works more effectively when dried and sprinkled as a powder into a drink, for example when pregnant women or women after childbirth are racked with pain. Sow's milk drunk with <term>mulsum</term> <gloss>wine mixed with honey</gloss> aids women in childbirth. Drunk by itself, it refills the failing breasts of new mothers. Breasts smeared all around with a sow's blood grow less. If they ache, they are soothed by drinking donkey's milk; taken with honey added, it also aids their purgations. The aged tallow of the same animal heals ulcerations of the womb, and applied in wool it softens hardness of the womb. By itself, whether fresh or aged, applied in water, it has the power of a <term>psilothrum</term> <gloss>a depilatory</gloss>. The spleen of the same animal, aged and applied with water to the breasts, makes milk abundant. <unclear>Vultus suffigit.</unclear> <note>The printed reading here is corrupt; the context suggests a remedy for the womb by fumigation.</note> Donkey hooves burned as a fumigation hasten childbirth, so much so that they even bring on a miscarriage; nor are they used otherwise, since they kill a living fetus. The dung of the same animal, if applied fresh, is said to calm fluxes of blood wonderfully; and so too the ash of that dung, which is also beneficial to the womb when applied.

Mare's foam, smeared on for forty days before the first hairs appear, keeps them from growing. Likewise deer horn boiled down; it works better if the horns are fresh. The womb is helped by being washed with mare's milk. If the child in the womb is felt to be dead, the mare's spleen, drunk in sweet water, expels it. Likewise fumigation with the hoof, or with dried dung. Butter poured in checks a prolapsing womb. Ox bile mixed with rose oil opens a hardened womb, when applied outwardly in wool with terebinth resin. They also say that fumigation with the dung of a male ox holds back a prolapsing womb and aids childbirth; and that conception is helped by drinking cow's milk. It is certain that barrenness arises from injury suffered in childbirth. Olympias of Thebes affirms that this is remedied by bull bile, serpents' fat and verdigris, with honey added, the parts being treated before intercourse. Calf bile also, if sprinkled on the womb during the purgations at the time of intercourse, softens hardness of the belly; with the navel pricked it reduces flux; and it benefits the womb in every way. They fix the dose: bile to the weight of a <term>denarius</term> <gloss>a silver coin, here used as a unit of weight</gloss> with a third as much celery, mixed with almond oil, as much as seems enough; this they apply in wool. The bile of a male calf, ground with half its amount of honey, is kept for the womb. If women eat calf's flesh roasted with <term>aristolochia</term> <gloss>birthwort, a plant long linked with childbirth</gloss> around the time of conception, it is promised that they will bear males. Calf marrow boiled in wine and water with tallow benefits ulcerations of the womb. So too does fox fat, and cat excrement applied with resin and rose oil.

They think fumigation of the womb with goat horn is most useful. The blood of wild goats with sea-palm removes hair. The bile of other goats, sprinkled on, softens callus of the womb and after the purgation brings about conception. In this way, too, it acquires the power of a depilatory, if the hairs are plucked and it is kept applied for three days. Midwives promise that a flux, however immense, is stopped by drinking goat's urine, and also if the dung is applied. The membrane of she-goats in which the kid was born, dried and taken in wine as a drink, expels the afterbirth. They think fumigation of the womb with kids' hair is useful; and in a flux of blood, that rennet should be drunk or henbane seed applied. Aeschines <note>an Athenian physician cited by Pliny, not the orator</note> says that if a woman's loins are pricked with the blood of a black wild ox or of a he-goat, she comes to feel a distaste for love. The same writer says that love is aroused by drinking he-goat's urine, with nard added because of its foul smell.

For infants nothing is more useful than butter, alone or with honey, especially in teething, for the gums, and for ulcers of the mouth. A wolf's tooth tied on as an amulet keeps away infants' night terrors and the illnesses of teething. A wolf's skin does the same. Indeed, their largest teeth, tied on horses too, are said to give them tireless speed. Hare's rennet smeared on the breast stops infants' looseness of the bowels. A donkey's liver mixed with a little <term>panaces</term> <gloss>"all-heal," a medicinal plant</gloss> and dropped into the mouth protects infants from the <term>morbus comitialis</term> <gloss>epilepsy, literally "the assembly disease," because a seizure in public broke up the Roman assembly</gloss> and other diseases. They prescribe doing this for forty days. A donkey's skin thrown over infants makes them fearless. A horse's first-shed teeth, tied on infants, give them easy teething; more effectively if the teeth have not touched the ground. Calf's spleen is eaten in honey, and applied for pain of the spleen; with honey for running sores. Calf's spleen boiled in wine, ground, and applied heals little ulcers of the mouth. The magi pass a goat's brain through a gold ring and drop it into infants before they are given milk, against epilepsy and the other diseases of infants. Goat dung tied on in a cloth calms restless infants, especially girls. Gums rubbed with goat's milk or hare's brain make teething easy.

Cato <note>Cato the Elder, author of a work on farming and household remedies</note> believes that sleep comes from eating hare. The common people think that it also gives grace to the body for nine days, a frivolous jest; yet some reason must lie beneath so strong a belief. The magi say that sleep is brought on by the bile of a goat, but only one that has been sacrificed, smeared on the eyes or placed under the pillow. The ash of goat horn stops sweating in those anointed with it in myrtle oil. Boar's bile applied as a liniment, on the other hand, stimulates intercourse.
