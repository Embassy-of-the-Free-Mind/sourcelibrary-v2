*Corollary.* Hence, from the law of resistance and from the difference *Aa* between the arcs *Ca* and *CB*, the proportion of the resistance to gravity can be found very nearly. <note>In other words, by measuring how much shorter each swing of a pendulum becomes, one can estimate how strong the resistance of the medium is compared with the weight of the swinging body.</note>

For if the resistance *DK* is uniform, the figure *BKTa* will be a rectangle under *Ba* and *DK*; and hence the rectangle under ½ *Ba* and *Aa* will be equal to the rectangle under *Ba* and *DK*, and *DK* will be equal to ½ *Aa*. Therefore, since *DK* is the exponent of the resistance, and the length of the pendulum the exponent of gravity, the resistance will be to gravity as ½ *Aa* is to the length of the pendulum; exactly as was demonstrated in Proposition XXVIII.

If the resistance is as the velocity, the figure *BKTa* will be very nearly an ellipse. For if the body, in a non-resisting medium, described the length *BA* in one whole oscillation, its velocity at any place *D* would be as the ordinate *DE* of a circle described on the diameter *AB*. <note>An "ordinate" here is a line drawn perpendicular to the diameter, up to the curve. In a frictionless pendulum the speed at each point follows the height of a semicircle drawn over the path.</note> Accordingly, since *Ba* in the resisting medium and *BA* in the non-resisting medium are described in roughly equal times, and therefore the velocities at the individual points of *Ba* are very nearly to the velocities at the corresponding points of the length *BA* as *Ba* is to *BA*, the velocity at the point *D* in the resisting medium will be as the ordinate of a circle or ellipse described on the diameter *Ba*; and therefore the figure *BKVTa* will be very nearly an ellipse. Since the resistance is supposed proportional to the velocity, let *OV* be the exponent of the resistance at the middle point *O*; and the ellipse *BRVSa*, described with centre *O* and semi-axes *OB* and *OV*, will very nearly equal the figure *BKVTa*, and the rectangle *Aa* × *BO* equal to it. Therefore *Aa* × *BO* is to *OV* × *BO* as the area of this ellipse is to *OV* × *BO*: that is, *Aa* is to *OV* as the area of a semicircle is to the square of its radius, or as 11 to 7 approximately. <note>The ratio of a semicircle's area to the square of its radius is half of pi, about 1.571; Newton uses the familiar approximation 11/7, which equals about 1.571.</note> And therefore 7/11 *Aa* is to the length of the pendulum as the resistance of the oscillating body at *O* is to its gravity.

But if the resistance *DK* is in the duplicate ratio of the velocity, <note>"Duplicate ratio" is the older term for the ratio of squares: the resistance grows as the square of the speed.</note> the figure *BKVTa* will be almost a parabola, having its vertex at *V* and its axis *OV*, and will therefore be very nearly equal to the rectangle under ⅔ *Ba* and *OV*. Therefore the rectangle under ½ *Ba* and *Aa* is equal to the rectangle under ⅔ *Ba* and *OV*, and so *OV* is equal to ¾ *Aa*: and therefore the resistance of the oscillating body at *O* is to its gravity as ¾ *Aa* is to the length of the pendulum.

<summary>Newton completes his proof that the loss of a pendulum's swing equals an area built from the resistance along its path. In a corollary he shows how to estimate the ratio of resistance to gravity from the shortening of the swing, working out the cases of uniform resistance, resistance proportional to velocity, and resistance proportional to the square of velocity.</summary>
