For indeed, arsenic can scarcely be fixed at all <note>to "fix" a substance is to make it stable in the fire, so that it no longer flies off as vapor</note> unless the working artist has an equally thorough knowledge of its tinging spirit <note>original: "spiritum eius tingentem"; the active coloring principle of the substance</note>. On this very point all the philosophers have dozed, trying in vain to make something of it. Whoever, therefore, does not know this spirit should hold no hope of fixing arsenic, nor of giving it the power by which it may gain the perfection of transmutation. For this reason I make it known to all that the whitening mentioned above is drawn from a false foundation, and that by it copper is falsely whitened, not changed. The Sophists <note>Paracelsus's name for fraudulent or misguided alchemists who imitated the outward signs of the Art</note> mixed <term>Venus</term> <gloss>copper</gloss>, masked and painted up in this way, with twice its weight of <term>Luna</term> <gloss>silver</gloss>, and sold it to goldsmiths and to mint-masters. In the end, however, they turned themselves into counterfeiters: not only those who sold it, but also those who bought it.

Some of the Sophists took red arsenic <note>probably realgar or orpiment, red and yellow arsenic sulfides</note> in place of white, and this too ended in a false art. For however it is prepared, it yields nothing but whiteness. Some went further still, to common sulphur, which, yellow as it is, they boiled in vinegar, lye, or very sharp urines for a day and a night, until it came out white. Afterwards they raised it from common salt and from lime of eggshells <note>original: "calce ouorum"; calcined eggshell, used as an alkaline base</note>, boiled it and sublimed it <note>sublimation: heating a solid so that it rises as vapor and settles again as a purified deposit</note> by sublimation, and this many times over.
