4 The second difference between the Old and New Testament is placed in figures: that the former, with the truth absent, displayed only an image, and a shadow in place of the body; the latter shows the truth present and the solid body. And this is mentioned almost wherever the new testament is set against the old; yet it is treated more fully in the Epistle to the Hebrews than anywhere else. There the Apostle argues against those who did not think the observances of the Mosaic Law could be abolished without dragging with them the ruin of the whole of religion. To refute this error, he takes up <margin>Psalm 110, b, 4</margin> what had been foretold by the Prophet about the priesthood of Christ. For since an eternal priesthood is conferred on him, it is certain that the priesthood in which successors were substituted for one another every day is abolished. And he proves that the institution of this new priest prevails because <margin>Hebrews 7, b, 11; c, 19; and 9, c, 9; and 10, a, 1</margin> it is established by an oath. He adds afterward that in this transfer of the priesthood a change of the Testament is also involved. And he confirms by reason that this was necessary, since such was the weakness of the Law that it could bring nothing to perfection. Then he goes on to explain what that weakness was: namely, that it had outward righteousnesses of the flesh, which could not make its worshippers perfect according to conscience; that by the sacrifices of beasts it could neither blot out sins nor procure true holiness. He concludes, therefore, that there was in it a shadow of good things to come, not the living image of the things themselves; and so it had no other office than to be an introduction into a better hope, which is shown forth in the Gospel.

Here we must see in what respect the covenant of the Law is compared with the covenant of the Gospel, and the ministry of Christ with that of Moses. For if the comparison concerned the substance of the promises, there would be a great disagreement between the two Testaments; but since the state of the question leads us elsewhere, we must aim there in order to find the truth. Let us, then, set in the middle the covenant which God once ratified as eternal and never to perish. Its fulfillment, from which it finally has its firmness and validity, is Christ. While such confirmation is awaited, the Lord prescribes ceremonies through Moses, which are like solemn symbols of that confirmation. What came into dispute was this: whether the ceremonies ordained in the Law ought to give way to Christ. These, although they were only accidents of the covenant, or certainly additions and appendages, and (as the common people say) <term>accessoria</term> <gloss>accessories</gloss>, yet because they were the instruments for administering it, they bear the name of covenant; as is also customarily given to other Sacraments. In sum, therefore, the Old Testament is here called the solemn manner of confirming the covenant, comprised in ceremonies and sacrifices. Since nothing solid lies beneath it unless one passes further, the Apostle contends that it had to be made obsolete and abrogated, so that place might be given to Christ, the guarantor and mediator of a better Testament, through whom eternal sanctification has been acquired once for all for the elect, and the transgressions which remained under the Law have been blotted out.

Or, if you prefer, take it this way: the old Testament of the Lord was that which was handed down wrapped in the shadowy and ineffectual observance of ceremonies; and it was therefore temporary, because it was, as it were, held in suspense until it should rest on a firm and substantial confirmation. Only then did it become new and eternal, after it had been consecrated and established by the blood of Christ. Hence Christ calls the cup which he offers to the disciples at the Supper <margin>Matthew 26, c, 28</margin> the cup of the New Testament in his blood: to signify that the Testament of God truly attains its truth at the moment when it is sealed with his blood, by which it becomes new and eternal.

5 From this it is clear in what sense the Apostle said that the Jews were led <margin>Galatians 3, d, 24; and 4, a, 1</margin> to Christ by the <term>paedagogia</term> <gloss>tutelage, the guardianship of a child's attendant</gloss> of the Law before he was shown forth in the flesh. He also confesses that they were sons and heirs of God, but such as had to be kept, because of their childhood, under the custody of a tutor. <note>In the ancient world a <term>paedagogus</term> was the slave who escorted and supervised a child, not a teacher in the modern sense.</note> For it was fitting, while the sun of righteousness had not yet risen, that there should be neither so great a brightness of revelation nor so great a sharpness of understanding. So the Lord dispensed the light of his word to them in such a way that they still saw it from afar and dimly. Therefore Paul marks this slenderness of understanding with the word "childhood": the Lord willed it to be exercised in the elements of this world and in petty outward observances, as in the rules of a child's discipline, until Christ should shine forth, through whom the knowledge of the faithful people was to grow to maturity. Christ himself marked this distinction when he said that the Law and the <margin>Matthew 11, b, 13</margin> Prophets were until John; from then on the kingdom of God is proclaimed. What did the Law and the Prophets deliver to the people of their time? They gave a taste of that wisdom which was one day to be clearly manifested, and showed it beforehand as it gleamed from afar. But where Christ can be pointed out with the finger, the kingdom of God has been opened. For in him are laid up all the treasures <margin>Colossians 2, b, 9</margin> of wisdom and understanding, by which one penetrates almost to the very sanctuaries of heaven.

<summary>Calvin explains the second difference between the Old and New Testaments: the Old offered shadows and figures, the New the present truth and substance in Christ. Following Hebrews and Galatians, he argues that the ceremonies were temporary aids for a people in spiritual childhood, made obsolete once Christ sealed the eternal covenant with his blood.</summary>
