for he shines forth in piety, religion, prudence, worship and veneration of God, having beheld true reason as if with his eyes; and by the confidence of his belief he excels among men as far as the sun surpasses the other stars with its light. For the sun itself lights up the other stars not so much by the greatness of its power as by its divinity and holiness. For believe, O Asclepius, that this is a second god <note>original: "Secundum deum"; the Hermetic texts often call the sun a visible "second god," an image of the invisible supreme God</note>, governing all other things and illuminating all the living beings of the world, whether animate or inanimate. For if the world is a living being, always living, which was, and is, and will be, nothing in the world is mortal. For in each living part that is in the world itself, as in one and the same ever-living creature, there is no place for mortality. Therefore God himself must be completely full of life and eternity, if he must necessarily live forever. So the sun, just as the world is everlasting, is likewise forever the governor of living things, the one who constantly attends to their whole vitality and dispenses it. <note>original: "frequentator, & dispensator"; the sun is pictured as a steward who keeps visiting and distributing life</note> God, therefore, is the everlasting governor of living and life-bearing things that are in the world, and the eternal dispenser of that life. Yet he dispensed it once and for all by an eternal law, granting life to all living things, in the manner that I shall now describe.

<summary>Hermes compares the pious man, who outshines other people, to the sun, which outshines the stars by its holiness. The sun is presented as a "second god" governing and distributing life to an ever-living world in which nothing truly dies, under an eternal law set by God.</summary>
