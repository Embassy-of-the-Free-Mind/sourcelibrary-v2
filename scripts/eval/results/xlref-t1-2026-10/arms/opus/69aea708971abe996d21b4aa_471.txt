### On the shame that comes from undeserved praise.
## DIALOGUE XXVI.

> Praise sparingly; for when you have praised unjustly,
> If you then condemn again, it will look like fickleness.

*Sorrow.* I am accused of having praised an unworthy man.
<note>A large ornamental initial L opens Reason's reply.</note>
*Reason.* <meta>The OCR repeats Sorrow's line "Laudasse arguor indignum" at the start of Reason's reply; this appears to be a transcription duplication and is not rendered twice.</meta> It is indeed a fault, but it often grows from a good root. For good and innocent people imagine others to be such as they are themselves. This makes them readier to praise than is right. And although I admit that praising the unworthy is bad, blaming the undeserving is far worse.

*Sorrow.* I praised an unworthy man.

*Reason.* If you did it knowingly, you deserve blame; but if unknowingly, you can be excused. Even so, it is a lesser evil to be deceived than to deceive. For being deceived is another's crime; deceiving is the deceiver's own.

*Sorrow.* Another man's praise is turning into my disgrace.

*Reason.* No: rather it is your own fault, or your own ignorance. For just as no praise comes to you from what belongs to another, so no disgrace comes from it either.

*Sorrow.* I am deeply sorry that I praised an unworthy man.

*Reason.* Then take care that you do not often have to be sorry for this very thing. Beware of rushing rashly into praise or blame. For there is an appetite for both, and, to speak plainly, a disease, a kind of itch of a restless tongue that does not know how to keep still. Its bridle and its guarding are counted among the works of outstanding perfection, as Scripture says: <note>Petrarch is about to cite the Bible, probably the Letter of James 3:2, which calls the one who does not stumble in speech a perfect man able to bridle the whole body.</note>

<summary>Reason closes the dialogue on ill repute by urging Sorrow to embrace Virtue as a queen worth any loss. A new dialogue, the twenty-sixth, begins on the shame of having praised an unworthy person, and Reason warns against a restless tongue too quick to praise or blame.</summary>
