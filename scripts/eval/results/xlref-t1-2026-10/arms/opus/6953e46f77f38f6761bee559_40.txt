# SIDEREAL OBSERVATIONS

<meta>continues from previous page: the opening words "coetum offendis" (you will encounter a crowd) close the sentence already rendered on the previous page</meta>Further, and what is even more remarkable, the stars which every astronomer up to this day has called NEBULOUS <note>original: "NEBVLOSAE"; cloudy patches of light in the night sky</note> are flocks of small stars packed together in a wonderful way. From the mingling of their rays, while each one escapes the sharpness of our eyes because of its smallness or its very great distance from us, there arises that whiteness which until now has been believed to be a denser part of the sky, able to reflect the rays of the stars or of the Sun. We have observed several of these, and we have chosen to add the <term>Asterisms</term> <gloss>star groupings</gloss> of two of them.

In the first you have the NEBULA called that of the Head of Orion, in which we have counted twenty-one stars.

The second contains the NEBULA named PRAESEPE <note>Latin for "the Manger," today called the Beehive Cluster, in the constellation Cancer</note>, which is not a single star but a heap of more than forty little stars. Besides the <term>Aselli</term> <gloss>the "Little Donkeys," two stars beside the Manger</gloss>, we have marked thirty-six, arranged in the order that follows.

<summary>Galileo reports that the so-called "nebulous" stars are really dense clusters of faint stars whose blended light was mistaken for thicker parts of the heavens. He gives charts of two examples: the Head of Orion, with twenty-one stars, and the Praesepe, with thirty-six stars besides the Aselli.</summary>
