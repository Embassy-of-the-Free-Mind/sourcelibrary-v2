5 *sua sponte* without theoretical
instruction, ‘instinctively,’ beneficio
ingenii (Quint. v. 10. 121), cf. II.
46. 193.

6 *digessisse* So (or perhaps even
coegisse, § 191. II. 33. 142, ad Her.
IV. 40. 52) we must read for the
meaningless id egisse; as Quint. v.
10. 119 sqq, who evidently had this
passage before his eyes and employ-
ed it for his subject, clearly shows:
Haec non idcirco dico, quod inutilem
horum locorum—cognitionem puto
—sed ne se qui cognoverint ista—
perfectos protinus atque consumma-
tos putent—Neque enim artibus
editis factum est, ut argumenta in-
veniremus, sed dicta sunt omnia,
antequam praeciperentur, mox ea
scriptores observata et collecta edide-
runt; put together in a certain order
and classification (§§ 108, 186, 190;
II. 19. 79, ad fin.) [Kayser reads id
egisse*, Ell. would explain it, ei rei
operam dedisse].

8 *eiicio* II. 24. 102, ‘I do not
altogether reject.’

10 *exercitatio* To learn the theory
of rhetoric it is necessary, at least
for those who are at the beginning
of their career, to enter also upon a
definite practical exercise.

11 *quamquam* a rhetorical correc-
tion or recalling of the just-employed
vobis; sed after the parenthesis as
in § 163.

12 *ingrediuntur in stadium* a
phrase used of those who enter upon
a career, in order to begin their
course from the very starting-point.
Hence ingred. alone in the sense:
Or. 23 75, magnum opus et arduum
—sed ingredientibus (before one is
yet out at sea) considerandum fuit,
quid ageremus, nunc quidem iam
quocumque feremur danda nimirum
vela sunt.

13 *acie* §§ 81, 157, quasi ludicra,
II. 20. 84, i. e. quasi in ludo gladia-
torio, the palaestra, the fighting and
wrestling school, as Div. in Caec.
14. 47, prolusio and ipsa pugna are
opposed.
