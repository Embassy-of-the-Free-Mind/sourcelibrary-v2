Page 363, l. 1. *I, for one, do not know what I ought to say.* This speech Druon places in 412, about a year later than the last. Anysius had gone, and the situation had again become gloomy. The passionate outpouring of this oration led Lapatz, in his delightful commentaries on the letters of Synesius (p. 266) to emit the opinion that the document is a private letter which was mistaken in aftertime for a speech. He speaks of it as ‘si pathétique qu’on l’a prise à tort pour un discours.’ The suggestion is certainly an interesting one. The phrase (line 8, Migne) γράφε σὺ is one such as he would use to a correspondent, and is inexplicable as a phrase addressed to an audience. Again ἀγγειλάτω τις αὐτοῖς seems to mean: ‘Let *some one* of your circle of influential friends announce to the council &c.’ In other words: ‘if you write to every one you know, some one of them is sure to do it.’ The first sentence of the speech: ‘Ἐγὼ μὲν οὐκ κ.τ.λ. is clearly not an oratorical opening, but rather an epistolary one. Of the 159 actual letters of Synesius to correspondents, the following six commence with the first personal pronoun, ep. 25 ἐμοὶ μέν, ep. 30 ἐγὼ καὶ σοῦ, ep. 67 ἐγὼ καὶ βούλομαι, ep. 80 ἐγὼ μὲν οἷος ἦν, ep. 96 ἐγὼ μάρτυρα ποιοῦμαι, ep. 108 ἐμοὶ λόγχαι. This way of beginning a letter was not so uncommon in other fourth-century writers. Taking at random the first 157 letters of Libanius, I find seven which begin with the first personal pronoun, viz. epp. 22, 45, 50, 52, 58, 76, 125 (ed. Foerster). Julian, writing to Hecebolius, begins: ‘Ἐγὼ μὲν κέχρημαι τοῖς Γαλιλαίοις . . . πράως . . . ὥστε μηδένα μηδαμοῦ βίαν ὑπομένειν, the famous defence of his policy towards the Christians (115 Bidez-Cumont, 43 Hertlein, 40 Wright), again to Atarbius we find ‘Ἐγὼ . . . οὔτε κτείνεσθαι τοὺς Γαλιλαίους . . . βούλομαι (83 Bidez-Cumont, 71 Hert, 37 Wright) the first sentence in his letter. Other instances might be given, e.g. epp. 1, 30, 71 (Wright).

Now when we turn from the epistolographoi to the orators, we observe that it seems to have been an unwritten law with these that a speech must not begin with ἐγὼ . . . or ἐμοί . . . This is true of the speeches, about 160 in number, of the ten great orators and those few which we possess of Gorgias, Lesbonax, Herodes, Alcidamas, and Hyperides.
