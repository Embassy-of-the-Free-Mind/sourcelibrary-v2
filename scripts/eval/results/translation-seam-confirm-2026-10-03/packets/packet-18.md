The Chapter <note>the body of canons governing a diocese</note> during a vacancy of the see <note>original: "sede vacante"</note> enjoys the right of <term>Austregarum</term> <gloss>the right to have disputes settled by an arbitral tribunal rather than by the Imperial Chamber</gloss>, both because the episcopal power resides within it (by argument of chapter 11 and 13, *extra on Majority and Obedience*, and it enjoys the right of a Bishop in all things, *unique chapter on Majority and Obedience in the Sixth*), wherefore it is also called to Assemblies, though not Electoral ones; for the right of electing the Emperor is granted to Ecclesiastical Electors by special privilege: but rather to Collegial assemblies, as the subscription of the *Recessus* <gloss>Imperial decree/conclusion of an assembly</gloss> of the years 1555, 1559, and 1576 proves. 6 Therefore, the Astronomer will consider motion, magnitude, and figure, which are affections and boundaries of natural bodies, but only as certain Mathematical ratios are found in them (such as the ratio of greater to smaller, equal to unequal, distant to near, proportionality, and the like); whereas the Physicist refers everything to the nature from which they are understood to flow.

7 So that it is no wonder that due to this diversity, sometimes (as in 2 Physics, ch. 2) the Philosopher said that Astrology abstracts from motion, yet sometimes (1 Metaphysics, text 19) that it considers with motion, or indeed even the motion itself.
