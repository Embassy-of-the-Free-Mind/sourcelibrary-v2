## IX.

The liberal person should strive to observe the mean in receiving no less than in giving. Therefore, he should not receive from those from whom he ought not, nor when he ought not at all. Nor should he be importunate or troublesome to others in asking and receiving, lest he be called a <term>beggar</term> <gloss>original: "αἰτητικός"</gloss> by others. We have already established, not without foundation, that the liberal person ought to be more occupied with giving than with receiving. Likewise, let him who intends to do good confer benefits from his own property, and not pour out the liquor of liberality by diverting the sources of others to himself. *For indeed, as Seneca says in his first book on Clemency, he is not a liberal person who is liberal with what belongs to another, but he who, in what he gives to another, subtracts from himself.* Meanwhile, the liberal person should not neglect his own household affairs, but should strive to promote them by honest and lawful means, at least to the end that he may possess his own goods, with which he may both suffice for his own sustenance and, in an urgent necessity, be able to assist the indigence of others. For by prospering ourselves, we are liberal to others. See the Axiom 2, Table 8 of the Course of Ethics by the Honorable President.

## X.

Regarding the extremes of liberality, it has Prodigality as its opposite in excess, and Avarice in defect. The former, so called from "squandering" <note>Latin: *prodigendo*</note>, is a vice by which we exceed the measure in giving and diminish the measure in receiving, with no regard for the circumstances that were suggested in the immediately preceding theses. Prodigality is usually divided into two types: *Simple and mixed.* The former squanders resources rashly on any object whatsoever, with all hope of receiving back abandoned. This vice principally falls upon the age of youth. *Mixed* prodigality is implicated with many other vices; it bestows upon some, but takes away from others through injury, whence such a prodigal person is also unjust and stained with other vices.
