->The treatment of pleurisy is accomplished through surgery, pharmacy, and diet.<-

<margin>Treatment.</margin>
XLI. The last thing we undertake to discuss in our present dissertation is the **TREATMENT** of pleurisy. This must be established according to a threefold indication—namely, the preventative, the curative, and the vital—so that the humors causing trouble by their motion may be drawn back and diverted (on account of the nearby heart, one should refrain here from using repellents and interceptors); those abundant in quantity must be diminished and evacuated; those excessive in quality must be altered; and the humor that has already flowed in must be dispersed, or if that is not possible, matured and concocted so that it may be rejected by coughing as soon as possible; and finally, so that the strength and vigor of the parts may be conserved. All and each of these things must be satisfied through remedies drawn from surgery, pharmacy, and diet.

## IX.

The liberal man should also strive to observe the mean in receiving. Therefore, he should not receive from those from whom he ought not, nor where it is least appropriate to receive. He should not be importunate or troublesome to others in asking and receiving, lest he be called <term>αἰτητικός</term> <gloss>a mendicant or beggar</gloss> by others. We have already established, not without foundation, that the liberal man ought to be more occupied with giving than with receiving. Furthermore, he who is about to perform a benefit should do so from his own resources, not diverting the fountains of others toward himself to pour forth the liquid of liberality. *For indeed, as Seneca says in his first book on Clemency, he is not liberal who is liberal with what belongs to another, but he who subtracts from himself what he gives to another.* Meanwhile, the liberal person should not neglect his own family affairs, but should strive to promote them by honest and lawful means, if only to the end that he might possess his own goods with which he can both provide sustenance for his own and, when necessity presses, assist the indigence of others. For we are able to benefit ourselves when we are liberal to others. See the Axiom 2, Table 8 of the Moral Course by the Lord President.

## X.

As regards the extremes of liberality, it has Prodigality as its opposite in excess, and Avarice in defect. The former, named for spending, is a vice by which we exceed the limit in giving and diminish the limit in receiving, having no regard for the circumstances which have been suggested in the immediately preceding theses. Prodigality is usually established as twofold: *Simple* and *Mixed*. The former rashly pours out resources on any matter whatsoever, with all hope of receiving omitted. This especially befalls the youthful age. The *Mixed* is entangled with many other vices; it bestows upon some, but unjustly takes from others, whence such a prodigal is both unjust and stained with other vices.

The reason "mostly" is used is that there are many such things. If refuting the ten categories <note>referring to the system of categories in Vaisheshika-derived philosophies</note>, nine categories are considered truly existent, while the tenth is "non-existence" <term>abhava</term>. Because the majority are truly existent, the word "mostly" is used. Regarding "obtained through direct perception" <term>pratyaksa</term>, if refuting the six categories, according to the treatise below, five categories—such as substance—are obtained through direct perception. Only the category of "inherence" <term>samavaya</term> is not obtained through direct perception; thus, the word "mostly" is used. If refuting the ten categories, speaking generally of all categories, [such as] difference, inherence, those with ability, and those without ability, "non-existence" is said not to be obtained through direct perception, while the other five are. However, among those that are mostly truly existent, five are obtained through direct perception and four are not. Therefore, it is said that the majority are obtained through direct perception. The word "mostly" applies to both "truly existent" and "direct perception." Since maintaining the six categories is their original position, the *Shastra of One Hundred Verses* <note>original: "Bai lun"</note> and others do not refute the ten categories, and this treatise does the same. However, refuting the ten categories as well does not contradict the logic.

Their position is irrational.

This follows with the second part: a direct refutation of their errors. Within this, there are three sections. First, a general refutation. Next, the opponent responds.

and he encamped with the entire army against <term>Ocilis</term>. Being successful in warfare, he immediately brought the city to submission and granted them mercy, having taken hostages and thirty talents of silver. The <term>Nercobriges</term> <gloss>a Celtiberian tribe</gloss>, having learned of this change in fortune, sent envoys and asked what they must do to obtain peace. When he ordered them to provide one hundred horsemen to serve with him, they promised to do so. However, they did not appear at the appointed place, and they came to the one hundred horsemen who were leading the baggage train, as if they had indeed come according to the agreement. When they were at the rear of the column, they claimed to be ignorant of the agreements and to have erred. He gave the one hundred horsemen <note>this refers to his own cavalry, who were then returned</note> to the horses themselves, and having overrun the plain, he distributed the booty to the army and encamped at the city. The <term>Nercobriges</term>, as siege engines and earthworks were being set up against them, sent a herald, reminding him that a truce had been sworn, and asked for mercy. He said he would grant it if the <term>Belli</term>, the <term>Titthi</term>, and the <term>Arevaci</term> would all join in the petition. These nations, learning of this, eagerly sent embassies and requested that Marcellus, having imposed a penalty upon them, grant them peace and lead them to the terms established by <term>Gracchus</term>. Certain locals, who had been fought by them, spoke against this. Marcellus sent envoys from both sides to Rome to confer with one another, and privately wrote to the Senate, urging them to reach a settlement. He wanted the war to be resolved under his own command, thinking that he would gain distinction from this as well. Of the envoys, those from the friendly side entered the city and were entertained, while those from the hostile side, as was the custom, lodged outside the walls.
