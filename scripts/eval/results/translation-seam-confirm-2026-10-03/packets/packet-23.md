Just as, therefore, the proposition "Every man is an animal" is valid because this predicate applies to all possible individual men, and thus infinitely in a <term>syncategorematic</term> <gloss>potentially, or successively infinite</gloss> sense, and on the other hand does not apply to certain other things, such as wood or stone, so by the same law will the proposition that is made about future years or men be valid, because it applies to individuals and does not apply to certain other things. Otherwise, almost all scientific propositions, which always apply collectively or distributively to infinite possible individuals contained in this or that species, would have to be condemned. Moreover, an equivocation is contained in the objection. For some things are future with the simultaneity of their parts, such as a man and a horse, and these must sometime exist in the same difference of time according to all their parts. Others, however, are future successively, such that one part exists before another, as happens in motion or in time, or in other collections of things that are produced through various times. And in these, it is sufficient that their individual parts be future in some difference of time, but it is not required that the parts themselves all coexist at any one time. This response is confirmed and explained. Even men who are future until the Day of Judgment will never actually be together. Likewise, the hours of tomorrow will not all be together at any distance of time; yet who would deny that it can be affirmed simply of all of these, and of the whole of tomorrow, that they are future?

You will urge: That can be called future which, in its entirety, will be past. But eternal duration <note>from the side of the future</note>, and the individuals that are produced successively in that duration, will not be adequately past at any distance of time. Therefore, they cannot be called simply future. I respond: It is no less part of the nature of the future to be sometimes present than to be sometimes past. Indeed, Aristotle, as is clear from logic, says that "past" and "future" are cases of the present tense in verbs, and both he and other philosophers explain the matter through the future and the past in relation to the present as to the primary <term>analogatum</term> <gloss>the primary thing to which a name refers</gloss>. And yet, for something to be called future, it is sufficient if any part of it, taken separately, is at some time present. Therefore, it also suffices if any part of it, considered separately, is at some time past, although in its entirety it is never adequately past.

I respond secondly. To say that infinite duration is not future because it will never be past is a <term>petitio principii</term> <gloss>begging the question</gloss>. For "past" <note>Latin: "præter-itum"</note> means the same as "passed-by" or "gone-by." But "past" signifies the term and end of a thing, that which is gone by. Therefore, it is the same to say "Infinite duration is not future because it will never be past" as it is to say "It is not future because it has no end" or "because it is infinite." And thus, in other words, that which is in question is assumed as the reason.

I respond thirdly: This mode of philosophizing is openly repugnant to Aristotle, who, not only in the 8th book of the *Physics*, but in this very 3rd book where he denies the infinite, supposes that the world has been from eternity and thus concedes that infinite time and infinite individuals are past. Therefore, he did not consider all those things absurd which are inferred from this by reason of the infinite, but he employed other reasons to exclude it, by which it is only proven that it is not given as a matter of fact and according to the order of nature. To Aristotle, however, who did not recognize that God could act miraculously and against the requirement of nature, the same reasons concluded even regarding the possible.

Nor are the differences sought from the form known with greater trouble. For that the body is purged more than is right, the quantity and quality of the rejected matter, the patient's <term>dysphoria</term> <gloss>uneasiness/distress</gloss>, and the worsening of symptoms indicate; as can be gathered from *Aphorisms 1, 2* and *Commentary 2 on the Prognostics of Hippocrates, text 14*.

<margin>Symptoms.</margin>
51. The symptoms, however, are more often indicated beyond those already mentioned, which evidently not only accompany the purgation itself, but also partly accompany and partly follow any other laborious purgations; such as nausea, vomiting, fainting, and the rest, which Mesue recounts in the *Canon of Intention 2, Summa 2, chapter 3*, looking either to the natural, vital, or animal faculty.

52. We, however, for the sake of clarity, neglecting this order and having followed Mesue in the *Canon of Intention 4*, will consider them according to the parts which are most especially afflicted in this case. Wherefore, first in the stomach there presents itself its weakness; then pain, thirst, and hiccup; but in the intestines, an ulcer, bloody excretion, and tenesmus; in the head, pain, vertigo, and weakness of sight; but in the whole body here and there, convulsion; then in general, fever, weakness, and lassitude. We shall outline in a few words the generation of all these, whether symptoms or symptomatic diseases, from <term>hypercatharsis</term> <gloss>excessive purgation</gloss>.

53. Therefore I. Weakness is induced in the stomach 1. by the medicine, which, by infesting it with manifest and occult qualities, dissolves its strength: since all medicines which purge injure the stomach, if we believe Galen in the book *On Those Who Ought to be Purged*. Also *On the Faculties of Purgative Medicines, chapter 1* and *On Acute Diseases, Commentary 1, text 11 and 12*. 2. From humors carried upward. 3. By the immoderate evacuation itself: whence the body of the stomach is liquefied, thinned, relaxed, and with the heat resolved, coldness is introduced. 4. By error in the use of non-natural things; especially by the intemperate drinking of water for the sake of extinguishing thirst, which accompanies immoderate purgations, assumed.
