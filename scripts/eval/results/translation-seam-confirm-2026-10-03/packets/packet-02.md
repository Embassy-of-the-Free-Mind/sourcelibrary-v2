<header>Treatise on the Meaning of the Hundred Dharmas</header>

Because of the four great elements and false form, the light of wisdom, which is originally present, is transformed into false perception. Taking these false forms as the object of perception, and having perceived falsely for a long time, one clings to parts of the four great elements as "self." Thus, false perception relies upon these four elements to form a "body." Therefore, the four great elements are originally devoid of consciousness; it is only because of the attachment of false perception that they seem to have consciousness. The true mind is limitless, but now it is sealed and constrained by ignorance, slipping into the four great elements to function as a mind. This is what is called "mixing forms with false thoughts to form a body," which is why it is said, "Perception is sentient beings." This constitutes the sentient beings of the five <term>skandhas</term> <gloss>aggregates</gloss>. Therefore, the inner five roots and the outer six dusts all belong to the "object aspect" of the eight consciousnesses. Consequently, those who practice Chan must first detach from the body and mind internally and abandon the world externally; this is precisely to extinguish these two aspects—the object and the perceiving mind—in order to investigate the root essence of the eight consciousnesses' ignorance. This is why the world of body and mind must be dissolved, as it is always an obstacle to life and death. The "two attachments" of self and phenomena mentioned refer to clinging to the body as "self" and clinging to the roots and dusts as "phenomena." For the practitioners of the two vehicles, breaking the view of self is enough to escape the cycle of fractional birth and death. However, breaking the attachment to phenomena—which one begins to overcome only from the first stage of the path after passing through the three stages of virtue—is not an easy task.

The twenty-four types of non-associated dharmas represent the modes of form and mind. They are established nominally based on the previous three categories of dharmas, such as "acquisition" and the like. They are distinguished from mind, mental factors, and form, which is why they are called "non-associated." Because they do not associate with the mind-king, and because they cannot perform good or evil, they are not mental factors. They are merely distinctions within the phases established by <term>vijnapti-matra</term> <gloss>consciousness-only</gloss>. Because they are dharmas that the "self" attaches to, they are listed among the conditioned dharmas. There are many interpretations, but they are not the primary focus, so it is unnecessary to elaborate, lest it hinder the main practice.

The ninety-four types mentioned above are called conditioned dharmas. Because they are the dharmas of sentient beings' life and death, they are calculated by false consciousness and involve activity, hence they are called "conditioned" and "worldly dharmas." The sixteen unconditioned dharmas have six types: space unconditioned, cessation through discernment, cessation not through discernment, the unconditioned of immobility, the unconditioned of the cessation of perception and feeling, and the unconditioned of thusness. These six types are established as "unconditioned" to distinguish them from the conditioned. Although they are called "supramundane," they actually include the small vehicle; the "immobility" refers to the third fruit, <term>anagamin</term> <gloss>non-returning</gloss>, and the "cessation of perception and feeling" refers to the samadhi of total extinction. "Space unconditioned" derives its name from a metaphor, meaning the nature of the unconditioned dharma is like space, having no activity. The following five unconditioned dharmas are all understood through this metaphor. However, this metaphor of space has different levels of magnitude. For instance, the *Avatamsaka Sutra* says: "If one wishes to understand the Buddha's realm, one should purify the mind, making it like space, distancing oneself from false thoughts and all attachments, so that wherever the mind is directed, there is no obstruction." It also says: "The pure dharma-body is like space." This points directly to the emptiness of the nature of the dharma-realm; this is the great vehicle's true emptiness of dharma-nature, which is the absolute rejection of false pollution within the one mind. It is like space because it is empty. The meaning of "space" here spans both large and small vehicles, but it is specifically used as a metaphor for the vastness and lack of activity of the five subsequent unconditioned dharmas. "Cessation through discernment" refers to using analytical discernment to sever and extinguish; it is the realization of the truth revealed by the wisdom of the undefiled, which is why it is given this name. This belongs to the partial realization of the practitioners of provisional teaching and the Nirvana-emptiness realized by the two vehicles; it is properly attributed to cessation, so it is called "realization of cessation."
