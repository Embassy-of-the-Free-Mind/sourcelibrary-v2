For everything that produces by its own being, also acts, and bestows upon that thing what belongs to its essence. For it gives what it has, not what it does not have. Now its <term>hyparxis</term> was to be the One-All. Therefore, it gave to this generation of its own what it was, namely, that it should be One and all things. But why do we call the former Father, and call the begotten the Unity? (According to the doctrine of both Proclus and Damascius the Platonists, although theologians most truly hold that all things are contained in God, and not in a Son distinct from the Father and the Holy Spirit, in a most excellent way). Among Zoroaster and Hermes, indeed, that One also is called the Monad. But for us, for the sake of clearer doctrine, it has seemed better to call that One, this Unity. One, indeed, because it is only one and independent. Unity, however, both depends upon that, and looks toward multitude; in that One-All, indeed, there is multitude, but unified and most simple. In Unity, however, the multitude is already distinct and poured out into number. The One has snatched itself above every relation and transcends every respect of multitude. Unity, however, looks toward number and is referred to multitude. Therefore, this primary Unity is from the first One. But the multitude that is in this Unity comes from that, if I may say so, omnity. But for what reason did that One-All bring forth this Unity-All from itself? Why did that Father beget this Son? Was He who grants fruitfulness to all other things required to be sterile? He neither ought to have been, nor could He have been. He ought to have been, because He was and is good. He could have been, because He was and is omnipotent. Therefore, He had a necessity to produce; He made the will by producing. Will created necessity. For necessity follows the will of God, says Hermes. Therefore all things are from Him and through Him. Which he also said most correctly: 
"By His own will You created all things."
By His will He founded all things.
"And this is His work, to see the Father. His essence is to carry all things in the womb and to do."
And this is His work, to be the Father. His essence is to carry all things in the womb, and to do.
Elsewhere also he teaches thus:
"For all things that are, these are one in God, and made by God, and depending from thence."
For all things, whatsoever they are, are in God, and made by God, and dependent from thence.
Therefore, God is both the begetter and maker of all things begotten. But in what way did He beget or make them? By the power, clearly, which He had in making them. For God is the Father of beings and the begetter. But beings were made and begotten by Him. Between these two is the middle power, through which the begetter produces, and the begotten are produced. For unless He were able to produce, these things would not have been produced. Power, therefore, is a progression and a kind of ascent from the One, and an extension into the essence of beings. Thus, the transition from the One is made through power into essence. Therefore, this most primary power is supra-essential. For it is before essence, since it is intermediate between the One and essence. It precedes beings and is the first generation of the One itself. And for this reason, it is rightly celebrated by Zoroaster as the power of the Father, and by Hermes as the Son of the Father, as we shall see shortly. Let us return to the mode. If the transition into being is made from the One through the power of the One, and as if through an outpouring and progression of the One: then essence is begotten from the One by a certain motion. For it is necessary that this happens either by motion, or by rest, or in both ways, or in neither. If in neither, then not in both. If not in both, at least in one or the other. But is it by rest or by motion? Hermes says: 
"The One remains standing and is not moved."
The One, however, remains standing and is not moved. But that which remains does not progress. And conversely, that which progresses does not remain. But the One itself remains. Therefore, it does not progress. Progression is a certain motion. But motion is an action. Every action, however, is either in essence or from essence. In essence, it does not seem that the motion of progression and production happens. For beings would not exist thence through that motion. Therefore, in order for them to emerge, they required motion from essence. Or rather, from the <term>hyparxis</term> of the One. For its <term>hyparxis</term> is that which is the essence to beings. What then? By the exit of beings from the <term>hyparxis</term> of the One, is the One emptied for beings? Far from it. For it is not moved. Therefore, they proceeded by rest. But it does not rest either. For it does not have something in which to rest other than in itself. Nor is there anything toward which it might move. For it is everywhere. For Hermes says most truly:
"And nothing is outside of Him, nor is He outside of anything."
And nothing is outside of Him, and He is outside of nothing. But in what way, finally, did He produce the secondary beings? Perhaps by His own rest He draws them out from Himself? Are they indeed removed from Him, while He remains? But a drawing out is a certain action. And action is motion. Therefore, is He also moved? Does He have rest first, and motion afterwards? Does the question therefore return to the same point? Not at all. But in Him, rest moves, and motion rests. And that which moves in Him is moved stably from Him, and that which rests, rests movably. Namely, it remains itself. And it remains, drawing all things out of itself by its own fruitfulness and bringing them forth outside itself. It brings them forth from itself, remaining itself what it was before. For in what way could it become less than the One? Or how could the things which it brings forth from itself depart outside of it, when it is everywhere? And does it still contain all produced things within itself? Therefore, it is not emptied by those first things of its own, nor does it evacuate itself so that it becomes less than the One, or less than all things.

Christ wanted them to remain with him for some time on these lands, so that they might be both faithful witnesses and suitable teachers. How this election was made is described here, and explained at Matthew 10. Let us, however, briefly weigh the individual points as they are proposed by Mark.

<margin>Acts 1, 21</margin>
<margin>Matt. 10.1</margin>

First, he indicates where it took place: and he ascended (he says) into the mountain. In Luke 6 these things are explained more fully, namely that he prayed there all night, and thereafter called to himself those whom he wanted, as Mark has it. These things signify the gravity of the ministry. # COMMENTARIES ON ALCHEMY
## BY ANDREAS LIBAVIUS, M. D.
### Part II
#### THIRD TREATISE.
#### CONCERNING THE AZOTH OF THE PHILOSOPHERS, AND THE PERMANENT WATER.

TO THE FORMERLY CELEBRATED PHYSICIAN, PHILOSOPHER, AND CHEMIST, DR. JOHANNES RUNGIUS, of pious memory, of Stettin in Pomerania, when he had sent me a certain dry, shimmering mercurial powder, with the consistency of asbestos reduced to a feathery softness, under the name of Azoth and permanent water.

<margin>4. Metaphysics 8.</margin>
Since you, an outstanding man, have seemed to have possessed knowledge of natural things through your exploration, and have known that Aristotle tried to include everything within universal precepts, you know what he proclaims in his Meteorology regarding <term>peri peKton kai apekton</term> <gloss>things congealable and non-congealable</gloss>, namely: "Some things are congealed by heat, others by cold; and those things are congealable which do not have watery moisture, nor are they of water, but are more of hot earth, such as honey and must; and those which have water, but are more of blood, just as oil and molten silver," etc. It was easy for him, uncontradicted, to say that <term>apekta</term> <gloss>non-congealable things</gloss>, that is, concrete things, or as the chemical philosophers speak, those that are incoagulable, are those which suffer no concretion from heat or cold. But when the disposition and nature of matter must be placed beneath, he preferred to touch upon this rather than to designate the cause of non-concretion in it, since the acts of the agents are those of the patients, and <term>to energetikon</term> <gloss>the active principle</gloss> can do nothing unless the patient consents to it. He denies, therefore, that oil, quicksilver, honey, pitch, etc., congeal by heat or cold, because of the stated indisposition of the matter. But, although I do not now superstitiously examine whether heat and cold must be taken in isolation, <term>kath' hauto</term> <gloss>in itself</gloss>, and not some other formal principle, and by a certain property of nature, procuring concretion as if by the gaze of Medusa, just as Zuingerus writes concerning Orionic or Gorgonic salt; you, however, not long ago wrote about Mercury driven into the sublime, he indeed in regard to oil, you in regard to quicksilver: yet it seems a wonderful pronouncement, since it is certain that both oil and mercury, with the rest that he proposed, undergo concretion, even from cold alone. We experience this, of course, in olive oil during winter frosts. The experience regarding mercury is not as common. Yet I have seen—even in moderate cold (for these days, when I am writing this, the air has been softer than usual, to the extent that in the middle of December we have not yet seen proper snow)—the Mercury of the Philosophers, which I had in a glass geranium <note>likely referring to a specific type of glass vessel</note>, compacted into a solid tablet by cold alone, and I observed this also several times the previous year; when brought into a heated room, it immediately returned to its former liquid state. Indeed, it has also been observed that in the middle of mercurial waves there exist rays, like pyramidal crystals, which are those true crystals, and when resolved by heat alone, that perennial phosphoric water is produced; shining with a silver appearance without transparency, just as they excellently teach that there is in them that <term>dynamin</term> <gloss>power</gloss> of coagulating which the philosophers have so anxiously sought. If, therefore, such mercury congeals by cold alone, just as the Philosopher had differed on the generation of metals; and is resolved by heat; how does he write that it is non-congealable? He does not, however, use a light argument. He says that air excels in it, and that this is true, say the chemical philosophers.

# COMMENTARIES ON ALCHEMY
## OF ANDREAS LIBAVIUS, M. D.
### Part II.
#### TREATISE THREE.
#### OF THE AZOTH OF THE PHILOSOPHERS, AND OF THE PERMANENT WATER.

TO THE ONCE MOST FAMOUS PHYSICIAN, PHILOSOPHER, AND CHEMIST, Master Johannes Rungius, of pious memory, of Stettin in Pomerania, when he had sent a certain dry, shimmering mercurial powder, with the consistency of asbestos reduced to a feathery softness, under the name of Azoth and permanent water.

<margin>4. Meteor. 8.</margin>
Since you seemed to have held a knowledge of natural things explored for yourself, and therefore Aristotle attempted to include everything under universal precepts, you know, distinguished man, what he proclaims in his Meteorology περὶ пηκτῶν καὶ ἀπήκτων <gloss>concerning things that can and cannot be congealed</gloss>, namely: τὰ μὲν ὑπὸ θερμοῦ πήγνυται πῆξιν, τὰ δὲ ὑπὸ ψυχροῦ: πηκτὰ δὲ εἶναι ὅσα μὴ ἔχει ὑγρότητα ὑδατώδη, μηδ' ὕδατος ἔστιν, ἀλλὰ πλέον θερμοῦ γῆς, οἷον, μέλι καὶ гλεύκος: καὶ ὅσα ὕδατος μὲν ἔχει ἔστι δὲ πλεῖον αἵματος, ὥσπερ τὸ ἔλαιον καὶ ἄργυρος χυτός <gloss>things are congealed by heat, others by cold; things that can be congealed are those which do not have watery moisture, nor are they of water, but rather more of terrestrial heat, such as honey and must; and those which have water, but more of blood, such as oil and molten silver</gloss>, etc. It was easy for him to say in reverse that ἄπηκτα <gloss>un-congealable things</gloss>, that is, concrete-like, or as the Philosophical Chemists say, non-coagulable, are those which suffer no concretion from heat or cold. But since the disposition and nature of matter must be laid as a foundation, he preferred to touch upon this, and therein to designate the cause of not congealing, since the actions of agents belong to the patients, and τὸ ἐνεργητικόν <gloss>the active principle</gloss> cannot do anything unless the patient concurs with it. He denies, therefore, that oil, quicksilver, honey, pitch, etc., congeal by heat or cold, because of the said indisposition of matter. But although I do not now superstitiously inquire whether heat and cold are to be taken in isolation, καθ' αὑτὸ <gloss>by themselves</gloss>, and not some other formal principle, and by a certain property of nature, as if procuring concretion by the aspect of Medusa, just as Zuingerus writes concerning the Orionian or Gorgonian salt; you, however, not long ago concerning Mercury brought on high, he indeed by reason of oil, you of quicksilver: yet it seems a marvelous pronouncement, since it is established that both oil and mercury, along with the others he proposed, undergo concretion, even from cold alone. We experience this, of course, in olive oil during winter frosts. Regarding mercury, the experience is not so common. I have seen, however, even in moderate cold (for in these days during which I write this, the air was softer than usual, so much so that in the middle of December we have not yet seen proper snow), the Mercury of the Philosophers, which in a glass vessel was compacted into a solid tablet by cold alone, and I also noticed this several times last year, which, when brought into a heated room, immediately returned to its former liquid state. Indeed, it has been observed that in the middle of the mercurial waves there exist rays, like pyramidal crystals, which are those true crystals, and by which, when dissolved by heat alone, that phosphoric permanent water is made; shining with a silvery appearance without transparency, so that they excellently teach that in themselves is that δύναμιν <gloss>power</gloss> of coagulating, which the Philosophers have sought so anxiously. If, therefore, such mercury congeals by cold alone, just as the Philosopher had also argued concerning the generation of metals: and is resolved by heat; how is it that he writes it is un-congealable? Yet he does not use a light argument. He says that air excels in it, and that this is true, say the Chemical Philosophers.

You will urge: That which will be entirely past according to itself can be called future; but eternal duration from the after-part, and the individuals that are produced successively in that duration, will not be adequately past at any distance of time; therefore they cannot be called simply future. I respond: It is no less of the definition of the future to be sometimes present, than to be sometimes past; indeed Aristotle, as is evident from Logic, says that the past and future are cases of the present tense in verbs, and both he and other philosophers explain the matter through the future and the past in relation to the present as to the primary <term>analogatum</term> <gloss>the primary term of comparison</gloss>, and yet for something to be called future, it is sufficient if any part of it taken separately is sometimes present; therefore it also suffices if any part of it considered separately is sometimes past, even though as a whole it may never be adequately past.
