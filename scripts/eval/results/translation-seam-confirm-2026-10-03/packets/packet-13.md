# Meanings of the Treatise on the Hundred Dharmas

Because of these four great elements and false forms, the original wisdom-light is transformed into false perceptions. By taking these false forms as the object of perception, and through long-held false views, one grasps a small portion of the four great elements to be a "self," and the false perception relies on these four great elements to be a "body." Thus, the four great elements are originally without knowledge, but because of the clinging of false perception, they appear to have knowledge. The true mind is infinite, but now it is sealed and solidified by ignorance, submerged into the four great elements to function as a mind. This is what is called the body formed by the intermingling of forms and false thoughts. Therefore, it is said that "knowledge and perception are sentient beings," which refers to the sentient beings of the five aggregates. Therefore, the inner five roots and outer six dusts all belong to the "object-aspect" of the eight consciousnesses. Consequently, in the practice of Zen, one must first strip away the body and mind internally and abandon the world externally; the goal is to extinguish these two aspects of subject and object, and to investigate the fundamental substance of the eight consciousnesses and ignorance. If the body, mind, and world are not extinguished, they are always barriers to life and death. Regarding what is called the two执 <term>drsti</term> <gloss>attachments/views</gloss> of self and dharma: clinging to the body as "self" is the attachment to self, and clinging to the roots and dusts is the attachment to dharma. Practitioners of the Two Vehicles <note>the Hearer and Solitary Realizer vehicles</note> only break the view of self to exit the life and death of sections <note>ordinary transmigratory life</note>. The attachment to dharma, starting from the initial faith through the three stages of the virtuous to the first stage of the bodhisattva, is only then broken. This is not easily done.

The twenty-four types of "non-associated" <term>citta-viprayukta-samskara</term> <gloss>dharmas not associated with the mind</gloss> are divisions of form and mind. They are established nominally based on the previous three categories of dharmas, such as "acquisition." They are distinguished as being different from the mind, mental factors, and forms, and thus are called "non-associated." Because they do not associate with the mind-king and cannot perform good or evil, they are not mental factors; they are merely differences in the divisions of positions accounted for by consciousness-only. Because they are the dharmas to which my attachment clings, they are also listed among the conditioned dharmas. There are many interpretations, but this is not urgent and therefore not discussed in detail, lest it hinder the main practice.

---

The ninety-four types of dharmas above are called "conditioned dharmas" because they are the dharmas of sentient beings' life and death, and are constructed by false consciousness. Thus, they are called conditioned and worldly dharmas. The sixteen unconditioned dharmas have six types: space unconditioned, cessation through selection unconditioned, cessation not through selection unconditioned, immobility unconditioned, cessation of thought and feeling unconditioned, and suchness unconditioned. These six types are established as "unconditioned" to distinguish them from the conditioned. Although they are called supramundane dharmas, they actually extend to the Two Vehicles. "Immobility" refers to the third fruit, the Anagamin, and "cessation of thought and feeling" refers to the extinction concentration. "Space unconditioned" derives its name from a metaphor, meaning the substance of the unconditioned dharma is like empty space, without any construction or creation. The following five unconditioned dharmas are explained using this metaphor. However, this metaphor of space has different degrees of magnitude. As the *Flower Ornament Sutra* says, "If a person wishes to know the Buddha-realm, they must purify their mind like space, far removed from false thoughts and all attachments, making the mind's direction unobstructed." It also says, "The pure dharma-body is like space." This points directly to the emptiness of the nature of the dharma-realm. This is the true emptiness of the great vehicle's dharma-nature, which is the absolute purity of the one mind, and thus is like space. The meaning of space here applies to both the great and small vehicles, but primarily takes the meaning of being empty and unobstructed, without construction, as a metaphor for the following five unconditioned dharmas. ### XXXVIII.

A demonstration <term>propter quid</term> <gloss>of the reason why</gloss> can proceed from every type of cause; principally, however, from the formal cause; and hence, what is commonly said by some, that the most potent middle term of a demonstration is the definition of the subject itself, you should understand as the formal one.

### XXXIX.

Nor do I think it should be denied that it can be proven, and that demonstratively, that such a formal definition agrees with its subject; but not as the definition of the subject itself; nor even through a formal cause; but either from the matter, or from the end, or from the efficient cause of the same; for of a formal definition, there is not given another definition in the same genus.

Among Zoroaster and Hermes, indeed, that One is also called the "monad." To us, however, for the sake of clearer doctrine, it has seemed better to call that One "unity." It is "the One," because it is only one and independent. "Unity," however, both depends upon it and looks toward the multitude; in the "One-all," the multitude is indeed unified and most simple. In unity, however, the multitude is now distinct and poured out into number. The One has snatched itself above every relation and transcends every respect of multitude. Unity, however, looks toward number and is referred to the multitude. Therefore, this primary unity is from the First One. The multitude, however, which is in this unity, comes from that, so to speak, "all-ness." But for what reason did that One-all bring forth this unity-all from itself? Does the question therefore return to the same point? Not at all. But in it, rest is moved, and motion rests. And that which is moved in him is moved stably from him, and that which rests rests movably. Namely, it remains itself. ## IX.

The liberal man should strive to observe the mean in receiving no less than in giving. Therefore, let him not receive from those from whom he ought not, nor where he ought not to receive at all. Let him not be importunate or troublesome to others in asking and receiving, lest he be called <term>αἰτητικός</term> <gloss>a beggar or mendicant</gloss> by others. For we have affirmed above, not without foundation, that the liberal man should be occupied more with giving than with receiving. Furthermore, he who is about to perform a benefit should receive from his own resources, nor should he pour out the liquid of liberality by channeling the sources of others toward himself. *For, as Seneca writes in his first book on Clemency, he is not liberal who is liberal with what belongs to another, but he who takes from his own store what he gives to another.* Meanwhile, the liberal man should also not neglect his own household affairs, but should strive to promote them by honest and lawful means, at least to the end that he may possess his own goods, by which he can provide sustenance for his own and, when necessity presses, assist the indigence of others. Indeed, we are able to benefit others only if we have the means ourselves. See the *Cursus Philosophicus* of the Master, Table 8, Axiom 2.

## X.

As regards the extremes of liberality, it has Prodigality as its opposite in excess, and Avarice in defect. The former, named from squandering <note>Latin: "a prodigendo"</note>, is a vice by which we exceed the measure in giving and fail to reach the measure in receiving, with no regard for the circumstances that were suggested in the preceding theses. Prodigality is usually established as twofold: *Simple and mixed.* The former squanders resources recklessly on any objects whatsoever, with all hope of repayment abandoned. This kind falls especially upon the youthful age. *Mixed* prodigality is implicated with many other vices; it bestows upon some, while taking away from others through injury, whence such a prodigal is also unjust and stained with other vices.
