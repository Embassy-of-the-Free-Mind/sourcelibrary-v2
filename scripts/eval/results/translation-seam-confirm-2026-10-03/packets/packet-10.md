# THEOPHRASTUS, ON ODORS

<note>A large, ornate, historiated drop-cap letter 'A' featuring floral and vine-like scrollwork.</note>

Odors, in their entirety, arise from a mixture, just as flavors do; for everything that is unmixed is odorless, just as it is flavorless. Thus, many things are odorless, such as water, air, and fire. Earth, however, possesses odor more than any other element; for this reason, it is the most mixed. Among odors, some are, as it were, unpleasant and watery, just as they are in the case of flavors; others possess certain specific forms. These forms seem to correspond to those of flavors, though they do not all have the same designations, as we stated previously. Nor are they distinguished by their species in the same way as flavors are; rather, they are classified by their nature, as either sweet-smelling or foul-smelling. The varieties of foul odors have not been named, even though they possess great differences. For, since there are odors of sweet and bitter things, others are called pungent and strong, and a sweet odor is soft and heavy. Some of these are common to both, as well as to foul odors. The universal odor that exists in all things that are decaying is putridity. For everything that is putrefying is foul-smelling, unless one calls sharpness a primitive state due to the similarity of decay. This kind of foulness exists in all things, even in plants. # THEOPHRASTUS, ON ODORS

<note>A large, ornate, historiated drop-cap letter 'A' featuring floral and vine-like scrollwork.</note>

Odors, as a whole, arise from mixture, just as flavors do; for anything that is unmixed is odorless, just as it is flavorless. This is why most things are odorless, such as water, air, and fire. Earth, however, is almost the only thing that possesses an odor, which is why it is highly mixed. Among odors, some are, as it were, unpleasant and watery, just as with flavors; others possess certain specific forms. These forms appear to follow those of flavors, yet not all of them possess the same designations, as we stated previously. Nor are they altogether defined by these categories in the same way as flavors are; rather, they are distinguished as they are by nature, in that some things are fragrant and others are foul-smelling. As for the <term>iodes</term> <gloss>violet-like</gloss> and foul-smelling odors, these types are no longer distinctly named, even though they possess great differences. For since, among sweet and bitter things, some are called pungent and strong, there is also a soft and heavy odor that is sweet; some of these are shared with foul odors. Generally, there is a certain <term>saprotēs</term> <gloss>rot/putrefaction</gloss> common to all things undergoing decay. For everything that is putrefying is foul-smelling, unless one calls this sharpness a sort of priority, by the similarity of the decay.
