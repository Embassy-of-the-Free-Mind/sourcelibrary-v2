# COMMENTARIES ON ALCHEMY
## OF ANDREAS LIBAVIUS, M. D.
### Part II.
#### THIRD TREATISE.
#### OF THE AZOTH OF THE PHILOSOPHERS AND THE PERMANENT WATER.

TO THE ONCE MOST CELEBRATED PHYSICIAN, PHILOSOPHER, AND CHEMIST, D. Johann Rungius, of pious memory, of Stettin in Pomerania, when he had sent a certain dry, shimmering mercurial powder, with the consistency of asbestos reduced to a feathery softness, under the name of Azoth and the permanent water.

<margin>4. Metaphysics, 8.</margin>
Since you seemed to have held a knowledge of natural things explored for yourself, and therefore Aristotle attempted to include everything under universal precepts, you know, distinguished man, what he pronounces in his Meteorology <note>original: "περὶ пηκτῶν καὶ ἀπήκτῶν" meaning "concerning things that congeal and do not congeal"</note>, namely: "Some things are congealed by heat, others by cold: and those things are congealable which do not have watery moisture, and are not of water, but are more of hot earth, such as honey and must; and those which have some water, but are more of blood, such as oil and molten silver," etc. It was easy for him, conversely, to say that <term>apēkta</term> <gloss>things that do not congeal</gloss>, that is, concrete-like, or as the chemical philosophers say, the incoagulable, are those which suffer no concretion from heat or cold; but when the disposition and nature of matter is to be laid down, he preferred to touch upon this, and in it to designate the cause of not congealing, since the actions of agents are of those things which suffer, nor can the <term>to energētikon</term> <gloss>the active principle</gloss> do anything unless the patient assents to it. He therefore denies that oil, quicksilver, honey, pitch, etc., congeal by heat or cold, because of the said indisposition of the matter. But, although I do not now superstitiously examine whether heat and cold are to be accepted in isolation, <term>kath' hauto</term> <gloss>per se</gloss>, and not some other formal principle, and by some property of nature, procuring concretion like the gaze of Medusa, just as Zuingerus says of the Orionian or Gorgonian salt; you, however, not long ago regarding Mercury brought to the sublime, he indeed by reason of oil, you of quicksilver, you report: yet that pronouncement seems wonderful, since it is certain that both oil and hydrargyrum, with the others he proposed, sustain concretion, even by cold alone. We experience this, of course, in oil expressed from olives during winter frosts. Regarding hydrargyrum, the experience is not so common. I have, however, seen even in moderate cold (for in these days when I write this, the air was softer than usual, so that in the middle of December we have not yet seen proper snow) the Mercury of the Philosophers, which I had in a glass geranium, compacted into a solid tablet by cold alone, and I observed this also several times the previous year, which, when brought into a heated room, immediately returned to its former liquid state: Furthermore, it has been observed that within the mercurial waves there exist rays, like pyramidal crystals, which are those true crystals, and by which, when dissolved by heat alone, that permanent phosphoric water is fashioned; shining with a silver appearance without transparency, so that they teach excellently that there is in them that <term>dynamis</term> <gloss>power</gloss> of coagulating which the philosophers have sought so anxiously. If, therefore, such hydrargyrum congeals by cold alone, just as the Philosopher had also differed regarding the generation of metals; and is resolved by heat; how is it that he writes it is incoagulable? He does not, however, use a light argument. He says that air excels in it, and that this is true, say the chemical philosophers.

==== TRANSLATION (this page) ====
They say, however, that even those things which their maritime power carries with it—not less than clemency and goodness—have taken up arms for the sake of ecclesiastical and Etruscan liberty, in order to restore to every people their own rights. Or, even if they accomplish their pious intention with great slaughter, they will perceive all the greater pleasure from their good deeds the less they have disturbed the state of Etruria in the process. For this reason, therefore, they seek your alliance, so that they may preserve your liberty without any harm and restore to you what has been lost, extending your borders. They see that you, joined to the Florentines, would suffer great losses from the war before any time for gain or self-perception arrives. For in the heat of wars, since the causes of individuals are not clearly discussed, it is often the case that what the method of victory suggests is considered, not what each one deserves. I say that the two earthly luminaries of the Christians seek nothing else from you than that you should share with them the fruit of victory without any detriment of war. For if you believe that the weight of such a great war was undertaken in the hope of your aid, you are greatly deceived, and you do not sufficiently understand the wisdom of the King and the Pope. They bore much so as not to see war in Italy; they tried in vain everything by which it was probable that the fury of the tyrant, Lorenzo de' Medici, would be moderated. They proceeded to cut away the incurable wound with the sword, lest the healthy part be infected, when they already clearly saw the outcome of the war and that Mars—almost always common to them—was with them. They could not be torn away from the embrace of peace, not by the hope of gains, which they always despised, nor by the vengeance of injuries, nor by the desire for glory, nor for any cause or reason whatsoever, and [they are men] who in spirit and strength were not only superior to Lorenzo, who was recently raised to tyranny by trade and the mockery of fortune, but [would] not even [be] inferior to the powers of the whole world. Yet they did not undertake the war before they were provoked by the most just and necessary complaints of allies and friends, and they looked forward to the end of the matter from afar, as if from a watchtower, having examined the strength of their own side and the enemy’s regarding the hope of victory. If there had been any doubt about the victory, they would surely have foreseen it by their own intelligence and by that prudence in affairs by which they overcome both those of the past and those of the present.
