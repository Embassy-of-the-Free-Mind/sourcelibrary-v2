Book 5 of the Ethics, 8. And this is perhaps because the crime committed is not the same in that instance. A magistrate striking a private citizen possesses a certain superiority over him, which a private citizen—who, by striking a magistrate, withdraws the respect due to him—lacks, thereby aggravating his own offense. If, however, a simple retaliation were valid here, then a simple repercussion would suffice, whereby the private citizen would be struck in return just as he struck the magistrate, which is far from the truth. Indeed, one must suffer not only according to the measure of the crime but also in consideration of the dignity of the magistrate, and therefore be afflicted with a greater punishment. We see that Aulus Gellius expressed this by proposing a threefold purpose for penalties when he says: *The first is when a penalty is applied for the sake of correcting and improving, so that he who has committed a fault by chance may become more attentive and upright; whence it is called* <term>nouthesia</term> <gloss>admonition</gloss> *or* <term>kolasis</term> <gloss>correction/chastisement</gloss>. *The second is when the dignity and authority of the person against whom the sin was committed must be protected, lest the failure to take notice produce contempt for him and diminish his honor; whence it is called* <term>timōria</term> <gloss>retribution/punishment for the preservation of honor</gloss>. *The third is called* <term>paradeigma</term> <gloss>example/demonstration</gloss>, *when punishment is necessary for the sake of an example, so that others may be deterred from similar sins, which it is in the public interest to prohibit, by knowing of the punishment.* Book 6, Attic Nights, chapter 14.
