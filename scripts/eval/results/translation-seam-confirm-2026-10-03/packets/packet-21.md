## TH. IX.

Through excessive sleep and a sedentary life, the ventilation of the blood and insensible perspiration are hindered. Hence, heterogeneous parts, which are otherwise wont to be drawn out through insensible perspiration, remain mixed with the blood mass. From this, obstructions arise, and the blood is not a little infected, from which a sharp intemperance subsequently grows. While movement and waking hours exceed their limits, the spirits are impoverished. The more spirituous parts fly away through the pores of the skin, the provided gate, while only the thicker parts, which are less suited for movement, remain. While that which ought to be excreted is retained, and conversely that which ought to be retained is excreted, it is an evil. There is, however, more danger in the retention of things that should be excreted than in the retention of things that should be retained. That the passions of the mind, when they exceed, bring various alterations to the fluids and have much efficacy in producing this effect, no one will deny. There is, therefore, no need for me to speak at greater length about them, since their manner of working and afflicting is already known to the common people.

Therefore, the forms and qualities ought to be simultaneous, and thus all will be in act in the mixture. Seventh, one simple form cannot be an instrument for two forms entirely in species, but each part of the mixture is an instrument of the form of the mixture. Therefore, not of the elementary forms. Eighth, Aristotle shows in the second book of *On Generation* that there are four bodies in the world, because there are four combinations of the poles. Then I argue: in the mixture there are four pole-conjugations. Therefore, the four elements are there in act; otherwise, the reasoning of Aristotle is null, and especially since Aristotle there says that water is the coagulation of cold and moist, and fire is the burning of hot and dry. But in the mixture are the goods of this; therefore, they are finite. Plato also says that man is a <term>microcosmon</term> <gloss>little world</gloss>, that in him four elements are observed, and four qualities, which Plotinus also affirms. For either the elements are in the mixture, because the forms and qualities of the mixture can exercise the effect, and then <note>they are</note> theirs; or because there are in the mixture, not indeed qualities of the same species as the qualities of the elements, but because of powers of another kind, distinct from the forms and qualities of the mixture. First, let us destroy the first part, and we will refute the second. If therefore it is (as he says nearby), then the heaven would be a mixture of primary qualities and of the forms of the four bodies, which Aristotle proves to be false in the book *On the Heavens*. And he brings an enthymeme: for the light of the sun can exercise all the works of the elements, as Averroes demonstrates in the second book of *On the Heavens*, second part, 42. It exercises, moreover, dryness in autumn, coldness in winter, moisture in spring, heat in summer. Therefore, the four qualities are contained in the light by power. Therefore, light would be the complexion of the sun, just as the quality of the mixture is the complexion of the mixture. Furthermore, the sun produces fire by its presence, water by its absence, and the intermediate elements by its intermediate disposition. Therefore, the sun contains the four elements, both because of a similar action and because of its nobility. Therefore, because of its substance, it will contain elementary forms, and because of its light, qualities. Therefore, it is a tempered mixture, just as a generable mixture. Again, then gold would be mixed from metals; for gold, because of its nobility and because of convenience in working, contains all metals, as the alchemists say. Therefore, there will not be only four simple bodies. Moreover, then the rational soul would be a mixture of the forms of the four elements and the natural soul, both because it can use their works and because it is nobler. These things, however, we have not yet seen in any of the books of Aristotle. Now let us argue against the second member. First, because if they are alone in the mixture, then they are not qualities, nor forms; then they will not be able to come into operative act from their own sort. Moreover, as a thing relates to being, so it relates to working; but a being in power is not a perfect being. Therefore, it cannot act. Again, the moving species always estimates, as he hands down in the ninth book of philosophy and the third of physical listening; since therefore the elements are not there in act, nor can they act, unless we recur to the form of the mixture, and thus it will be the first member. But because this position (however one understands it) is against Aristotle, I argue with his words. He himself says at the end of the first book of *On Generation*, concluding <column-break/> what mixture is: "mixture is the union of things mixed, which have been altered." Nor does it avail to say (as some assert), "The Philosopher of altered things" or "have been altered," assuming it is past tense. For that word "of mixed things" says what is actual: that there is a mixture of those things that have altered themselves, as the text says. But neither powers nor the ability have altered, because it is not a mixture. Furthermore, he says in the same place: "When a drop of wine is not mixed in a thousand thousand amphorae of water, it is dissolved; for the species is transmuted into the whole water." Therefore, that which is mixable must remain somewhat in act. Again, he shows which are easy by division, of which the humid are examples. If, therefore, the mixables were only in the mixture by power or potentiality, why would he labor so much in showing what is easy and difficult to mix? Averroes moves this question in the third book of *On the Heavens and the World*, commentary 47, up to the words: "And it must be asked why the forms of the elements do not remain in act regarding the powers, namely in mixtures, carrying other forms, namely of the mixtures, which they receive." Therefore, it is their question why elementary forms, received under perfect being, are not dispositions of matter to receive other forms of mixtures, and he argues that it ought to be. It is said, when you have already posited that they are not of matter for some forms except for their own forms, that if it is argued thus: the forms by which matters are properly made proper matters for mixtures are dispositions, by the mediation of which they receive in matter the forms of mixtures. But elementary forms are those by which matter is properly made proper for the mixture. Therefore, by the mediation of elementary forms, they receive the forms of the mixtures. And thus, elementary forms will be in act in all mixtures. Then he solves the question, and first he proves that elementary forms under perfect being do not receive <note>forms</note> in matter, so that by their mediation they receive the forms of the mixtures, and he says: "Let us say therefore that if it were as we said, that namely they received under perfect being, it would be necessary that no being be generated from them; that is, that no being would receive in matter by their mediation that would be different in substantial forms, but yet it would be an accident." This is said: if elementary forms were first received in prime matter, then everything coming to matter and received in it by their mediation would be an accident. He does not deduce the enthymeme here, but in many places of *Physics* and *First Philosophy* and in the booklet *On the Substance of the World* he has deduced it. For everything coming to a being in perfect act and specific act is an accident. This is the difference between accidental and substantial forms, as he declared the same in the second book of *On the Soul*. Second, principally, he proves that elementary forms are corrupted, that is, remitted. To this end, so that matter by their mediation might receive the forms of mixtures, and he says: "And therefore it is necessary, when one form is generated from them, that their forms be corrupted according to mediation, that is, according to part." This is said: that if matter by their mediation ought to receive the form of the mixture, they are remitted according to their substances somewhat in part. Then third, he proves that they are not corrupted from every part, so that naked matter receives the forms of mixtures, and he says: "Since if they were corrupted according to the whole, then prime matter would receive all forms essentially by itself."

Second, it would follow that it would not receive the forms of mixtures by the mediation of these bodies. Averroes immediately moves a question against himself and says: "If therefore anyone should say it follows from this that the substantial forms of the elements receive more and less, which is false." For this disposition is of accidents, not of substantial forms. And he argues, because everything is in many places, as in the *Categories*: that substantial forms do not receive more and less. From these two, that statement appears false. He answers and says, first, that the substantial forms of the elements are diminutive with respect to perfect substantial forms. Second, he says that their being is a medium between substantial forms and accidents. From these he infers third.

RABBI MOSES.

what would the precepts and statutes, or even the studies, bring in the way of utility? From which it would come about that fools would overthrow the Law of Moses of blessed memory: not to mention the reason for refuting their opinion, together with all those arguments which are brought by the philosophers to refute what is said by the <term>Chaldeans</term> <gloss>Babylonian astrologers</gloss> and their followers. In the same way, the words of the philosophers who say that things of this kind happen rashly and by chance are established by us as false, because of the tradition of the Law and its ways, upon which we rely, and which we embrace, and in whose ways we walk; which is that which we say, that this Reuben or Simon is in no way compelled to be a perfumer and wealthy, and this <column-break/> Rabbi Eleazar responds to the former, that this is not an argument by which one could escape and avoid God's punishment. Since although a planet sometimes incites a man to something, he can nevertheless free and vindicate himself from that which has the power of driving and inciting: just as that wise man, and one who was pious above all, says: I sustained great labors to correct my own propensity born from natural causes. For the natural evil inclination <term>concupiscentia</term> <gloss>lust/desire</gloss> vehemently incites me. etc.
