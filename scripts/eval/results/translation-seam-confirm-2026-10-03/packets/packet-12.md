### ASTRONOMICAL THESES.

<note>A decorative woodcut initial 'D' depicts a scholar seated at a desk within a landscape setting.</note>

By the common suffrage of the learned, it emerges from the mode of consideration that the subject of a science is rendered adequate, and one science is distinguished from others; however, sciences that receive a common denomination must also have a common mode of consideration.

1° Since Astronomy, by the calculation of all who think correctly, is called Mathematical, being subalternated to Geometry, it cannot have any mode of consideration other than mathematical, just as it cannot have any subject other than the one considered.

2 Therefore, we maintain that motion around the center as such must be excluded from proper Astronomical contemplation; and we affirm that whatever is said about it by the Astronomer is entirely borrowed from the Physicist.

3 Therefore, in our judgment, nothing can be said more thoughtlessly than to assert that a celestial body moving around the center as such, or motion as it is measurable, or finally magnitude as it is movable around the center, is the subject of Astronomy; since those who speak thus commit a manifest error either in the thing considered, or in the mode of consideration, or in both.

4 Furthermore, looking more deeply into the condition and nature of a subalternated science, let us say that the subject of Astronomy is magnitude movable around the center, as it is magnitude.

5 Therefore, motion will be considered only as a sensible accident added to the thing considered; meanwhile, we think the entire proper consideration of Astronomy refers to the knowledge of quantity alone.

6 The Astronomer will therefore consider motion, magnitude, and figure, which are affections and boundaries of natural bodies, but only as certain mathematical ratios (such as the ratio of greater to lesser, equal to unequal, distant to near, proportionality, and the like) are found in them; whereas the Physicist refers everything to the nature from which they are understood to flow.

7 So it is not a wonder that, because of this diversity, the Philosopher sometimes (as in 2 Physics, ch. 2) said that Astrology abstracts from motion, but sometimes (1 Metaphysics, text 19) that it considers with motion, or indeed even considers the motion itself.

The air, according to the diverse ratio of its qualities, was once divided by the ancients into two parts, namely the lower and the upper, of which the latter was called hot and the former cold. At this time, however, by almost all philosophers, in accordance with the common custom of the schools and for the sake of greater convenience and ease, it is distributed into three regions: the highest, the middle, and the lowest.

### LVIIII.

The highest region of the air, which is placed immediately beneath the sphere of fire, is hot and dry on account of the proximity of fire and the perpetual motion of the celestial bodies, with which it is also driven around from east to west by the swiftest and most rapid motion of the <term>primum mobile</term> <gloss>the first moved sphere in the Ptolemaic system</gloss>.

### LX.

The middle region is cold and humid, because it is not thinned by celestial motion, but the motion weakens here, nor do reflected solar rays reach it; however, the heat surrounding it on all sides causes it, on account of the cold vapors included in this region of the air, to be colder, which coldness is also sometimes intensified by <term>antiperistasis</term> <gloss>the intensification of a quality by the pressure of its contrary quality surrounding it</gloss>, that is, by the inhibition or compression of the quality by the contrary quality surrounding it.

### TH. XI.

Once the wound is dressed, the patient should be placed in bed, with the head slightly elevated, and he should rest until he complains of the pressure of the fluid; then he should be redressed, with the body placed in the same position in which it was at the time of the incision, so that the wounds of the parts correspond and the fluid flows out. Once all obstacles are removed, the lungs should be pressed down, the humor evacuated, and finally, the wound should be bandaged again. Take care, however, not to evacuate it all, since with it many spirits indeed depart, and the patient is overly weakened. While the wound is being bandaged, do not let air enter as much as possible, for <term>gangrene</term> <gloss>tissue death</gloss> seizes the parts, a case of which <term>C. Solingen</term> saw in *Manual of Surgical Operations, part 2, chapter 1*, and for this reason, it should be changed without a bright fire. While we draw out the fluid daily, it often happens that we cannot manage its exit either with an instrument or a finger, either because by chance the lungs have adhered to the <term>pleura</term> <gloss>membrane lining the chest cavity</gloss>, or because the humor is thickening: in these cases, the lungs should be separated with the finger when they cohere to the pleura at least with the help of tiny fibers, and there is no fear of rupturing major vessels. The thickness of the humor should be diluted with tepid injections prepared from <term>barley water</term> and <term>rose honey</term>, taking care that at the beginning they do not become bitter from the addition of <term>aloe</term>, for the patient rejects them with his mouth; nevertheless, they are very conducive to consolidating the vessels. The injections, once performed, should be drawn out again on each occasion, which should be done twice, thrice, or four times a day if the fluid has become watery and presses too much. The flow of the humor should be promoted until nothing further flows, except what the wound itself provides, which sometimes does not happen before the second or third month.
