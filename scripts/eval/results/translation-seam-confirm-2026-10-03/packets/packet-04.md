陳氏朱人書目說山海經曰漢侍中奉車都尉臣秀所校
祕書秀即劉歆也晉郭璞注案唐志二十三卷音二卷今
本錫山尤袤延之校定世傳禹益所作其事見吳越春秋
曰禹東巡登南嶽得金簡玉字通水之理遂行四瀆與益
共謀所至使益疏而記之名山海經此其爲說恢誕不典
司馬遷曰言九州山川尚書近之矣至禹本紀山海經所
書怪物余不敢言之也可謂名言孰曰多愛乎故尤跋明
其非禹伯翳所作而以爲先秦古書無疑然莫能明其何
人也洪慶善補注楚辭引山海經淮南子以釋天問而晦

...the truth, exhausting the subtle emotions. How profound! How profound! This book spans three thousand years across generations. Although it briefly appeared during the Han dynasty, it was soon neglected and forgotten. The names and locations of its mountains and rivers became full of errors, differing from those of the present day. Without teachers to pass down the knowledge, it faded into obscurity. Where the Way resides, the common customs often lose it. How sad. I have felt deep apprehension regarding this, and thus have initiated this commentary to clear the blockages and prune the overgrowth. I have grasped its singular qualities and highlighted its profound reach, hoping that the lost texts do not vanish from the world, and that the marvelous accounts do not cease for those living today. So that the traces of the Great Yu might not be erased in the future, and the affairs of the eight desolate regions might be heard by descendants—is this not a worthy pursuit? Without the swirling flight of the three-legged bird, one cannot understand the heavens. Without the traversing of deep waters and shallow pools, one cannot know the soaring of the crimson dragon. The courts of the heavenly music are not places that mere performers can tread. The fords without ships are not places that the azure rhinoceros can cross. If one does not possess the utmost understanding of the world, it is difficult to discuss the meaning of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*. Alas, may the learned readers with broad perspectives take heed of this.
