29. For it would be entirely unreasonable if he knew Egyptian Thebes accurately, which is slightly less than four thousand stadia <note>Gosselin reads four thousand, emending from five thousand; Groskurd and Forbiger follow, and C. Müller approves.</note> from our sea <note>The Mediterranean Sea.</note>, yet was ignorant of the recess of the Arabian Gulf <note>The Red Sea.</note> and the isthmus near it, which has a width of no more than one thousand stadia. It would seem far more unreasonable still if he knew that the Nile was called by the same name as that vast country, but failed to perceive the reason for this.

<margin>C 36</margin>
For the saying of Herodotus would especially apply here, that the country was a gift of the river, and on this account was deemed worthy of the same name. Moreover, among the distinctive features belonging to each region, the best known are those that possess something extraordinary and are conspicuous to everyone. Such are both the rising of the Nile and the silting up of the sea. And just as those who are brought to Egypt inquire first about nothing concerning the land before they ask about the nature of the Nile, because the inhabitants have nothing newer to tell foreign visitors, nor anything more remarkable regarding their own country (for to the one who has inquired about the river, the whole country also becomes clear as to what sort of land it is), so too those who hear of it from afar inquire into this first. Add to this, then, both the poet's love of knowledge and his fondness for travel abroad, which all who record his life testify to; and many examples of such traits are taken from the poems themselves. He, then, is proved on multiple grounds both to have known this and to state it explicitly

<summary>Strabo argues that Homer must have known the geography of the Arabian Gulf and the Nile's isthmus if he knew distant Thebes, emphasizing that the Nile's unique nature and seasonal flooding were famous subjects known to all learned travelers.</summary>
