Let Venus be content with herself: let her threaten haughtily,
While we, humble and mild, endure: let the victrix bear standards
Dripping with gore, our triumphs being without slaughter,
Or am I worn out because I have not fought against nature?
If I was alluring to Anchises, did I belie my sex?
If I gave birth, to whom was I harmful? My Cupid inhabits the stars,
Your Aeneas dwells in Phrygia. Is this a fault?
Does Venus devise this destruction for the gods above and the world?
To these I give men, to these I give gods. Therefore let the unwed Pallas
Thus censure the ways of deserving Venus. Is she pleasing,
Pleasing to men whom she devours in war? Is she to be appeased by girls,
Whose sex she disdains? Is she worthy of serene gods,
Whom she calls sluggish and fearful? Thus she vents against all,
Thus she will please with her weapons, thus she is fair to all.
Yet she is a virgin: Aglauros denies it, the concealed serpent denies it;
I hold my peace. Yet she is strong in beauty: the questioned wave
And the swelling of her cheek cry out against it. Yet she is vigorous once swords are taken up:
She has nothing genuine to show, her feigned power
Brings joy for a short time, disgrace for a long time: after a few fair skies,
Her counterfeit glory grows filthy in perpetual grief.
Once there was sure trust, the imperious wars of the gods,
Not accomplished by a single hand, and the laurels were said to be given
To all; but the victorious shade of the Persean Gorgon
Extorted the titles, and the palm of unwarring Olympus.
Thus Pallas remembers herself, thus one must believe.
Believe it, Paris, a matter worthy of belief, and to be recorded in the annals.
She could do more than Jove, more than Phoebus; a woman could do more than Mars,
And would that she could do more than my Mars: for I do not envy you
Your descendants, Juno, if they are worthy nevertheless: nor is it shameful
To count Minerva as an allied race. Dione <note>Venus</note>, relying on so many
Avenging champions, could at least, if the fates had allowed,
Not be the parent of serpents, and could snatch away the footsteps
Of the wretched exile through the city in an unfolded circle.
Hermione of Mars, I myself saw, I saw nefariously
Slaughtered the people she bore. Having spoken thus, she bends down
Her tearful face, and having flared up again she speaks out:
Hurt as I am, I do not complain about the fates, the envy
Of the gods is to be feared. Semele had conceived under a favorable star,
Full of the god, destined to bring forth a god, and the tenth month
Was swelling, when Juno, feigning a severe expression,
Approaches, persuades, and departs: why did the gullible, simple,
Ignorant woman trust, pressed by Jove bound by oath? Thus your faithfulness,
Cadmus, perfected by long journeys, thus a year of wandering
Earned its reward, when, your sister having been seized, and while you prepared
To ratify Juno widowed for Jove, behold, she rewards you
With griefs, and takes up arms with flames against your children.
Let her rejoice, behold with what triumph the wrinkled goddess
Contrives titles, while she wages war with a trembling spear,
Having feigned the fidelity of a nurse: for to invent the rest
Cost no labor, gray hair comes easily to the temples,
Wrinkles come to the face. Old age was to be deceived,
Not fabricated. O if that mighty Beroe, to be joined with Dione <note>Venus</note>,
Stood at the contests of beauty, the mocking ape would stir up loud laughter.
Foolish Agenor, why do you seek Europa when Jove is your son-in-law?
She deserved to be seized, poverty excuses the abductor,
And love of a better bed. To herself she owes the most,
Because so often the wife of Jove is widowed: with a favorable bird <note>favorable omen</note>
She would have married, and to a stable bed, if the first treaties

Had brought more beauty, and less tongue. Let the shameless, base,
Chattering woman attack the conduct of a lawful lover.
Yet she is both sister and wife of Jove: well it turned out in one,
The sister redeems the offense of the wife, she reigns on high:
Yet upon the throne of her father: and she enjoys the bridal stars,
By the merit of lineage, not of the bed. Golden once was
That old man from whom I am said to be born, he was the only heir:
Nor yet did the world waver toward the fate-casting urn,
When the pious offspring of Saturn, to be equaled to Minerva,
Or at least was not an exile, and, tyranny conceding
The kingdom to three, common queen Venus stepped forth
Into the threefold world; which Juno was with Jove,
With Jove, she was with Triton, Dione was with Dis.
My power, not content with servant stars,
Consoles Tartarus, soothes the seas: you, pious throng
Of the sea god, excuse the crime of your sister,
If it is a crime to have drawn lineage from here: and you, most noble avenger,
Imperious Tethys, with Juno put to flight, demand your sky,
And you will make seized realms your own. Now, with the concubine driven away,
Now without care you will come destined to bear offspring to the Thunderer.
There is no faith in the fates, the Saturnian <note>Juno</note> will lose her wealth,
She will lose the price of beauty. She would not have come
To buy this splendor if she were fair at home. Ill did she take counsel,
Readily offering to grant you your ways, Dardanian <note>Paris</note>. He is not swayed by gold
Who possesses a title of equality; faith once expended
On victorious herds does not sell the nod of the balancing rein,
Not unmindful of ancient equity.
Shall she, charming Paris, enjoy a victorious countenance,
She for whom Hesione groans, for whom ancestral blood flowed,
The warlike virgin who poised the weapons of her Alcides <note>Hercules</note>
Against your citadels? Where then are the battles you boast of?
Where is fate? More was accomplished by the abducted Phrygian <note>Ganymede</note>: he snatched
The cups from the daughter, and the bedchamber from the goddess. Thus gently avenged,
Endowed with Olympus, the flower of Asia, venerable offspring
Of gods and kings, still mingles harsh things for the gods above.
My labor is not unstable, nor is it my glory to weave
Verses, nor to press fearful girls with wool tasks.
Phoebus did not contend with me, Arachne contended with me:
Pallas has promised these feet to you, let her weave these many
Threads. The gods, I confess, are compliant both to promise
And to give supreme things: but what do riches, what do kingdoms, what do weapons
Add to a mighty man, whose scepter rules the greater part of the world,
Whose wealth is of Phrygia, whose strength is the Dardanian race?
Yet if the need of a royal bed is to be consoled,
If strength, if scepters are nothing without the enjoyment of a wife,
Accept the gift of Venus, a gift in which Sparta takes pride,
A gift which Juno would wish to be called, and Minerva wish to be.
Why do I delay? Examine inner Dione <note>Venus</note> more closely.
The matter is at hand to be handled openly: now we disclose
Our bared breasts; with this face, with Phoebus as guide, I traverse the stars,
With this countenance I prolong the day. Handsome youth, congratulate
The deserving one, and as judge, do not despise a face like your own.
Having spoken thus, she reveals her cheeks, casting off her garment,
Her shoulders bare, her bosom exposed, and dawn breaks
Over her whole face. It shames the goddesses to have yielded the triumph to Venus.
Take counsel, Phrygian avengers, Venus rouses the distressed.
These things seen by me stand firm with faith, or dreams certainly
Have weight, here I follow the fates. Obey Dione <note>Venus</note>,
Who puts an end to tears for the citizen, and to laughter for the enemy.

# THE THIRD BOOK OF DARES THE PHRYGIAN ON THE TROJAN WAR.

### SUMMARY.

> The third book foretells the end of Troy, and thence
> Sends Paris across to Argos for the abduction of Helen,
> Since they were previously unwilling to return the captive Hesione.

> The ranks of the Greeks immediately conspire to arms,
> And the two brothers, the sons of Tyndareus <note>Castor and Pollux</note>,
> Are drowned in the stormy sea, extending their palms in the swelling flood.

<summary>Venus completes her speech before Paris in the judgment of beauty, mocking the claims of Minerva and Juno and offering Helen of Sparta, before displaying her divine form to secure his verdict. The text then transitions to the summary of Book Three, forecasting Paris's expedition to abduct Helen after the refusal to return Hesione, prompting the Greek mobilization and the fate of the Tyndaridae.</summary>
