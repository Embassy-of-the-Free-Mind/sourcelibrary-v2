> Provided that life remains, it is well.
> Even the sharp cross,
> Put it under me, I can endure it.

(Thus Acidalius restored, more happily than Lipsius, I think, verses that previously stood in a most corrupt state.) What would have been the most wretched fate if it happened is prayed for, and the delay of punishment is sought as if it were life. You would think him most contemptible if he wished to go on living right up to the cross. "You may indeed weaken me," he says, "as long as the breath of life remains in a shattered and useless body. You may disfigure me, as long as some time is added to my monstrous and twisted form. You may nail me fast, and place a sharp cross beneath me as I sit." Is it worth so much to press upon one's own wound and hang stretched out on a gibbet, merely to postpone that which is the best thing among evils: the end of punishment? Is it worth so much to retain a soul merely to suffer? What could you wish for such a man, except accommodating gods? What does that effeminate disgrace of a poem mean? What means this pact of utter, mad fear? What means so shameful a begging for life? Do you think Virgil was ever recited to this man:
*Is it then so very wretched to die?*—
He prays for the utmost of evils, and that which is heaviest to endure he desires to be prolonged and sustained. For what reward? Namely, for a longer life. But what is his living? To die for a long time. Can anyone be found who would prefer to waste away amid punishments, to perish limb by limb, and to let out his spirit drop by drop so many times, rather than exhale it once and for all? Can anyone be found who, driven to that cursed wood, already weakened, already deformed, crushed into an ugly hump extending from shoulders to chest, having many reasons for dying even without the cross, would wish to draw out a life that will only draw out so many torments? Deny now that it is a great kindness of nature that it is necessary to die! Many men are prepared to strike even worse bargains: even to betray a friend so that they may live longer, and to deliver their children into defilement with their own hands, just so that they may chance to see the light of day that is conscious of so many crimes. The craving for life must be shaken off, and we must learn that it makes no difference when you suffer that which must be suffered at some point; that what matters is how well you live, not how long; and often living well consists precisely in this: not living long. See the notes on book 4, § 40, and book 8, § 58, and book 9, § 2. Augustine, *Epistle* 45: "With what great labors do men strive, merely that they may labor for a longer time; and when death begins to impend, is it avoided only that it may be feared longer? With what pains are those who are treated by physicians and cut into tormented? Is it that they might not die? No, but that they might die somewhat later. Many certain tortures are undergone so that a few uncertain days may be added. And sometimes, overcome by the very pains which they endured out of fear of death, they die immediately. And since they do not at all choose to end life lest they suffer, but choose to suffer lest they end life, it happens to them that they both suffer and end life: not only because even if cured they nevertheless end life after their pains—a life which, compared to such great things, can neither be everlasting, because it is mortal; nor long-lasting, because it is entirely brief; nor secure in its own short span, because it is always uncertain—but also because they finally ended in pain that life which, lest they should end it, they were willing to suffer." <summary>Scholarly commentary on Marcus Aurelius discussing the philosophical folly of prolonging life at the cost of enduring shame, physical agony, or prolonged dying. The author cites Seneca, Maecenas, Augustine, Sophocles, and Euripides to argue that living well and dying nobly are far preferable to cowardly postponing death.</summary>
