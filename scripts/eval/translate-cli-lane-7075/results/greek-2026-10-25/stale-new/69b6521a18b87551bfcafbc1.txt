And yet, even if that noun were Greek, it would not immediately be permissible to say *baltea* with Alciato and *balteon* with Ursinus Velius: although Naso <note>the Roman poet Ovid</note> said both *Orphea* and *Orpheon*. For he indeed said *Orphea* as if from *Orpheus*, following analogy, but he did not say *Orpheon* in the same way: in the poem *In Ibin* <note>Ovid's *Ibis*, a curse poem against an unnamed enemy</note>,

> *And let the Thracian mothers tear apart your limbs with mad claws, thinking you to be Orpheus <note>original: "Orpheon esse ratae"</note>.*

Yet clearly, just as the Latins, when they say *Orpheum*, clothe a Greek word in a Latin termination, so on the other hand I judge that Naso wished to change that Latin termination in *-eum* into the Greek *-eon*: and this nevertheless not as one employing no license: namely, without taking account of the declension, which was different from that in which this was customary. In this way, however, *Pentheon*, *Peleon*, *Theseon* could be said instead of *Penthea*, *Pelea*, *Thesea*.
