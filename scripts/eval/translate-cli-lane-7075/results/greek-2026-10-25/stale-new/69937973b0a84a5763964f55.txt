And indeed, for this reason tyranny is fond of the base, for tyrants delight in being flattered, but no one possessing a free spirit would do this; rather, reasonable men love, or at any rate do not flatter. And base men are useful for base deeds, for <note>according to the proverb</note> nail drives out nail. It is also characteristic of a tyrant to take no pleasure in anyone who is dignified or free-spirited. For the tyrant claims to be the only person of such standing, and anyone who rivals him in dignity and acts freely strips away the superiority and despotic mastery of the tyranny. Tyrants therefore hate such men as destroyers of their rule. It is also characteristic of a tyrant to use foreign guests rather than fellow citizens as companions at meals and associates throughout the day, believing that citizens are enemies while foreigners do not contest his power.

These and similar measures are tyrannical and preserve the rule, yet they lack nothing in wickedness. All these practices may, so to speak, be comprehended under three forms. For tyranny aims at three goals: first, that the subjects have small minds, for a small-minded person would not plot against anyone; second, that they distrust one another, for a tyranny is not overthrown before some people place trust in one another; wherefore tyrants also make war on respectable men as harmful to their rule, not only because they refuse to be governed despotically, but also because they remain faithful both to themselves and to others, and do not betray either themselves or others; third, an incapacity for practical action, for no one undertakes what is impossible, so that no one attempts to overthrow a tyranny when the power is lacking.

These three goals, then, are the boundaries to which the intentions of tyrants are reduced; for one may reduce all tyrannical devices to these three premises: that the subjects may not trust one another, that they may lack the power, and that they may think small thoughts.

This, then, is one method by which preservation is achieved for tyrannies. The other method attends to their security in almost the opposite manner from what has been described. One can grasp it from the corruption of kingships; for just as one way a kingship is ruined is by making its rule more tyrannical, so the preservation of a tyranny lies in making it more kingly, guarding only one thing: power, so that the ruler governs not only willing subjects but also the unwilling. For if he abandons this power, he also abandons the tyranny itself.

This power must remain as a foundational premise, but in other matters the ruler should partly perform and partly pretend to perform the duties of a king, playing the kingly role well. First, he should seem

<column-break/>

to care for the public interest, neither expending lavish gifts of the sort that anger the multitude, when rulers take from men who work and labor hard for meager gains, and give lavishly to courtesans, foreigners, and craftsmen, nor failing to render an accounting of revenues received and expenses disbursed, a policy which some tyrants have already put into practice; for governing in this manner, he will appear to be a steward rather than a tyrant. Nor need he fear ever running short of money while master of the city.

Indeed, for tyrants who leave their home territory, this financial openness is even more advantageous than leaving accumulated wealth behind, for those guarding the treasury would be less inclined to seize power. And to tyrants traveling abroad, the guards left behind are more fearsome than the citizens, since the latter accompany him abroad while the former remain at home.

Next, he must appear to collect taxes and public levies for the sake of administration, and to use them whenever necessary in times of war, and generally prepare himself as a guardian and steward of common funds rather than private property. He must also appear not harsh, but majestic, and furthermore of such character that those meeting him feel reverence rather than fear.

To achieve this, however, is not easy for a ruler who is contemptible. Therefore, even if he does not cultivate the other virtues, he must cultivate civic virtue and foster such a reputation concerning himself. Furthermore, he must appear not to outrage or sexually violate any of his subjects, whether young man or maiden, and ensure that none of his entourage does so either. In the same way, the women of his household must behave modestly toward other women, since many tyrannies have been destroyed through the insolence of women.

Regarding bodily pleasures, he should act contrary to how some tyrants behave today. For they not only indulge in pleasures straight from dawn and continuously for many days, but they even wish to be seen doing so by others, so that people may marvel at them as fortunate and blessed. Instead, a ruler should especially be moderate in such matters; failing that, he should at least avoid being seen by others. For the sober man is neither easily attacked nor easily held in contempt, but the drunkard is; nor is the watchful man, but the sleeper.

Indeed, he must do almost the exact opposite of all the traditional tyrannical practices previously described. For he must adorn and equip the city as if he were a trustee and not a tyrant.
