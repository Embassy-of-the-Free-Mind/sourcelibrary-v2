But whether virtue consists rather in right actions; for the temperate person also needs orderliness. For how else would this man or any other be manifest? It is disputed whether moral choice <term>proairesis</term> <gloss>deliberate moral choice</gloss> or actions are more authoritative for virtue, since virtue consists in both. It is clear that perfection would reside in both. Yet actions require many things; and the greater the actions, the more things are needed, and noble actions likewise. Virtue itself does not need the poets or external resources for the virtues; simply put, the disposition directed toward action does not require favor, nor is living for reputation and honor something chosen by the one who accomplishes right action. Such things will be needed, then, for living as a human being.

That happiness is contemplative, from what source would this be clear? And indeed from both: for we assume the gods above all to be blessed and happy. But what kinds of actions ought we to assign to them? Are they just actions? Or will they appear ridiculous making contracts and returning deposits and all such things? Rather, courageous actions, enduring fearful things and facing dangers because it is noble? Or generous actions? To whom will they give? It is absurd if they should have currency or anything of that sort. What would their private benefit be? Or would they be fearful? Nor is praise for the base use of wealth or desires appropriate. To those who examine all these matters, everything concerning actions appears small and unworthy of gods.

Yet surely all say that the living must live, and therefore must be active. For surely they do not sleep like Endymion <note>Endymion was a figure in Greek mythology granted eternal sleep by Zeus so that he would never age</note>. If, then, action is taken away from a living being, and even more so productive making, what remains except contemplation <term>theōria</term> <gloss>contemplation or study of truth</gloss>?

So the activity of God, surpassing in blessedness, would be contemplative. And among human activities, that which is most akin to this is the most conducive to happiness. A sign of this is that the remaining animals do not partake of happiness, being entirely deprived of such activity. For to the gods, all of life is blessed.

<summary>Aristotle argues that true divine blessedness consists not in practical actions, which are petty and unfitting for gods, but in contemplation. Consequently, the highest human happiness is the contemplative life, which shares most closely in this divine activity.</summary>
