<meta>continues from previous page: this was the first open rupture between the Lacedaemonians and the Athenians</meta> Inaros son of Psammetichus, a Libyan and king of the Libyans bordering Egypt, set out from Marea, the city above Pharos, and caused the greater part of Egypt to revolt from King Artaxerxes. Having made himself ruler, he called in the Athenians. They happened to be campaigning against Cyprus with two hundred ships of their own and of their allies. Leaving Cyprus, they came and sailed up from the sea into the Nile. Gaining control of the river and two parts of Memphis, they pressed on against the third part, which is called the White Fortress <note>the fortified citadel of Memphis</note>. Those of the Persians and Medes who had fled there were inside, along with those Egyptians who had not joined the revolt.

The Athenians, landing from their ships at Halieis, fought a battle against the Corinthians and Epidaurians, and the Corinthians won. Later the Athenians fought a naval battle off Cecryphaleia against the ships of the Peloponnesians, and the Athenians won.

When war was declared against the Aeginetans by the Athenians, a great naval battle took place off Aegina between the Athenians and the Aeginetans. The allies of each side were present, and the Athenians won. Taking seventy of their ships, they landed on the island and laid siege to it under the command of Leocrates son of Stroebus. Then the Peloponnesians, wishing to bring aid to the Aeginetans, sent across to Aegina three hundred hoplites who were previously mercenaries of the Corinthians and Epidaurians. The Corinthians with their allies seized the heights of Mount Geraneia and descended into the Megarid, believing it would be impossible for the Athenians to assist the Megarians, since a large force was absent at Aegina and in Egypt; or, if they did assist, they would have to withdraw from Aegina.

The Athenians did not move the army at Aegina. Instead, from those remaining in the city, both the oldest and the youngest, they came to Megara under the command of Myronides. After an indecisive battle was fought against the Corinthians, the two sides separated from one another, and each side considered that they had come off no worse in the engagement. The Athenians, who nevertheless had the upper hand, set up a trophy after the Corinthians departed. The Corinthians, reproached by the elders in their city and having spent about twelve days preparing, returned and set up a rival trophy themselves, claiming victory. The Athenians came out in force from Megara, killed those who were setting up the trophy, engaged the rest, and defeated them.

The defeated Corinthians began to retreat. A considerable portion of them, forced back and missing the road, fell into an enclosure belonging to a private citizen, which happened to be surrounded by a deep ditch with no way out. The Athenians, seeing this, blocked the entrance in front with their hoplites, and surrounding them on all sides with light-armed troops, stoned to death all who had entered. This was a great disaster for the Corinthians. The main body of their army retreated back home.

At this time the Athenians also began to build the Long Walls down to the sea, both the wall to Phalerum and the wall to Piraeus.

When the Phocians campaigned against Doris, the ancestral homeland of the Lacedaemonians—consisting of Boeum, Cytinium, and Erineum—and captured one of these small towns, the Lacedaemonians went to the aid of the Dorians. They were under the leadership of Nicomedes son of Cleombrotus, acting on behalf of King Pleistoanax son of Pausanias, who was still a youth. They brought fifteen hundred hoplites of their own and ten thousand of their allies. Having compelled the Phocians by treaty to surrender the town, they prepared to return. If they wished to cross by sea across the Crisaean Gulf, the Athenians were prepared to prevent them by sailing around with their fleet. Nor did marching through Mount Geraneia appear safe to them, since the Athenians held Megara and Pegae. Mount Geraneia is difficult to traverse and was continually guarded by the Athenians; and at that time they perceived that the Athenians intended to block them along that route.

They decided to wait in Boeotia and consider how they might make their passage most safely. In addition, certain Athenian men were secretly inviting them, hoping to put an end to the democracy and to the construction of the Long Walls. The Athenians marched out against them in full force, along with one thousand Argives and contingents from the other allies as each became available, totaling fourteen thousand men in all. Believing that the Lacedaemonians were at a loss for how to pass through, and partly suspecting a plot to overthrow the democracy, they went on campaign against them. Thessalian cavalry also came to the Athenians in accordance with their alliance, but during the action they deserted to the Lacedaemonians.

When the battle took place at Tanagra in Boeotia, the Lacedaemonians and their allies won, and great slaughter occurred on both sides. The Lacedaemonians went into the Megarid, cut down the fruit trees, and returned home through Mount Geraneia and across the Isthmus.
