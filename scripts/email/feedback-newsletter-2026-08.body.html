Every page in this library was read by a machine before it was read by a person. Six million scanned pages, and nearly five million of them turned into English by an AI model. Almost none of them have ever been read by a human proofreader, and at that scale almost none of them ever will be.

Which means that when you open a page and actually look at it, there is a real chance you are the first person ever to do so — and the only one who will notice if something is wrong.

Two of those noticings, from the last few weeks.

“I’m seeing chapter 4 on the image page and chapter 5 in the text”

Fourteen words, sent from a book page in July. It turned out that 261 books were serving every scan one leaf behind its own text. The Internet Archive keeps scanner calibration cards inside its raw archive files but strips them from the public images; our importer counted one of those cards as page one and shifted everything after it. Around 105,000 pages, wrong for four months.

Every one of those pages looked completely fine on its own. The fault is only visible if you read the scan against the translation — which is what a reader does and a script doesn’t. Someone had reported the same thing ten days earlier, in eleven words: “The page ocr and text of image do not appear to be aligned.” It took the second report before we connected them. Both people were right, and we were slow.

“What is this page about? Real?”

Four words, typed on our encyclopedia entry for the botanist Matthiolus. Fair question. The page claimed he appeared on pages 47–58 of a book that names him on pages 44 and 100. Our index had been crediting every page of a large extraction batch with every name found anywhere in that batch, and roughly four out of five page citations across the encyclopedia turned out to be unverifiable.

They now read “discussed in pp. X–Y” wherever we can’t pin a page down. A coarse true claim is worth more than a precise false one, and the reason we know that is a stranger who typed four words into a box.

Three ways to tell us

1. From the bottom of any page. Scroll down. It’s there on every page in the library.

Click it and a box opens. Write whatever you noticed. It quietly records which page you were standing on, so you never have to explain where you are.

2. From under a translation, while you’re reading. There is a faint line beneath the English text: Notice a translation issue? Let us know. It carries the book and the page number with it, so a note as short as “wrong word in line three” is enough to act on.

3. Ask us for something. A book we don’t have. A translation that doesn’t exist yet. On an untranslated book the page offers “Request one” — that is a real queue, not a suggestion box. Forty-nine pages exist in English today because somebody clicked it.

What happens after you send it

It lands in a queue that gets read. 336 messages so far; 298 of them are marked handled — fixed, answered, or filed as a tracked issue. If you leave an email address we write back when it’s done. Some take an afternoon. The alignment bug above took an audit of 2,262 books.

None of it needs to be polished. Some of the most useful messages we have ever had are four words long:

“Not the original edition.”
“Some books need cropping white or black space.”
“This image doesn’t correspond with this text.”
“Opening the collection should put me at the top of the page. I landed in the middle and it was confusing.”

Every one of those was acted on.

We put these books online so they could be read closely. Reading them closely is also the only thing that finds what’s broken in them. The two turn out to be the same activity, which is a nice piece of luck for a library built in the open.

If something looks wrong, it probably is. Tell us.
