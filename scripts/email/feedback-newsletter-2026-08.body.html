Every page in this library was read by a machine before it was read by a person. Six million scanned pages, nearly five million of them turned into English by an AI model. Almost none have ever been read by a human proofreader, and at that scale almost none ever will be.

So when you open a page and actually look at it, you are often the first person to do so — and the only one who will notice if something is wrong.

That isn’t a disclaimer. It’s how the library works. Reading these books closely is the only thing that finds what’s broken in them, which makes every careful reader part of the quality control.

Two from July. Someone sent fourteen words — “I’m seeing chapter 4 on the image page and chapter 5 in the text” — and it turned out 261 books were serving every scan one leaf behind its own text. Around 105,000 pages, wrong for four months. Someone else sent four: “What is this page about? Real?” Our encyclopedia had been citing page numbers it couldn’t back up, roughly four out of every five.

Neither was found by us.

Three ways to tell us

1. From the bottom of any page. Click, type, send. It records which page you were on, so you never have to explain where you are.

2. From under a translation, as you read. A faint line beneath the English: Notice a translation issue? Let us know. It carries the book and page with it, so “wrong word in line three” is enough.

3. Or ask us for something. A book we don’t have, a translation that doesn’t exist yet. Forty-nine pages exist in English today because somebody clicked “Request one.”

Nothing needs to be polished. Two of the most useful messages we have ever had:

“Not the original edition.”
“This image doesn’t correspond with this text.”

Four words is plenty. Both were acted on. 336 messages so far, 298 of them handled — and if you leave an email address, we write back when it’s done.

If something looks wrong, it probably is. Tell us.
