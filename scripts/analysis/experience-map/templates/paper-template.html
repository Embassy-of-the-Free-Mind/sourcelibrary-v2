Towards a Map of Conscious Experiences Across History

A first attempt, what it teaches about the requirements, and why the obvious version of the idea does not work

Working draft, not for circulation. Citations marked are from background knowledge and have not been verified against the literature. Classification is by a single language model with no human-validated subset, so no inter-rater agreement statistic is available. Both are blocking issues for submission.

Abstract

For four thousand years people have tried to write down what happened to them when their consciousness changed — in vision, contemplation, fever, bereavement, intoxication, terror. Those reports survive in hundreds of thousands of pages, in dozens of languages, scattered across traditions that mostly could not read one another. They have never been assembled into anything a person could look at whole. This paper asks what it would take to build such a map, and reports a first attempt.

We work over approximately 3.9 million machine-translated pages of primary sources. A blind random sample establishes the size of the field: 5.4% of corpus pages describe experience from the inside, implying 156,000–279,000 such pages in this library alone. We then build two independent sorting instruments — one derived from modern psychometric scales, one extracted from the Visuddhimagga in its own vocabulary — and run both over identical material.

The attempt yields four requirements that any such map must satisfy, each learned by violating it first: retrieval must be stratified by language or the map becomes a portrait of the largest language; genre categories must be historically appropriate or they manufacture their own findings; the instrument must be plural, because a single taxonomy can only rediscover itself; and the map must be honest about measuring descriptions rather than experiences. On that last point we report a result that constrains the whole programme: nearest-neighbour analysis shows the embedding space is organised more strongly by source text (12.4× over chance) and language (5.1×) than by phenomenological content (3.0×). We also report one substantive finding that survives those constraints — the Visuddhimagga's concentration ladder does not travel outside its tradition while its sequence of destabilisation does (χ²(1) = 14.61, p < .001, OR = 3.1) — and treat it as a demonstration that the map, built carefully, can answer questions the humanities have only been able to argue about.

1. Why this map is worth building

The historical record of inner experience is the largest body of evidence about consciousness that exists, and it is almost entirely unused. Consciousness science works with laboratory reports from living subjects, mostly Western, mostly recent, mostly answering a questionnaire someone else wrote. The historical record is larger by orders of magnitude, spans every culture that kept writing, and covers states no ethics board would permit inducing. What it lacks is not evidence but access: the material is scattered, mostly untranslated, and unsearchable by what it describes.

Machine translation and semantic retrieval remove that specific obstacle for the first time. It is now possible to ask a library, in one query, for every passage in any language that reports the sense of a presence in an empty room — and get back the Syriac, the Tibetan, the Icelandic, and the case note. The question this paper takes up is what one should build with that capacity, and what it takes to build it without producing something that merely looks like knowledge.

A map of conscious experiences across history would do at least four things no existing resource does. It would let the perennialism–constructivism dispute — whether mystical experience has a common core or is constituted by tradition, argued since Stace and Katz — be posed as an empirical question over thousands of sources rather than a canon of exemplary figures. It would recover categories of experience that modern instruments have no items for, and that consequently go unstudied. It would let a person in a state they find frightening discover that it has been described before, by name, in five traditions. And it would give the historical testimony of ordinary people a place in a science that currently has no way to cite them.

2. What would count as such a map

The naive version — embed everything, project it, look at the clusters — produces a picture that is confidently wrong, for reasons we document below. Before reporting our attempt we state the criteria we think any serious version must meet. Each was learned the hard way.

  • Every point must be verifiable. A map of experience assembled by machine is worth nothing if a reader cannot check it. Every passage here is a quotation checked character-for-character against its source page and linked to the scan of that page. Where the map is wrong, a reader can see that it is wrong.
  • Coverage must be stratified, not popular. Similarity search over a multilingual corpus returns a popularity contest won by the largest language. Coverage has to be enforced.
  • The instrument must be plural. A map sorted by one taxonomy can only rediscover that taxonomy. At minimum it must be possible to run a tradition's own categories over the same material and compare.
  • There must be a denominator. Without knowing how much of the corpus is experiential to begin with, there is no way to tell whether an instrument is selecting signal or returning whatever is nearest.
  • Genre and register must be modelled, not assumed. Historical texts arrive as instruction, testimony, doctrine, and narrative in proportions that differ sharply by tradition; flattening those is not neutral.
  • It must say what it measures. This is a map of descriptions. The distance between a description and an experience is the subject of §6, and it is larger than one would like.

3. The size of the field

A blind random sample of 788 pages, classified by the same pipeline used everywhere else, gives the first number the programme needs: 5.4% of corpus pages (95% CI 4.0–7.2, Wilson) describe an experience from the inside. Extrapolated across the corpus that is 156,000–279,000 pages of experiential material in one library. Our map plots 1,952 of them — roughly a 1% core sample.

The same sample shows what the material mostly is: 48.5% of random pages are doctrinal, 26.5% unrelated, 11.0% instructional, and only 1.3% first-person testimony. Testimony is rare enough that any map built on it must be built by search, not by browsing — which makes the quality of the search instrument the central methodological problem.

4. First attempt

4.1 Two instruments over identical material

Both instruments express each category as several first-person report sentences rather than a topic label — "I felt myself dissolve and become one with everything around me" rather than "ego dissolution" — because retrieval matches passages, and the aim is experiential register rather than topical vocabulary. Probes are embedded and matched by cosine similarity against page embeddings.

Instrument A takes 36 dimensions: twenty anchored to the MEQ30, 5D-ASC and Hood M-scale so results stay comparable to the psychometric literature, plus sixteen the modern scales have no items for but the record documents heavily — felt presence, sleep paralysis, near-death, apparitions of the dead, fever delirium, mystical eros, pain and childbirth, unsourced music, animal transformation, mesmeric trance, sensory deprivation, fasting, inspired dictation, ecstatic dance, autoscopy, uncanny foreknowledge. All sixteen retrieved substantial material, which is itself an argument about what the modern instruments miss.

Instrument B is not ours. The Visuddhimagga, Buddhaghosa's fifth-century Theravāda manual, defines nine attainments of concentration and sixteen knowledges of insight, each with stated criteria for recognising it — an ordered, criterion-based state model written fifteen centuries before the MEQ. We located the pages of our own copy where each stage is defined and extracted the recognition markers and first-person report sentences in the manual's own vocabulary, forbidding substitution of modern psychological terms. This gave 93 probes across 26 stages. For ādīnava-ñāṇa, the knowledge of danger:

“Everywhere I look — at my own body, my thoughts, and the world — I see only a burning charcoal pit, or a murderer with a raised weapon.”

— a sentence assembled from the manual's own similes, by a procedure that let the tradition speak.

4.2 Integrity constraints

Every quotation was checked character-for-character against its source page and discarded if it did not match; this rejected 106 silently altered quotations, roughly 3%. Passages were deduplicated at the level of the bibliographic work, so a scripture held in many editions and languages contributes once. Quotations, not pages, were embedded for projection. Classification recorded register, whether the passage is experiential, the antecedent that brought the state on, what followed for the person, what agent the text credits, and whether the text records a dispute about the experience's source.

5. What the attempt teaches

5.1 Requirement: stratify, or map the largest language

Our first pass used global similarity search and produced a map that was 22.3% English against English's 6.5% share of the corpus, and 2.8% Tibetan against Tibetan's 14.5%. Ethiopic, Persian, Japanese and Armenian did not appear at all. Giving every language an identical probe budget corrects this. The cost is that the resulting sample is neither proportional to the corpus nor random, and language frequencies within it must never be read as corpus frequencies — a stratified map is a deliberate instrument, and has to be labelled as one.

5.2 Requirement: a denominator, so selection can be distinguished from proximity

Instrument A judged 36.3% of what it retrieved to be experiential, against the 5.4% baseline — an enrichment of 6.7×. Without the baseline this number does not exist, and there is no way to answer the obvious sceptical question of whether the probes are selecting anything at all. Instrument B, instructed to refuse weak matches, claims only 11.0% of what it retrieves: an indigenous stage model turns out to be far stricter than a psychometric checklist, because it asks whether an experience occupies a specific position in an ordered sequence rather than whether it had a quality.

5.3 Requirement: model register, because traditions transmit different things

Across 5,728 classified passages, categories differ sharply in the register through which they reach us. Contemplative-emptiness material arrives 57% as instruction and 6% as testimony; body-dissolution material arrives 7% as instruction and 63% as testimony. Contemplative traditions transmit predominantly method; visionary and medical traditions transmit predominantly report. A map that flattens this would show the contemplative traditions as strangely absent from the phenomenology, when in fact they are present as instructions for producing it. It also has a consequence for the wider field: a questionnaire can only ever collect testimony, so the prescriptive half of the historical record has no modern counterpart against which to be compared.

5.4 Requirement: historically appropriate genre categories

An early pass forced every passage into a testimony/fiction binary and reported 31% testimony. Adding a vision-narrative register — for visions related as real within hagiographic, scriptural or devotional narrative, where the modern true/invented distinction does not apply — reassigned most of that material: vision-narrative became the largest single register at 924 of 1,952 passages, and testimony fell to 6.5%. The first number was an artefact of imposing a modern genre distinction on premodern texts. This is the clearest case in the study of a category manufacturing its own finding, and it is the reason we regard category pluralism as a requirement rather than a refinement.

5.5 Demonstration: what a plural instrument can show

Running the Visuddhimagga's categories over the same corpus produces a result neither instrument yields alone. Its model claimed 295 passages, 82 from inside the Buddhist tradition and 213 from outside it — and its two ladders behave differently across that boundary.

Christian mystical sources supply 104 of the outside matches, most often to bhaya-ñāṇa, appearance as terror; Sufi sources match most often to muñcitukamyatā, desire for deliverance. Ephrem the Syrian and a 1374 Armenian manuscript match to terror; the Jain Uttarādhyayana Sūtra to ādīnava:

“In this hollow human existence, which is a home for sickness and disease, and is gripped by old age and death, I cannot find even a moment's pleasure.”

What this does and does not show. The concentration ladder describes attainments produced by a technique taught in a specific institutional setting; it does not travel, and there is no reason it should. The destabilising phase describes something that happens to people — practitioners and non-practitioners, contemplatives and the bereaved — and it does. This is far weaker than perennialism: not that mystical experience has a common core, only that one particular phase of destabilisation is described in convergent terms by traditions with no contact. The constructivist can absorb it without difficulty, since terror and the wish to escape are ordinary capacities with ordinary vocabularies. What the result demonstrates is not an answer to the old dispute but that the dispute is now addressable: two independently constructed taxonomies, run over identical material, disagree in a measurable and interpretable way.

6. The hard constraint

The most important result for the programme is a negative one. For every plotted passage we took its ten nearest neighbours in quotation-embedding space and asked what they share.

A passage's neighbours are 12.4 times more likely than chance to come from the same book and 5.1 times more likely to share a language, while sharing the actual phenomenological content lifts only 3.0×. The corpus is machine-translated into English and embedded by an English-centric model; proximity is therefore substantially proximity of translationese. There is also a chain of roughly a dozen lossy transformations between an experience and a point on the map — the experience, the memory of it, the decision to write, the conventions available, the copying, the survival, the digitisation, the OCR, the machine translation, the embedding, the probe that retrieved it, the model that judged it.

We take three conclusions. First, honest naming: this is a map of descriptions of experience, and calling it anything else is a claim the data does not support. Second, claim discipline: we report the ladder asymmetry because it compares rates between two populations within one instrument, which is robust to a uniform translation effect, and we decline to report cluster structure as evidence about experience. Third, a research target: the translator effect is measurable, and measuring it — via same-work-different-translator controls and probes written natively in each source language — is the single highest-value next step for the programme.

7. Roadmap

What we have is a proof that the field is large, that instruments can be built for it, that a tradition's own categories can be extracted and run as one of them, and that the results are interpretable. What remains, in the order we would do it:

  • Native-language probes. Written in Pali, Persian, Tibetan, Classical Chinese, not translated from English — the direct attack on the constraint in §6.
  • More indigenous taxonomies. Already located in this corpus and awaiting the same extraction treatment: Sufi maqāmāt and aḥwāl (Hujwīrī's Kashf al-Maḥjūb), the Māṇḍūkya four states, Teresa's seven mansions, Daoist inner alchemy (Xingming Guizhi, 1615), the alchemical colour sequence, scholastic faculty psychology. Where independently constructed taxonomies agree is where the interesting evidence lies.
  • Human validation. A scholar-annotated subset, inter-rater agreement, and adjudication of the cases where the model and the annotator differ. Nothing here is publishable without it.
  • Antecedents and aftermath as first-class axes. The corpus records what brought a state on and what it did to the person. Modern experimental work cannot ask the first question, because it holds the cause fixed by design.
  • The visual record. Alchemical plates, visionary diagrams and cosmological figures are phenomenological reports in another medium, and no text probe will ever retrieve them.
  • Non-elite sources. Everything here is a printed book by someone literate enough to publish. Asylum casebooks, trial depositions, medical notes and letters are where most describers are.

8. Data

Every plotted passage carries a resolvable link to the scanned page it was transcribed from, so any individual claim can be checked against the facsimile. Both datasets are released in full below.

Limitations

  • Circularity. A probe-based map cannot discover a category its probe set did not name. The sixteen dimensions added to Instrument A were added because their absence was noticed by inspection; others are certainly still missing.
  • No second rater. Single model, single pass, no human-validated subset, therefore no inter-rater agreement statistic. The most serious outstanding gap.
  • Over-matching in literary sources. A Pushkin lyric about breaking free of nets and a line of Akhmatova's were matched to insight stages. Aggregate rates are defensible; individual matches are not.
  • The inside/outside boundary is crude, drawn by a tradition-tag list rather than by a scholar, and the classifier itself misassigned many Vajrayāna sources.
  • Normativity. The Visuddhimagga is soteriological: it describes what should happen to someone progressing correctly, and was used institutionally to authorise some experiences and pathologise others. Substituting it for the MEQ trades a psychometric bias for a monastic one; the argument of this paper is for holding several such lenses at once, never for crowning one.
  • Corpus composition reflects digitisation and translation priorities, not history. Century distributions are not evidence about when people had these experiences.
