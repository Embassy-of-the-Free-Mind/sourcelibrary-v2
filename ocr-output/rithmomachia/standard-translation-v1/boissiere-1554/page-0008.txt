...placed and honored him, who not only during wars, but also after having performed countless feats of prowess—even among foreign and barbarian nations—and after having been afflicted by his own kin in the depths of his misfortunes, has not only received these natural sciences but has also, with great zeal, embraced the supernatural and divine. [[notes: The author is praising the dedicatee's ability to remain a scholar and a man of faith despite the "vicissitudes of fortune," a common trope in Renaissance dedications meant to highlight the subject's stoicism.]]

Who is he in this regard who will excel you, my most honored lord? Is there another *Cato*, another *Marius* who has carried himself more steadfastly and modestly in his adversities than you? [[notes: *Cato* the Younger was a Roman statesman famous for his stoic endurance and moral integrity. *Marius* was a Roman general who famously survived exile and extreme hardship before returning to power.]] Is there another *Scipio* who has more wisely and steadfastly bore himself in his travels [[gloss: peregrinations]] to regions both foreign and domestic, by sea and by land, for the protection [[gloss: tuition]] and defense of his homeland, as you have done and continue to do daily for the sole protection and defense of the so-flourishing kingdom of *France*? [[notes: *Scipio* Africanus was a Roman general celebrated for his campaigns across the Mediterranean. "Tuition" here carries its original Latin sense of guardianship or protection (*tuitio*), rather than modern education.]] 

As for me, I will say no more and leave everything to the judgment of all: for your illustrious deeds are so apparent that no one is ignorant of them. I am well assured, too, that such feats of prowess will not be omitted from books more authoritative than mine own.
