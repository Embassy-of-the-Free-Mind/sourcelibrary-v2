[[margin: 34]]
¶ Whatever is gathered from numbers taken twice from unity: is an oblong [[notes: *Altera parte longior*, literally "longer by one part," refers to a number of the form $n(n+1)$, such as $2 \times 3 = 6$.]] number and is less than its sides: the greatest of those gathered.
¶ Let there be numbers of this kind taken in a double order: unity, **a**, **b**, **c**, **d**, which are added together to make **m**. I say that **m** is an oblong number, and its lesser side is **d**. For by the preceding [proposition], if I add **e** to the aforesaid numbers, a square **f** is made, whose difference from the middle oblong is **e**. Therefore, removing the number **e** from the square **f**, there remains the oblong number midway between **f** and the next smaller square. But what remains when **e** is removed is the sum of the two assigned orders, namely **m**. Therefore **m** is the oblong mean between **f** and the next smaller square. But that oblong mean is made from **d** times **e**, the sides of those squares, by the twenty-fourth [proposition] of the present [book]. But **d** is the lesser; therefore the lesser side of the oblong **m** is **d**, which is the greatest of the assigned orders. Thus the whole proposition stands firm.

[[margin: 35]]
¶ If a number is a square-cube: the side of the square will be a cube, while the side of the cube will be a square [[notes: A "square-cube" is a number that is both a square and a cube, which we identify as a sixth power ($x^6$). For example, 64 is $8^2$ and $4^3$. Its square root (8) is a cube ($2^3$), and its cube root (4) is a square ($2^2$).]].
¶ Let **a** be a square-cubic number, whose side as a square is **b**, and whose side as a cube is **c**. I say that **b** is a cube and **c** is a square. The first part is evident: for I multiply **b** by **a** and let it become **d**. It is clear that **d** is a cube and **a** is a cube that measures it according to **b**. Therefore, by the corollary of the seventeenth [proposition] of this [book], **b** is a cube because it is the first. The second part is shown: let **e** be the square of **c**. Since that which is contained under the extremes **c** and **e** is equal to that which is made from **b** times itself [[notes: Meaning $c \times e = b^2$]], therefore by the twenty-sixth of the second [book], **e**, **b**, and **c** are in continuous proportion. But **e** was first established as a square; therefore **c**, by the tenth [proposition] of this [book], is also a square. Thus the whole proposition is established.

[[margin: 36]]
¶ If there are two square-cubic numbers: the mean between them in continuous proportionality will be a cube; but each of the two [other] means will be a square.
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two square-cubics. Because **a** and **b** are squares, by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book], there is one proportional mean between them, which is **c**. And because **a** and **b** are cubes, by the same corollary, there are two proportional means between them, which are **d** and **e**. I say, therefore, that **c** is a cube, and **d** and **e** are squares. That **c** is a cube is declared thus: for I multiply **a** by **b** and let **f** be produced. Because **a** and **b** are cubes, **f** will also be a cube by the sixteenth [proposition] of this [book]. And since **a**, **c**, and **b** are proportionals, then by the twenty-sixth of the second [book], the cube **f** is made from **c** multiplied by itself. Wherefore **f** is the square of **c**. Thus **f** is a square-cube; wherefore **c**, the side of **f** as a square, is a cube by the preceding [proposition], which is the first [point]. But that **d** and **e** are squares is clear from this: because **b**, **e**, and **d** are in continuous proportion and **b** is first a square, therefore the third, **d**, is also a square by the tenth [proposition] of this [book]. Likewise, **a**, **d**, and **e** are proportional numbers and **a** is first a square; therefore by the same tenth [proposition], the third, **e**, is a square. Thus the whole proportion is known.

¶ To find two square-cubics between which any number of proportional means may be taken.
¶ If I am commanded to find two square-cubics between which any number of proportional means may be placed, I take numbers proportional [starting from unity] more by two than the means I am commanded to find [[notes: To find 4 means, take 6 terms in proportion]]. For example, if I am commanded to find two square-cubics between which there are four proportional means, I take six numbers in continuous proportion, such as **a, b, c, d, e, f**. Of these, let the cubes be taken in order: **g, h, k, l, m, n**, which by the sixth [proposition] of this [book] are also proportional. Let these cubes indeed be multiplied by themselves in order, and let **o, p, q, r, s, t** be produced. I say, therefore, that **o** and **t** are two such square-cubics. For since **g, h**, and the others are multiplied by themselves, **o, p, q, r**, and the others are squares. And because **g, h**, and the others (that is, their sides) are proportional, by the fifth [proposition] of this [book], **o, p, q**, and the others (their squares) are proportional. But since **g** and **n** are cubes, then by the sixteenth [proposition] of this [book], **o** and **t** will be cubes. Therefore **o** and **t** are square-cubics between which the four numbers **p, q, r, s** are proportional means, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 38]]
¶ To find two square-cubics between which it is possible to take means according to given numbers.
¶ Let the given numbers be **a** and **b**, and the goal be to find two square-cubics between which there are proportional numbers according to **a** and also according to **b**. Let **a** be two and **b** be three. Because proportions are always one more than the means themselves, and **a** is two, therefore to find means according to **a**, there must be three proportions. And because **b** is three, to find means according to **b**, there must be four proportions. I multiply three by four and let **e** be produced. According to **e**, I arrange continuous proportions in the numbers **r, d, e, f, g, h, l, m, n, o, p, q**. Let **q** and **r** be set as square-cubics by the preceding [proposition]. And I join four of those proportions as here, according to groups of four, and I will have three proportional means, namely **n, l**, and **f**, by equal proportionality. And similarly I join three of them as here, by groups of five, and I will have **m** and **g**, two proportional means between the square-cubics **q** and **r**, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 39]]
¶ Between any two similar plane numbers: it is necessary that there be a third number in continuous proportionality [[notes: This is Euclid VIII.18: between two similar plane numbers there is one mean proportional]].
¶ Let **a** and **b** be similar plane [[notes: *Superficiales*, or numbers representing the area of rectangles with proportional sides.]] numbers, and the sides of **a** be **c** and **d**, and the sides of **b** be **e** and **f**. I say that between **a** and **b** there is one number in continuous proportion. For because **a** and **b** are similar by definition, their sides are proportional. Therefore, as is the proportion of **c** to **d**, so is **e** to **f**. Wherefore, by permutation, as is the proportion of **c** to **e**, so is **d** to **f**. I multiply **e** by **d** and let it become **g**. And because **c** times **d** makes **a**, and **b** times that same **d** [[notes: This likely means "e times d"]] makes **g**, therefore by the eighth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **a** to **g** is as **c** to **e**. And because again **e** times **d** makes **g** and **e** times **f** makes **b**, therefore the proportion of **g** to **b** is as **d** to **f**. But the proportion of **c** to **e** was proven to be as **d** to **f**; therefore the proportion of **a** to **g** is as **g** to **b**. Thus, the number **g** is a proportional mean between **a** and **b**, which was to be demonstrated.

[[margin: 40]]
¶ And if a third number falls between them as a proportional mean: then any two numbers will be similar plane numbers.
¶ This is the converse of the preceding. For let **a** and **b** be any two numbers between which **c** is a proportional mean. I say that **a** and **b** are plane and similar. Let **d** and **e** be the least numbers in the proportion of **a** to **c**, which, because they are the least, will measure **a** and **c** equally by the nineteenth [proposition] of the third [book]; let this be according to **f**. And [the same numbers] **k** [should be **d, e**] will measure **c** and **b** equally; let this be according to **g**. It is therefore manifest, since **a** is contained under two sides **d** and **f**, and **b** is contained under two sides **e** and **g**, that **a** and **b** are plane numbers by definition. But that they are similar is clear. For because by hypothesis **d** and **e** measure **c** and **b** according to **g**, **d** measures **c** according to **g**, and **e** measures **c** according to **f**, as was also posited. Therefore, by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], **d, f** and **e, g** are proportionals. But **d** and **f** are the sides of **a**, and **e** and **g** are the sides of **b**. Therefore, **a** and **b** are similar plane numbers by definition, which is what was proposed.
