[[margin: 21]] ¶ **In the Chromatic fourteen-string scale, within the assigned limits, the fourth occurs three times: only once as an immobile interval, and twice [as mobile]. The fifth occurs twice: once as immobile and once as partly mobile. The octave, however, occurs seven times: three times in an immobile fashion, just as in the Diatonic genus, and four times as a mobile interval.**

¶ The *tetradecachordum* [[notes: literally "fourteen-stringed"]] is composed of four tetrachords; it is named so because it contains fourteen strings or voices. I say first that among these fourteen strings in the Chromatic genus—arranged continuously by the letters b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, k, l, m, n, o, p, starting from the *hypate hypaton*—from the first to the fourth boundary, the *diatessaron* [[notes: the interval of a perfect fourth]] is contained three times, but only once in an immobile way. For according to the tenth rule, b to e sounds a fourth; this is the first fourth. But because by the tenth rule c to f is an *apotome* and d to e is a *trisemitonium*, it follows that c to e contains two tones. By the eleventh rule, e to f is a semitone; therefore, by the sixth rule of the third book, the span from c to f contains two tones and a semitone, making it a fourth. This is the second fourth. Furthermore, because by the eleventh rule e to f is a minor semitone and f to g is an *apotome*, the span e to g is a full tone. Since d to e was shown to be a *trisemitonium*, d to g contains two tones and a semitone; thus, d to g is the third fourth. Since the first fourth (b to e) goes from the *hypate hypaton* to the *hypate meson* [[notes: "lowest of the low" to "lowest of the middle" strings]], it is immobile according to the eighteenth rule. The second (c to f) and third (d to g) are from the *parhypate* and *lichanos* of the principal notes, which the nineteenth rule revealed to be mobile. Thus, the first point is established.

¶ Second, I say that within the fifth boundary, the *diapente* [[notes: the interval of a perfect fifth]] is contained only twice. For since b to e was just shown to be a fourth, and e to f plus f to g together make a tone, then by the thirteenth rule of the third book, b to g sounds a fifth. This is the first fifth. However, it is completed by six voices rather than five, failing to preserve the [standard] property of a fifth. But c to g will not complete a fifth; for as just shown, it lacks the minor semitone b to c to reach a full fifth. Nor does c to h; for by the eleventh rule, g to h contains a *trisemitonium*. Since c to g was only a minor semitone short of a fifth, the interval c to h exceeds the consonance of a fifth by a full tone. Nor does d to h contain a fifth; for since c to h exceeds a fifth by a whole tone, once the *apotome* c to d is removed, d to h remains exceeding the fifth by a minor semitone. But e to i does sound a fifth: for h to i is a tone by the twelfth rule. Since e to h was just shown to exceed a fifth by a minor semitone—and if the minor *trisemitonium* d to e is removed—e to h is left lacking a tone for a fifth; thus, when the tone h to i is added, it becomes a fifth. Therefore, e to i is the second fifth. From b to the fifth boundary f, the fifth is taken only twice. Since the first fifth (b to g) moves from the immobile *hypate hypaton* to the partly mobile *lichanos meson*, it is considered partly mobile. But since e to i moves from the *hypate meson* to the *paramese*, both of which are shown to be immobile, it is an immobile fifth and preserves the property of moving from the fifth position to the fifth position.

¶ Third, I say the *diapason* [[notes: the interval of an octave]] occurs seven times: three times immobile and four times mobile. For since b to e was shown to be a fourth in the first part, and e to i is a fifth by the second part, then by the twentieth rule of the third book, b to i sounds an octave. This is the first octave. Furthermore, because c to f was found to be the second fourth, and f to g is an *apotome*, and g to h is a *trisemitonium*, then f to h consists of two tones; since h to i is a tone and i to k is a semitone, f to k is a fifth. Thus c to k sounds an octave, being the second octave. By the same logic, d to l is the third octave (aided by the twelfth rule), e to m is the fourth, f to n the fifth, g to o the sixth, and h to p the seventh. But that it occurs three times immobile and four times mobile is shown in the same manner as in the twentieth rule.

[[margin: 22]] ¶ **In the Enharmonic fourteen-string scale, the fourth occurs three times and the octave seven times among the assigned limits, just as in the preceding sections. However, the fifth occurs only once and is immobile.**

[[notes: In the Enharmonic genus, the intervals are *Die.* for *diesis* (a quarter-tone) and *Dito.* for *ditonus* (a major third).]]

¶ We use *tetradecachordum* and *tessaradecachordum* to mean the same thing. Let b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, k, l, m, n, o, p be the Enharmonic fourteen-string scale. I say first that between b and e, the fourth is contained three times. For because by the fifteenth rule b, c, and d are two quarter-tone *dieses*, the span b to d is a minor semitone. Since d to e is a *ditone* [[notes: a major third]], b to e is the first fourth. By the same logic, e to f is the second, and d to g is the third. Because b and e are the *hypate hypaton* and *hypate meson*, the first is immobile. Since c and d are the *parhypate hypaton* and *lichanos hypaton*, which are mobile, the others occur in two mobile ways.

¶ Second, I say that from the first to the fifth boundary, the fifth occurs only once, and indeed as an immobile interval. For b to f does not make a fifth because it only adds a quarter-tone *diesis* to the fourth b to e. Nor does b to g, because it only adds two *dieses* (which make a minor semitone) to the fourth. Nor does b to h, because it adds a minor semitone and two tones to the fourth. For the same reason, a fifth is not formed by e to g or e to h; in the former case a tone and a *diesis* would be in excess, while in the latter it would lack a tone minus one *diesis*. Nor d to g, for it lacks a tone. Nor d to h, for a tone would be in excess. However, since e to h is a fourth (for e, f, g are two *dieses* and g to h is a *ditone*), and since h to i is a tone, e to i is a fifth. Since e is the *hypate meson* and i is the *paramese*, which are shown to be immobile, the fifth occurs only once within the assigned limits and is immobile.

¶ Third, the octave occurs seven times, as explained in the Diatonic section according to the fifteenth and twentieth rules.
