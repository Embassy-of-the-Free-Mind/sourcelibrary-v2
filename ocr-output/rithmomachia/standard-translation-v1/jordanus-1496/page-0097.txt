...the tone into two equal parts: but he also divides and cuts it into three and four. For first, he cuts the tone into two equal parts, which for that reason he calls **hemitones** [[notes: Literally "half-tones"]]. Second, into three parts, and he names any of those thirds a **tritemerian diesis** [[notes: From the Greek *tritos* (third) and *meros* (part); a "third-part" division]]. Third, into four, and he calls this fourth part of a tone a **tetartemerian diesis** [[notes: From *tetartos* (fourth); a "fourth-part" division]]. These **dieses** [[notes: The plural of diesis, the smallest interval used in ancient Greek melody]] are sometimes third-parts and sometimes fourth-parts of a tone. For *tritos* is "third," *tetartos* is "fourth," and *meros* is "part." He also proposes a third definition of the diesis, so that it may be called a third of a tone plus half of a third. And again, he calls the dieses of the first mode and first definition **chromatic**; the second he calls **enharmonic**; however, the third he calls **hemiolias** [[notes: A ratio of 3:2, or 1.5]] of the enharmonic division. In part, he stands with **Aristoxenus** [[notes: A Greek philosopher (c. 335 BCE) who argued that musical intervals should be judged by the ear rather than by mathematical ratios]], and in part, he disagrees. He agrees insofar as Aristoxenus makes the half of a tone a semitone and calls a third of a tone a chromatic diesis (specifically of the "soft" chromatic), and a quarter of a tone an enharmonic diesis. But he disagrees in that he calls a quarter of a tone plus half of a quarter a "hemiolic" chromatic diesis. 

And certainly, either **Martianus** [[notes: Martianus Capella, a 5th-century author whose *Marriage of Philology and Mercury* was a standard textbook on the liberal arts]] must be thought to have fallen into a new error in this third definition of the diesis, or he thought that a hemitone and an enharmonic hemiolia were the same thing. For since every whole is made complete by its three thirds: it is therefore necessary that one third plus half of a third should fulfill exactly half of the whole. But since these things prove themselves to be false enough, and cohere in no part, there is no reason why this discourse should be prolonged further. For those who, following the judgment of a dull sense, abandon the intellect, easily find themselves exploded [[notes: Rejected or hissed off stage]] from the gates of the disciplines.

[[margin: 8]] ¶ Whatever number is led into the terms of a tone: it will leave the interval of a tone.
¶ For since a tone and the interval of a tone consist in a **sesquioctave proportion** [[notes: The 9:8 ratio]], its terms will be nine and eight, or any other numbers that correspond to each other in that same proportion. But since by the seventh [proposition] of the second book of *Arithmetic*: if one and the same number multiplies two others, the proportion of the products is the same as the proportion of the multipliers; therefore, whatever number is led into the terms of the sesquioctave [9 and 8] will produce a sesquioctave and will leave a tone and the interval of a tone. This is what was proposed. And this is to be understood not only regarding the sesquioctave and the interval of a tone, but also regarding any other interval.

[[margin: 9]] ¶ Every number can be established as the difference between the extremes of a tone.
¶ For if a tone is established in its smallest terms, which are nine and eight, the **monad** [[notes: The number one, considered the indivisible unit]] is the difference, since nine and eight differ by only a single unity. And if the number two is led into nine and eight, and *a* and *b* are produced, then by the preceding [rule] *a* and *b* will be the extremes of a tone. But however, by the ninth [proposition] of the first book of *Arithmetic*, that which is made from two times nine is as much as that which is made from two times eight and one. But two times one, by common speech, produces itself; therefore, the number two is established as the difference of the extremes of the tone *a* and *b*. And if the number three is led into nine and eight, by the same argument, three will be established as the difference of the extremes of the tone. And so, whatever other number is led into those same smallest terms of the tone, that same number will be established as the difference of the extremes of the tone. It is therefore manifest that every number can be established as the difference of the extremes of a tone. 

And although this is so, it nevertheless pleased **Philolaus the Pythagorean** [[notes: A 5th-century BCE Greek philosopher and the first to document Pythagorean musical theory]] to establish the origin of the tone and its primordial difference as the number three, which first procreates and gives birth to a **cube** from the first odd number [[notes: In Pythagorean thought, 1 was the source of number; 3 was the first odd number. 3 cubed (3x3x3) is 27]]. This number was highly honorable among the Pythagoreans. For when you have led three, the first odd number, into itself three times, nine arises. Which, led three times, returns twenty-seven; namely, the cube from the first odd number. But 27 and 24 are distant by a tone and enclose the interval of a tone; and the difference of these is established as three. For three is the eighth part of the sum of 24, which, when added to that same sum, restores and completes the first cube from three. And the same Philolaus cut the sum of twenty-seven into two parts, 13 and 14, of which the latter is considered the larger and the former the smaller; and he makes the latter the origin of the **Apotome** [[notes: The "major semitone," literally the "cut-off" part]], which is the larger portion of the tone; but the former he makes the principle of the **diesis**, which is the smaller cut and portion of the tone, and which we shall later call the **minor semitone**. And the difference of those two parts, which is unity, he makes the principle of the **comma** [[notes: An extremely small interval, the mathematical "leftover" in tuning systems]]. But enough of these things.

¶ Although in the case of numbering numbers, which exist as examples in the soul, the unity exists as inseparable; nevertheless, in the case of numbered numbers—such as numbers of motions in physics, lines and planes in geometry, times in astrology, and tones and intervals in music—the unity, or more truly "the one," admits of division. Let *a* therefore be any number whose part denominated by *b* is sought. I resolve *a* into all its parts denominated by *b*: specifically by multiplying the denominator of number *a* into the denominator of the part *b*, so that *c* results. It is manifest that the parts of *c* denominated by *b* together equal the whole number *a*. I divide *c* by *b* and *d* results: I say *d* is the sought part, and is numbered by *a*. That is, by the number denominating *a*. And if it is the part of *a* denominated by *b* that was sought: it is clear because *a* into *d* by the seventh partition reproduces *c*; wherefore *d* is the part of *c* denominated by *b*. But *c* equals *a*. Therefore *d* is also the part of *a* denominated by *b*. 

But that *d* is numbered by *a* is proven: because *a* into *b* produces *c*: therefore by the sixth partition, when *c* is divided by *b*, *a* will return. Or, by the same *c* divided by *b*, *d* previously came forth. Therefore *a* numbers *d* once. And thus the proposition and its corollary are known. ¶ And although this is very easy to demonstrate, its use is somewhat more hidden; an exemplary deduction will easily make it clearer. For example, if the sixteenth part of the sum of seventeen is sought: I multiply the denominators of both into each other. That is, 17 and 16, and 272 sixteenths will arise. For the number 17, when resolved, contains that many sixteenths. I therefore divide 272 by 16, and 17 sixteenths will result, which will be the sixteenth part of the 272 parts of the whole sum. Wherefore they are also the sixteenth part of the number 17. But 17 sixteenths contain a unity and one sixteenth. Therefore, one unity and one sixteenth is the sixteenth part of the given number 17.
