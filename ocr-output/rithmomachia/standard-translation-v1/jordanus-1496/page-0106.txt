# ¶ Five tones exceed two diatessaron consonances by one comma, and thus they turn out to be larger.

¶ The musician Aristoxenus [[notes: A Greek philosopher and pupil of Aristotle who argued that musical intervals should be judged by the ear rather than strictly by mathematical ratios.]] thought that the **diatessaron** consonance [[notes: The interval of a perfect fourth.]] consisted of two whole tones and a full semitone. Consequently, he believed that two **diatessaron** consonances would fill the space of five whole tones. His error is easily proven by the third book of music by the Saint Severinus Boethius [[notes: Boethius (c. 480–524 AD) was the primary authority on music theory in the Middle Ages; his "De institutione musica" transmitted Greek mathematical music theory to the West.]] and by the preceding arguments. 

For according to the preceding section, the **diatessaron** consonance does not consist of two tones and a full semitone, but rather of two tones and a *minor semitone* [[notes: In the Pythagorean system used here, the semitone is not exactly half of a tone. The "minor semitone" (or limma) is slightly smaller than a true mathematical half.]]. This is placed between the 18/19 and 19/20 proportions in the twenty-first [proposition] of the second book. A full semitone, however, would be placed between the 16/17 and 17/18 proportions according to the fourteenth [proposition] of the same book. Therefore, a minor semitone is smaller than a full semitone. 

Since, as was said in the preceding part, the **diatessaron** consonance contains two tones and a minor semitone, it follows that two **diatessaron** consonances will contain four tones and two minor semitones. And because, by the thirty-third [proposition] of the second book, a single whole tone contains two minor semitones and one **comma** [[notes: The "comma" is a tiny, almost imperceptible interval that represents the mathematical discrepancy between different ways of tuning the same note.]], two **diatessaron** consonances therefore contain five tones *minus* one comma. Thus, five whole tones exceed two **diatessaron** consonances by one comma and turn out to be larger, which is what was intended to be shown.

[[margin: 8]]
# ¶ To establish the diapente consonance on an assigned string.
[[notes: "Diapente" is the ancient term for the interval of a perfect fifth.]]

->**Consonance of a diapente.**<-

[[notes: The diagram illustrates a monochord—a single-stringed instrument used for measuring intervals. By dividing the string into three equal parts (a-c, c-d, d-b), the ratio of the whole string (3 parts) to two-thirds of the string (2 parts) creates the perfect fifth.]]

¶ Let **a b** be the assigned number upon which the **diapente** consonance is ordered to be placed. I divide **a b** into three mutually equal parts by the marks **a, c, d, b**, so that **a b** contains three parts and **c b** contains two of them. By definition, the relationship of **a b** to **c b** will be a **hemiolium** or **sesquialter** interval [[notes: Both "hemiolium" and "sesquialter" refer to the ratio of 3:2.]]. But since the **diapente** consonance, by definition, arises from that interval ratio, then **a b** to **c b** will sound a **diapente**. Thus, on the given chord or assigned string, the **diapente** consonance is established in the ratio of **a b** to **c b**.

[[margin: 9]]
# ¶ Three tones are less than a diapente consonance, and four tones exceed that same consonance.

¶ Although it is well enough known from the fifth [proposition] of this book that a **tritone** [[notes: An interval of three whole tones, famous for its dissonant, "unstable" sound.]] cannot produce a **diapente** consonance, it is also shown here that the **tritone** is smaller than the **diapente** consonance. For by the eighteenth [proposition] of the first book, three **sesquioctaves** [[notes: The ratio 9:8, which defines a whole tone.]] are less than a **sesquialter** interval (3:2). Furthermore, by the nineteenth [proposition] of the same book, four **sesquioctaves** exceed the **sesquialter** interval. Since the **diapente** consonance is situated in the **sesquialter** ratio, it follows that three tones consisting of three **sesquioctaves** are less than a **diapente** consonance, and four tones consisting of four **sesquioctaves** transcend that same consonance in size. This is the whole of what was proposed.

[[margin: 10]]
# ¶ The diapente consonance consists of three tones and a minor semitone. From this, it follows that if a tone is subtracted from a diapente, a diatessaron consonance remains; if a diatessaron is subtracted, a tone remains.

¶ According to the fifteenth [proposition] of the first book, if a **sesquitertium** interval (4:3) is taken away from a **sesquialter** interval (3:2), a **sesquioctave** (9:8) will be left behind. As was seen in the demonstration of the sixth [proposition] of this book, a **sesquitertium** (the fourth) contains two tones with a minor semitone. Therefore, the **diapente** consonance—which contains a **sesquioctave** (that is, a tone) beyond those two tones and a minor semitone—will consist of three tones and a minor semitone, just as was proposed. 

The **corollary** is thus recognized: for the **diapente**, by this present proof, contains three tones with a minor semitone. If a tone is subtracted, the remainder is two tones and a minor semitone; and by the sixth [proposition] of this book, two tones with a minor semitone constitute a **diatessaron** consonance. Therefore, if a tone is subtracted from a **diapente** consonance, a **diatessaron** remains. Likewise, since a **diapente** consists of three tones with a minor semitone, if a **diatessaron** consonance (which is completed by two tones and a minor semitone) is subtracted, a whole tone will be left over, as the second part of the corollary proposes. This is the entirety of the corollary.

---

[[margin: 11]]
# ¶ The diapente consonance contains less than eight minor semitones.

¶ Since one whole tone contains two minor semitones and one **comma**, three tones and one minor semitone will contain seven minor semitones and three **commas**. And since three **commas**, by the thirty-fifth [proposition] of the second book, are narrower than a minor semitone, the **diapente**—which by the preceding proof consists of three tones and a minor semitone—will contain less than eight minor semitones. Just as it has been easily shown that the **diapente** consonance does not yet reach the eighth minor semitone or the eighth **diesis** [[notes: A "diesis" is a very small interval, often used to describe the difference between major and minor semitones.]], so it would be just as easy to show that the same consonance does not yet reach the seventh **apotome** [[notes: An "apotome" is the "major semitone," which is slightly larger than the "minor semitone" or limma.]].

---

[[margin: 12]]
# ¶ The diapente consonance is joined from a ditone and a sesquitone.
[[notes: A "ditone" is a major third (two tones), and a "sesquitone" is a minor third (one and a half tones).]]

¶ By the penultimate [proposition], the **diapente** consists of three tones and a minor semitone. A **ditone** and a **sesquitone** together also make three tones and a minor semitone. Therefore, a **ditone** and a **sesquitone** coupled together equally join to form a **diapente** consonance, which is what was intended.

# ¶ The difference between the diapente and diatessaron consonances is a tone. From this, it follows that if a tone is added to a diatessaron consonance, it restores the diapente.
