Let there be any ratio of **a** to **b**, where **a** is the larger term and **b** the smaller. I say that taking **a** as many times as the smaller number **b** is equal to taking **b** as many times as the larger number **a**. For according to the eighth proposition of the first book of *Arithmetica* [[notes: This refers to the commutative property of multiplication ($a \times b = b \times a$), likely citing Boethius or Jordanus de Nemore]], the product of **a** times **b** is equal to the product of **b** times **a**. Since the product of **a** times **b** is simply **a** summed according to the number **b**, and the product of **b** times **a** is **b** summed according to the number **a**, it follows that **a** summed **b** times equals **b** summed **a** times. In any other instances, the demonstration and the proposition remain the same.

[[margin: 10]]
When several larger terms are equal to several smaller terms: the ratio of one of the larger terms to one of the smaller is the same as the ratio of the count of the smaller terms to the count of the larger terms.

This is the converse of the preceding statement. Since, by the previous rule, the smaller terms collected according to the number of the larger are equal to the larger terms collected according to the number of the smaller. But the number of larger terms is one larger term, and the number of smaller terms is one smaller term [[notes: The author is explaining how to flip a ratio. For example, if 2 strings of length 'A' equal 3 strings of length 'B', the ratio of the lengths A:B is 3:2]]. Therefore, the ratio of one larger term to one smaller is the same as the ratio of the count of the collected smaller terms to the count of the collected larger terms. Although these last two conclusions are simple, their application is somewhat more fundamental. 

By the first of these, which is the ninth, we hold that if the larger number is to the smaller in a **sesquialter** ratio [[notes: A ratio of 3:2, representing a perfect musical Fifth]] (as 3 is to 2), then two larger terms are equal to three smaller ones. If the larger is to the smaller in a **sesquitertian** ratio (as 4 is to 3), then three larger terms are equal to four smaller ones [[notes: A ratio of 4:3, representing a perfect musical Fourth]]. If the larger is **sesquiquartan** (as 5 to 4), then four majors equal five minors. If the larger is **sesquioctave** (9 to 8), then eight majors equal nine minors; and so it goes in similar cases. 

In **multiples**, however, if the larger is double (as 2 to 1), then one larger term equals two smaller ones. If triple, one major equals three. If quadruple, one equals four. If octuple, one equals eight. In **superpartients** [[notes: A ratio where the larger number contains the smaller number plus more than one "part" of it, e.g., 5:3]], it is similar: if the larger is superbipartient (as 5 to 3), then three majors equal five minors. If supertriquartant (7 to 4), then four majors equal seven minors. If indeed it is superoctupartient (17 to 9), then nine majors equal seventeen minors, and in this manner for the rest.

From the last rule, we derive this method for using **superparticular** ratios [[notes: Ratios of the form $(n+1):n$]]. If two majors are equal to three minors—or 4 to 6, 6 to 9, or 8 to 12—then one major to one minor is a **sesquialter** (3:2). If three are equal to four (or 6 to 8), the major to the minor is **sesquitertian** (4:3). If four majors equal five minors (or 8 to 10), one major is **sesquiquartan** to one minor. If eight majors equal nine minors, one major to one minor is known as **sesquioctave** (9:8). 

In **multiples**: if one equals two (or 2 to 4, 3 to 6), the major is double the minor. If one equals three (or 2 to 6), one major is triple the minor. If one to four (and 2 to 8), one major to one minor is quadruple. If one to eight (and 2 to 16), the major is octuple the minor. 

In **superpartients**: if three majors equal five minors (or 6 to 10), one major to minor is superbipartient (5:3). If four majors equal seven minors, the major is supertriquartant (7:4) to the minor. If nine are equal to seventeen, the major is superoctupartient to the minor. 

The same applies to **composite numbers**. For instance, if two **a** are equal to four **b** and a half of one—as in the case of two joined sesquialters [[notes: Compounding two 3:2 ratios: $3/2 \times 3/2 = 9/4$. 9 contains 4 twice, plus 1/4 of 4]]—since 4 and a half of one compared to 2 is double and a sesquiquartan, therefore one **a** to one **b** is double and a sesquiquartan ($2 + 1/4$). If three **a** equal four **b** and a third of one—as in two sesquitertians [[notes: $4/3 \times 4/3 = 16/9$]]—since 5 and a third of one contain 3 once, plus two-thirds and one-ninth, then one **a** will contain **b** once, plus its two-thirds and its one-ninth [[notes: $16/9 = 1 + 7/9$; $7/9$ is equal to $6/9 (2/3) + 1/9$]]. If four are equal to six and a fourth of one—as in two joined sesquiquartans [[notes: $5/4 \times 5/4 = 25/16$]]—since 6 and a fourth contain 4 once, plus a half and a sixteenth of four, therefore one major contains the minor once, plus its half, and its one-sixteenth. If eight are worth ten and an eighth of one—as in two sesquioctaves [[notes: $9/8 \times 9/8 = 81/64$]]—since 10 contains 8 once plus a fourth, and the eighth of one compared to eight is one sixty-fourth, therefore one major contains the minor once, plus its fourth part and its one sixty-fourth part. 

And if the series of composition becomes more extended: if two **a** are equal to six **b**, plus a half and a fourth of one—as in the joining of three sesquialters [[notes: $(3/2)^3 = 27/8 = 3.375$]]—since 6 contains 2 three times, and the half of one is the fourth part of two, and the fourth of one is the eighth part of two; therefore one **a** is triple to one **b**, containing in addition the fourth and eighth part of **b** [[notes: $3 + 1/4 + 1/8 = 3 + 2/8 + 1/8 = 3.375$]]. If three are equal to seven and a ninth of one—which happens with three sesquitertians [[notes: $(4/3)^3 = 64/27$]]—since 7 contains 3 twice plus a third of three, and the ninth of one to three is one twenty-seventh; therefore one major contains the minor twice, plus its third, and its one twenty-seventh. If four equal seven and a **dodrans** [[notes: A "dodrans" is three-fourths (3/4)]] of one (that is, three-fourths) and furthermore a sixteenth of one—as in three joined sesquiquartans [[notes: $(5/4)^3 = 125/64$]]—since 7 contains 4 once and three parts of four, and three-fourths of one to four are three sixteenths, and a sixteenth of one to four is one sixty-fourth; therefore one major contains one minor once, plus a dodrans, plus three sixteenths and one sixty-fourth. If eight are equal to eleven, three-eighths of one, and one sixty-fourth of one—as in three joined sesquioctaves [[notes: $(9/8)^3 = 729/512$]]—since 11 contains 8 once and three parts of eight, and three-eighths of one to eight are three sixty-fourths, and one sixty-fourth to eight is one five-hundred-and-twelfth; hence it follows that one major contains the minor once, plus its three-eighths, three sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth. 

We have added these points so that the use of these last two rules might be held more clearly and manifest in what follows.
