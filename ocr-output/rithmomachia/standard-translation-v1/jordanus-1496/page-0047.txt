That no others besides these are even-times-even is clear. For if another were given: divide it by half, and its half by half, and so on until a number is encountered that can no longer be divided. If this number were odd, since by the twenty-third of the first it will measure the final [original] number, the given number would not be even-times-even, as was supposed. 30 **¶ If an even-times-even is multiplied by an even-times-even: an even-times-even will be produced.**
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two even-times-even numbers: let one be multiplied by the other and let **c** result. I say that **c** is an even-times-even number. For if **c** is not even-times-even: then let some odd number, if it is possible, measure it, which let be **d**. Because, therefore, the odd **d** measures the product **c** from two undivided numbers **a** and **b**: therefore, by the fifth of the third, the odd **d** shares a factor with either **a** or **b**. Therefore, whatever number it is will commonly measure **d** and **a** or **b**. Because **d** is posed as odd: by the thirteenth of this book, it will be odd. Therefore, an odd number will measure **a** or **b**, namely an even-times-even number. This is impossible [[notes: Because by definition, powers of two have no odd divisors]]. Therefore, **c** will not be measured by an odd number; and since by the twelfth of this book it is even, by definition it is established that it is even-times-even. Which is what was proposed.

31 **¶ Whatever measures an even-times-even: is itself also even-times-even.**
¶ Let **a** be even-times-even, which any number **b** measures: I say that **b** is an even-times-even number. For if **b** were an odd number, since it is posed to measure **a**, **a** would not be an even-times-even number. This is against the hypothesis. Therefore, **b** will be an even number; if it were not even-times-even, some odd number would measure it. Wherefore that same odd, by the twenty-third of the first, would measure the even-times-even number **a**. This is impossible. It is manifest, therefore, that no odd measures **b**. Wherefore by definition it is even-times-even.

32 **¶ If any number of even-times-even numbers starting from unity are taken: all of them, along with unity added to them, will produce a number one less than the [next] even-times-even.**
¶ Let there be unity, and **a**, **b**, **c**, **d**, **e** even-times-even numbers proceeding in order: I say that unity, **a**, **b**, **c**, **d** aggregated together constitute a number one less than **e** [[notes: This is the formula for the sum of a geometric progression: $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 = 32 - 1$]]. For by the twenty-ninth of this book, each of the following is double the preceding: wherefore the following is as if the preceding were doubled. But **a**, the second from unity, exceeds it by one; wherefore unity and **a** are one less than **b**, which is **a** doubled. Therefore, one less than **b**. Wherefore unity, **a**, and **b** are one less than **b** doubled: therefore [one less] than **c**. Wherefore unity, **a**, **b**, and **c** are one less than **d**. Wherefore again unity, **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** together are one less than **e**. Which is what was proposed.

33 **¶ Every number whose half is odd: is even-times-odd.**
¶ Let **a** be an even number, whose half is the odd number **c**, and **b** be two: I say **a** is an even-times-odd number [[notes: *Pariter impar*; numbers of the form $2 \times \text{odd}$, such as 6, 10, 14]]. For because **c** is the half of **a**: therefore the two **b** will measure **a** according to it [c]. I say again that whatever other even number measures **a**, it measures it according to an odd number. And let it be that another even **c** measures it according to **d**: I say **d** is odd. For because that which is made from **d** into **e** is **a**, and that which is from **b** into **c** is likewise **a**: therefore by the twenty-fifth of the second, the proportion of **d** to **b** is the same as **c** to **e**. Therefore, by permutation, the proportion of **d** to **c** is that of **b** to **e**. But since every even number other than two is made by continually adding two, first to two and then to its predecessor: therefore two measures every even number. **b** will therefore measure **c**: wherefore also **d** will measure an odd; it is therefore, by the thirteenth of this book, odd. Therefore, whatever even measures **a**, measures it according to an odd: wherefore by definition **a** is even-times-odd. Which is what was proposed.

34 **¶ If an odd is multiplied by an even-times-odd number: that which results will be even-times-odd.**
¶ Let **a** be an odd number, and **b** an even-times-odd: and **c** the number produced from **a** into **b**. I say **c** is an even-times-odd number: for let **d** be the half of **c**; and since by the twelfth of this book it is even, and **e** is two. Because **e** into **d** makes **c** and likewise **a** into **b** makes **c**: therefore by the twenty-fifth of the second, the proportion of **e** to **a** is as **b** to **d**. Wherefore by permutation, the proportion of **e** to **b** is as **a** to **d**. But the two **e** is [contained] oddly in **b**, since **b** is even-times-odd. Therefore also the odd **a** is [contained] oddly in **d**. And **a** measures the number **d** according to an odd. Therefore by the eleventh of this book **d** is odd, and **c** by the preceding [proposition]: since its half **d** is odd, it will be even-times-odd. Which is what was proposed.

35 **¶ From even numbers arranged from unity, the third from the first, and the third from that, with one always intervening, all those taken as thirds are even-times-odd only.**
¶ Let the even numbers be continued from unity: and let them be **a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k, l, m**: and so following. I say the third **c**, and the [next] third from it, and **g** again the third from this, and so on, with one always intervening, are even-times-odd. For by the eighteenth of this book, the halves of those evens are numbers arranged from unity in natural order: of which one is even and the other odd, placed alternately. Of the first of the evens, i.e., two, since its half is unity which is potentially odd: it will also be potentially even-times-odd. The second from it will not be even-times-odd: because its half is even. But **c**, the third, and the seventh and ninth, will be even-times-odd by the penultimate of this book, since their halves are odd. Which is the proposal as to this. Nor will any other evens be even-times-odd: because their halves are even, so that even like two will measure them according to an even: wherefore by definition they will not be even-times-odd. Which is the whole proposition.

36 **¶ Of even-times-odd numbers taken continually: the differences are equal.**
¶ This is because if all evens are taken continually, the difference of all of them will be the same: namely two. Therefore, the parts being taken according to the requirement of the preceding and continually even-times-odd with one intervening: by perception, their differences will be composed from the differences of those same numbers to the middle ones: which since the middle ones are equal, the totals will also be equal by perception. Which is what was proposed.
