¶ **Between any two similar solid numbers, two numbers fall between as mean proportionals in continued proportion.**
[[notes: "Similar solid numbers" are the product of three sides that are proportional to each other, like $2 \times 3 \times 4$ and $4 \times 6 \times 8$. Jordanus is proving that if you have two such "volumes," you can always find two numbers to bridge them in a steady geometric scale (e.g., $a, ar, ar^2, ar^3$).]]
¶ For by the second-to-last proposition, the ratio of one of those solids to the other will be as the ratio of its side to the side of the other, tripled [[notes: "Tripled" here means cubed, or the ratio multiplied by itself three times]]. I shall therefore take four numbers in the ratio of side to side. And because the ratio of the first to the fourth will be the ratio of side to side tripled, that ratio will be the same as the ratio of solid to solid. Therefore, by the twelfth [proposition] of the fourth [book], as many means as are between the first and the last, so many will there be between solid and solid. But there are two means: therefore between solid and solid there will likewise be two: which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 53]]
¶ **If there are four numbers in continued proportion, the two extremes will be similar solid numbers.**
¶ This is the converse of the preceding. Let **a**, **b**, **c**, **d** be four numbers in continued proportion: I say that **a** and **d** are similar solids. For I take **e**, **f**, **g**, **h** as the four smallest numbers of that proportion; if these are the same as the assigned numbers, then by the second [proposition] of this [book], since **e** and **h** are cubes, likewise **a** and **d** will be cubes. Wherefore they are similar solids and the proposition stands firm. Furthermore, if they are not the same, since **e** and **h** by the fifth [proposition] of the fourth [book] are prime to each other: wherefore by the twenty-third [proposition] they are the smallest in their proportion, and by the nineteenth of the same they will measure **a** and **d** equally. But since **e** and **h** are cubes by the second [proposition] of this book, they are similar solids. Therefore, again by the fifty-first [proposition] of this book, **a** and **d** are similar solids: which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 54]]
¶ **Of any two similar solid numbers, the ratio of one to the other is as a cube to a cube.**
¶ Let **a** and **d** be similar solids: I say the ratio of **a** to **d** is as the ratio of a cube to a cube. Because by the fifty-second [proposition] of this book there are two proportional means between **a** and **d**. Let those be **b** and **c**: I take, as before, **e**, **f**, **g**, **h** as the smallest numbers in that proportion, which by the second [proposition] of this book it is established that **e** and **h** are cubes; if these are the same as the aforesaid, the proposition is established. If not the same: because by equal proportionality, the ratio of **e** to **h** (cube to cube) is the same as **a** to **d**: which again is what was proposed.

[[margin: 55]]
¶ **But if the ratio of one to the other is as a cube to a cube, it is necessary that those two solid numbers be similar.**
¶ This is the converse of the preceding, which is proved true for this reason: because by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book], between any cubes two proportional means are introduced. Therefore, by the twelfth [proposition] of the fourth [book], two proportional means will likewise lie between those solids. Therefore, by the fifty-third [proposition] of this book, the two extremes, which will be the assigned solids, will be similar solids: which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 56]]
¶ **If two solids are similar to one [same] solid, they will also be similar to each other.**
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two solids similar to solid **c**: I say **a** and **b** are solids similar to each other. For because **a** and **c** are solids similar to each other: therefore by the fifty-second [proposition] of this book there are two proportional means between **a** and **c**, which let be **d** and **e**. And likewise by the same [proposition] between **b** and **c**, which let be **f** and **g**. Therefore, by the seventeenth [proposition] of the fourth [book], two [means] will similarly be between **a** and **b**. Wherefore **a** and **b**, by the fifty-third [proposition] of this book, are similar solids to each other: which was to be demonstrated.

[[margin: 57]]
¶ **If there are four proportional numbers, and each of the two extremes is multiplied by the square of the other, the cubes of the others will result.**
[[notes: This is a complex way of stating a relationship in a geometric progression $a, b, c, d$. It suggests that $a \times (\text{square of } d)$ and $d \times (\text{square of } a)$ relate to the cubes of $b$ and $c$.]]
¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, **d** be four numbers in continued proportion: let **e** be the square of **a** and **f** the square of **d**, and let **d** be multiplied by **e** to produce **g**, and **a** by **f** to produce **h**. I say, therefore, that **g** and **h** are the cubes of **b** and **c**. For I multiply **b** by itself and let it become **k**: because **e** and **k** are squares whose sides are **a** and **b**, by the third [proposition] of this book the ratio of **e** to **k** will be as the ratio of **a** to **b** doubled: wherefore as **b** to **d**: therefore by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the product of **b** times **k** and **e** times **d** is the same. But that which is from **b** times **k** is the cube of **b**. That which is from **e** times **d** is **g**: therefore **g** is the cube of **b**. Similarly, if **c** is multiplied by itself and becomes **l**, by the same third [proposition] of this book the ratio of **l** to **f** will be as **a** to **b** doubled. Wherefore as **a** to **c**: wherefore by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], as before, the product of **c** times **l** and **a** times **f** will be the same: but that which is from **c** times **l** is the cube of **c**: and that which is from **a** times **f** is **h**. Therefore **h** is the cube of **c**: which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 58]]
¶ **Given the sides or the ratios of the sides, to investigate the sum and relationship of the solids.**
¶ This holds the same certainty of truth in continuous and discrete solids alike. If the sides are given first, multiply those three of the one and three of the other by each other in any way, and the sums of the solids will immediately result by the forty-eighth [proposition] of this book. But if the ratios of the sides are given, compose the ratios of the sides by the third [proposition] of the fifth [book]: or make them continuous by the sixth [proposition] of the fifth [book], and the ratio composed from the ratios of the sides by the fiftieth [proposition] of this book, or that which is composed from the continuous ones, will be the ratio of solid to solid; thus the relationship of the solids will be known, and everything the proposition proposes is known.

An **even number** is one that can be divided into two equal parts. An **odd number** is one in which some first part is without a pair, and it exceeds an even number by one unit. Among even numbers, one is **even-times-even**, another **even-times-odd**, and another **odd-times-even**.
[[notes: These definitions come from Greek arithmetic (like Nicomachus). "Even-times-even" refers to powers of 2 (2, 4, 8, 16...). "Even-times-odd" refers to numbers like 6 or 10 (an even number times an odd number).]]
**Even-times-even** is that which no odd number measures [[notes: i.e., has no odd divisors]]. **Even-times-odd** is that which, whatever even numbers measure it, they measure it according to an odd number. **Odd-times-even** is that which some measure as even according to an even [number] and some according to an odd [number].
A number is called **mean** between two numbers when, placed between them, it is equally distant from both. Two numbers are called **means** which, having no other placed between them, have equal differences to the extremes.
A **perfect number** is so called which is completed from all its parts joined together [[notes: e.g., 6 = 1+2+3]]. It is called **abundant** if all its parts compared [added] together are found to have more. It is **deficient** [diminutus] when all its parts joined together constitute less than itself.
