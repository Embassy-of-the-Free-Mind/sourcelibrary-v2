**2 If from any triangular number its side is subtracted, the triangle immediately below it remains.**

[[image-desc: A triangular arrangement of dots (tetractys) consisting of 21 dots arranged in six rows (base of 6, vertex of 1). The base corners are labeled 'a' and 'b'.]]

¶ A triangle "immediately below" another is one whose side [[notes: The "side" (latus) of a triangular number refers to the number of units in its base row, which is also the total number of rows]] is smaller by one than the side of the other. Therefore, from triangle **a b**, if the side **a b** is subtracted: I say that the triangle immediately below **a b** remains. For since triangle **a** (by the preceding [proposition]) has three equal sides, and the following [rows] increase only by unity; therefore, when side **a b** is subtracted, the remaining sides will be shorter by one. But because the rows of units always decrease by one unit, it is clear by the corollary of the preceding [proposition] that the remainder is triangular. And since its side is known to be exceeded by the side of triangle **a b** by one unit, it is evident that the remaining triangle is the one immediately below triangle **a b** in descending order; which is what was proposed.

**3 Every doubled triangular number produces an oblong number.**

[[image-desc: A rectangular/rhombic arrangement of dots (30 dots total, in a 6 by 5 grid oriented diagonally). The vertices and internal points are labeled with letters: 'b' at the top, 'e' on the left, 'f' on the right, and 'o', 'c' near the center.]]

¶ Let **a** and **b** be two equal triangular numbers. If the side **c** of triangle **a** is joined to the final unity of triangle **b**, and likewise the side **f** of triangle **b** is joined to the final unity of triangle **a**, two sides will be formed, each exceeding the side of triangles **a** and **b** by one unit. And if you fit and adapt the middle rows of one to the middle rows of the other in this way, then by the third [proposition] of the first [book], the rows will become equal to each other and to the outer rows **e**, **f**, and **c d**. For example: if you add and adapt the row following side **c** to the row following the unity **o**. Since, by the corollary of the first [proposition] of this [book], the row following side **c** is as far from **c** as the row following the unity **d** is from **d**. Therefore, by the third of the first [book], those joined together are equal to the row **c d**. The same follows for the other middle rows, if any remain. Thus, a complete quadrangle [[notes: a rectangle]] will be formed whose opposite sides are equal and therefore parallel. As side **e f** is shown to be equal to side **c d** and the middle rows, the units of side **b d** will be parallel to the units of side **a e**. Similarly, because **b d** equals side **e a** (for they are sides of equal triangles and middle rows), side **e f** will be parallel to side **c d**. And because sides **b d** and **e a** are opposite and parallel sides of the assigned triangles, and the two remaining sides **e f** and **c d** are similarly opposite and parallel—each of which is one unit greater than the others [[notes: In this context, "oblong" (altera parte longior) means a rectangle where the length is $n+1$ and the width is $n$]]—it is clear by definition that the whole figure **a b d e**, constituted from two equal triangular numbers (and thus from one triangular number doubled), is an oblong number; which is what was proposed.

**4 Half of any oblong number is a triangular number.**

[[image-desc: A diagram showing an overlapping pattern of dots representing two triangles combined into a rectangle. Labels include 'a', 'b', 'o', 'g', 'e', 'f'.]]

¶ This is the converse of the preceding. Let there be any oblong number **a b e**, and let its longer side be **a b**. I say that half of it is triangular. From the longer side **a b**, I subtract one unit **a**, and from the second row two, from the third three, and from the fourth four. If there are more, I will take away as many as the row is distant from unity; by the corollary of the first [proposition] of this book, the whole subtracted part, such as **a e f**, is triangular. And by the same [logic], because it consistently left one less until it reached unity, the remainder is triangular. But also, because the side of the subtracted part **e f** is equal to the side of the remainder **g b** (for side **g e** exceeded side **g b** by one unit, but **g e** also exceeds side **f e** by one unit). Therefore, **g b** and **f e** are equal. Thus, the remaining parts of one are equal to the remaining parts of the other, since by the first [proposition] of this book every triangle is equilateral, and the whole triangle **a e f** is equal to the whole triangle **a b g**. Therefore, the given oblong number is divided into two halves, each of which is triangular; which is what was proposed.

**5 Any two adjacent triangular numbers constitute a square.**

[[image-desc: A rhombic grid of dots representing a square (tetragonum) composed of two triangular numbers. Labels: 'a', 'b', 'o', 'g', 'f', 'e', 'h', 'k'.]]

¶ As in the preceding, let **a b g e** be any oblong number, which by the same [logic] is divided into two equal triangles **b g h** and **e a f**. It follows that **a e** is the side of triangle **a e f**, and side **b a** of the oblong number and its opposite **e g** are greater than side **a e** and the following rows by one unit. Therefore, when side **a e** is removed, there will remain **b h** equal to side **h k**, as well as **g** and **g b**. And so those four sides are equidistant from one another; therefore, what remains by definition is a square (*tetragonus*). But also, by the second [proposition] of this book, when side **a e** is removed from triangle **a e f**, the triangle immediately below **a e f** remains. Thus, it is also the neighbor to the equal triangle **h b g**. It is therefore established that the square **h k g b** consists of two adjacent triangular numbers; which is what was proposed. 

¶ This may also be known from what the holy Severinus [[notes: Referring to Boethius (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, c. 477–524 AD), whose work *De Arithmetica* was the foundational text for medieval number theory]] provides in the thirty-fifth chapter of his *Arithmetic*: that every square becomes an oblong number if its own side is added to it or subtracted from it. For since every square possesses equal sides on all sides: if one side is added to any [square], the sides will be made to differ by one unit. Furthermore, if one side is subtracted from any [square], the remaining sides will differ by one unit. Therefore, in both cases, oblong numbers are produced. From this it follows again that any adjacent oblong numbers are distant from a middle square by an equal difference. For an equal side was subtracted and added.
