[[margin: 25]]
¶ If the first is in the second as many times as the fourth is in the sixth; and likewise the third is in the fourth as many times as the second is in the fifth: it is necessary that the first is in the fifth as many times as the third is in the sixth.

¶ Let there be, as before, six numbers: **a** the first, **b** the second, **c** the third, **d** the fourth, **e** the fifth, and **f** the sixth. And let **a** be the same part of **b** that **d** is of **f**, and let **c** be the same part of **d** that **b** is of **e**. I say that **a** is the same part of **e** that **c** is of **f**. This is demonstrated entirely as the preceding one. [[notes: This proposition establishes a "chain rule" for proportions. If $b/a = f/d$ and $d/c = e/b$, then it follows that $e/a = f/c$.]]

[[margin: 26]]
¶ If the first is the same part of the second as the third is of the fourth; and likewise the first is the same part of the fifth as the third is of the sixth; then the first will be the same part of [the sum of] the second and fifth as the third is of [the sum of] the fourth and sixth.

¶ Let there be six numbers: **a** the first, **b** the second, **c** the third, **d** the fourth, **e** the fifth, and **f** the sixth. And let the first be in the second as the third is in the fourth, and the first in the fifth as the third in the sixth. I say that the first **a** is the same part of the second **b** and the fifth **e** [combined] as the third **c** is of the fourth **d** and the sixth **f**. Because, by the hypothesis, the first is in the second as the third is in the fourth, let **g** be the number naming how many times **a** is in **b** and **c** is in **d**. And because, also by the same hypothesis, the first is in the fifth as the third is in the sixth, let **h** be the number naming for both of them how many times **a** is in **e** and **c** is in **f**. And let there be a number **12** composed of **g** and **h**. Then thus: that which is produced from the multiplication of **a** by **g** and **h** is as much as **b** and **e**. And by the ninth [proposition], what is produced from **a** by **g** and **h** is as much as what is produced from **a** by the number **12** composed of them. Similarly, what is produced from **c** by **g** and **h** is as much as **d** and **f**, and it is what is produced from **c** by **12**. Therefore, the first **a** is the same part of the second **b** and the fifth **e** as the third **c** is of the fourth **d** and the sixth **f**, as they are numbered in those according to the same number **12**: which is what the proposition intends.

[[margin: 27]]
¶ If the first is the same part of the second as the third is of the fourth, and the first numbers are smaller, then the first will be the same part or parts of the third as the second is of the fourth. [[notes: In this context, "a part" (*pars*) refers to a unit fraction like 1/3, while "parts" (*partes*) refers to a non-unit proper fraction like 2/3.]]

¶ Let **a** be the first number, **b** the second, **d** the third, and **e** the fourth. Let **a** and **b** together be the smaller numbers, and whatever part **a** is of **b**, let **c** be that same part, and let **d** be the same part of **e f**. I say, therefore, that whatever part or parts **b** may be of **e f**, the same part or parts of the number **a** shall be **d**. For I divide **b** into its equal parts **a**, which are **b** and **c**. Likewise, I divide **e f** into its equal parts **d**. And because **e f** is the same multiple of **d** as **b** is of **a** by hypothesis, therefore there will be as many parts in number of **e f** as were placed for **b c**. Let those be **e** and **f**. Since, therefore, **b** (the first part of **b c**) is equal to **a**, and **e** (the first part of **e f**) is equal to **d**, then whatever part or parts **b** is of **e**, the same part or parts **a** is of **d**. And so for part **c** with respect to part **f**, and the remaining parts of one to the remaining parts of the other, if there were more. Understand, therefore, **b** to be the first number, **c** the second, **e** the third, and **f** the fourth. And because **b** is parts of **e**, I argue by the fifth [proposition]: because the first **b** is proven to be the same parts of the second **e** as the third **c** is of the fourth **f**. Therefore, **b c** (the first and third) are the same parts of **e f** (the second and fourth) as the first **b** is of the second **e**. But **a** to **d** shows the same part as **b** to **e**. Therefore, it is also the same as **b c** (the second) is to **e f** (the fourth). And if **b** were a part [a unit fraction], I would draw the argument in an entirely similar way through the fourth [proposition], as has now been done through the fifth. It is manifest, therefore, that if the first is in the second as the third is in the fourth, with the first being smaller: the first is the same part or parts of the third as the second is of the fourth.

[[margin: 28]]
¶ If the first is the same parts of the second as the third is of the fourth, with the first and second being smaller, the first will be the same part or parts of the third as the second is of the fourth.

¶ Let there be four numbers: **a** the first, **c** the second, **d** the third, and **f** the fourth. Of these, let the two first, **a** and **c**, be smaller than the remaining two; and let **a b** be the same parts of **c** as **d e** is of **f**. I say that **a b** is the same part or parts of **c e** as **c** is of **f**. For I divide **a b** into its parts of **c**, which are **a** and **b**, and similarly **d e** into its parts of **f**, which are **d** and **e**. Since, therefore, by hypothesis **a b** is as many parts of **c** as **d e** is of **f**, there will be as many parts in the number of the divided **a b** as in **d e**. And **a**, the first part of **a b**, will be the same part of its whole **c** as **d**, the first part of **d e**, is of its whole **f**. Wherefore, by the preceding [proposition], **a** (the first part of **a b**) is to **d** (the first part of **d e**) as the same part or parts as **c** is to **f**. And similarly **b** (the second part of the first) to **e** (the second part of the third) is the same part or parts as **c** is to **f**. And so for the remaining parts of the first to the remaining parts of the third, if there be more parts. But if **a** is a part of **b**, I make **a** the first number, **d** the second, **b** the third, and **e** the fourth. And I argue by the fourth [proposition]: because **a** is the same part of **d** as **b** is of **e**. Therefore, **a b** is the same part of **d e** as **a** is of **d**. But **a** to **d** was shown to be the same part as **c** to **f**. Therefore, the first **a b** to the third **d e** is the same part as the second **c** to the fourth **f**. But if **a** to **d** were "parts" [a non-unit fraction], you would argue in an entirely similar way through the fifth, just as the argument for a "part" was just established through the fourth. It is plain, therefore, that the proposition is solid. And if it happened that the first two numbers were larger than the last two, the conclusion would be reached in the inverse way: that the third is the same part or parts of the first as the fourth is of the second; which can easily be shown by permuting the order, namely by making the last two the first and the first and second the last.

[[margin: 29]]
¶ Every smaller number is a part of a larger one; or, when it is subtracted from the larger as many times as possible, what remains will be either a part or parts of it. [[notes: This is a fundamental definition of the relationship between any two integers, essentially describing the "remainder" in division.]]

¶ Let **a** and **b** be two numbers, **a** the larger and **b** the smaller. I say that **b** is either a part of **a**, or when it is subtracted as many times as possible from **a**, there will remain either a part or parts of **b**. For if the smaller **b** measures the larger **a**, by definition **b** is a part of **a**. If otherwise, however, **b** does not measure **a**. Therefore, when **b** is subtracted as many times as possible from **a**, there will remain something smaller than **b**. For if nothing remained, **b** would measure **a** according to the number naming how many times it was subtracted. For as many times as any number can be subtracted from another, so many times is it numerable in it, according to the sixth petition; if there remains something smaller than **b**, by the first proposition, it is either a part or parts of **b**: which is what was proposed.
