[[meta: This page continues the systematic exploration of ratios and proportions. On the previous page, the author discussed how to combine and compare ratios. Here, he moves into more complex "doubled" (squared) ratios and provides proofs for how ratios behave when numbers are in "continued proportion"—what we would today call a geometric progression (like 2, 4, 8, 16).]]

¶ Let the terms of the greater ratio be **a** and **b**, and the terms of the smaller ratio be **c** and **d**. Let **a** and **c** be the larger numbers. The goal is to find a third ratio which, when doubled [[notes: In the mathematical language of this period, "doubling" a ratio means squaring it—multiplying the ratio by itself.]], is greater than the smaller ratio and smaller than the larger ratio. First, I multiply **a** by **c**, and **d** by **a** [[notes: The OCR 'r' here likely represents 'a' or a similar variable used in the multiplication]], let the results be **e** and **f**. Then I multiply **b** by **c**, let the result be **g**. It is clear, by the seventh proposition of the second book [[notes: A reference to an earlier proof in this manuscript or a standard textbook like Euclid's *Elements*]], that **e** is to **f** as **c** is to **d**. And by the eighth proposition of the same book, **e** is to **g** as **a** is to **b**. And because the ratio of **e** to **f** is a smaller ratio than **c** to **d**, therefore by the tenth proposition of the second book, **f** is greater than **g**. Then I multiply **f** by **e**, and by **f**, and by **g**, producing **h**, **k**, and **l**. By the seventh proposition of the second book, these will be in the same ratio as **e**, **f**, and **g**; specifically, **h** to **k** is as **e** to **f**, and **h** to **l** is as **e** to **g**. Then I multiply **e** by **e**, producing **m**. Then, because **f** times **e** makes **h**, and **e** times itself makes **m**, therefore by the eighth proposition of the second book, **m** is to **h** as **e** is to **f**, which is also as **h** is to **k**. If, therefore, the ratio of **m** to **k**—which is composed of two ratios equal to the assigned smaller ratio—is smaller than the ratio of **h** to **l**, I seek some number like **o**, which when multiplied by itself produces a number **p** that is greater than **h** and smaller than or equal to **m**. I say that the ratio of **o** to **f** is the one whose doubled ratio is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d** and smaller than the ratio of **a** to **b**. For since **f** times itself makes **l**, and **o** times itself makes **p**, then by those things demonstrated at the beginning of the third proposition of this book, the ratio of **o** to **f** is added to itself. Therefore, the ratio of **p** to **k** is the double of the ratio of **o** to **f**. And since **p** is greater than **h**, the ratio of **p** to **k**, by the ninth proposition of the second book, is greater than the ratio of **h** to **l**. Therefore it is also [greater than] the ratio of **c** to **d**. And since the ratio of **m** to **l** was smaller than the ratio of **b** to **l**, the ratio of **p** to **l** (which is double the assigned ratio of **o** to **f**) would be smaller than that same ratio of **h** to **l**, and thus smaller than the ratio of **a** to **b**, as was proposed. But if the ratio of **m** to **l** is greater than the ratio of **h** to **l**, then their difference will be **m** to **n**. Then the ratio of **n** to **l** will be equal to the ratio of **h** to **l**. And then I will seek some number like **o**, which when multiplied by itself produces **p**, being greater than **h** but smaller than **n**. I will then argue as before that the ratio of **p** to **l**—being double the given ratio of **o** to **f**—is greater than the ratio of **h** to **l**, and thus greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. And it is smaller than the ratio of **n** to **l**, and thus smaller than the ratio of **h** to **l** and the ratio of **a** to **b**. This again is what was proposed. Note here, however, that where you cannot easily find a number like **o**, then multiply **k** by **h** by itself and by **l**, and again **h** by itself, until such a number conveniently occurs to you.

## ¶ How to divide any ratio into two parts, each of which is smaller than a given ratio.

¶ Let **b** be a given ratio. It is proposed to divide another given ratio, **a**, into two ratios, such that the difference of one to the other is smaller than the given ratio **b**. I take the difference between ratio **b** and ratio **a**, using the first proposition of this book, and call it **c**. If **c** is equal to or greater than **a**, then **c** must be smaller than **b**. Since by definition **c** is the ratio by which **b** exceeds ratio **a**, then **a** must be either equal to or, even more certainly, smaller than **b**. Therefore, into whatever two ratios **a** is divided, those parts will be smaller than **a**, because they are parts of it. Therefore, the difference of one to the other (since that is the portion of the higher part that is added over the other) will be smaller than the parts themselves, and thus smaller than the whole of **a**. Consequently, it is even more certainly smaller than the whole of **b**. Thus, in whatever way **a** is divided, the proposal is satisfied. But if the ratio **c** is smaller than **a**, I find by the preceding proposition some ratio whose double is greater than **c** and smaller than **a**. Let that doubled ratio be **d**. I then take the difference between **d** and **a** using the first proposition of this book, and call it **e**, which I say is smaller than **b**. For if it were equal, and since **b** would equal **a**, then **d** (which is greater than **c**) and **e** (equal to **b**) would constitute a greater ratio than **a**. Thus, **e** is not the difference between **a** and **d**, which contradicts the hypothesis. And if **e** were greater than **b**, then even more so **d** and **e** would constitute a greater ratio than **b** and **c**, and thus a greater ratio than **a**, which again would mean that it is not the difference between **a** and **d**, contradicting the hypothesis. It remains, therefore, that the ratio **e** is smaller than the ratio **b**. And because the two ratios **d** and **e** are equal to ratio **a**, I understand that ratio **a** has been divided into them. I say that the difference between them—that is, **d** to **e**, which we call **f**—is smaller than the given ratio **b**. For if it were equal to **b**, since **b** and **d** constitute a greater ratio than **a** as was said, then **d** and **f** would also constitute a greater ratio, and thus so would **d** and **e**. Therefore **f** would not be the difference between **d** and **e**. By the same logic, **f** cannot be greater than **b**. It is therefore established that ratio **a** is divided as proposed, and the goal is achieved.

## ¶ If three numbers in continued proportion are compared to three other numbers in continued proportion, the ratios of the extremes will be as the doubled ratio of the means.

¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in continued proportion [[notes: This means $a/b = b/c$]]; likewise, let **d**, **e**, and **f** be three other numbers in continued proportion [[notes: $d/e = e/f$]]. I say that the ratios of **a** to **d** and **c** to **f**, when joined together, are equal to the ratio of **b** to **e** when doubled (squared). I multiply **c** by **a** and by **d**, and let the results be **g** and **h**. And multiply **f** by **d**, let the result be **l**. By the seventh proposition of the second book, the ratio of **g** to **h** will be as **a** is to **d**, and **h** to **l**, by the eighth proposition of the same book, will be as **c** is to **f**. Thus, the ratios of **a** to **d** and **c** to **f** are joined together through **g**, **h**, and **l**. By the third proposition of this book, I add the ratio of **a** to **d** to the ratio of **c** to **f** by multiplying **a** by **c**, producing **l**, and **d** by **f**, producing **m**. It is clear that the ratio of **l** to **m** is composed of the ratios **g** to **h** and **h** to **l**, and is equal to them taken together. Then, I double the ratio of **b** to **e** by adding the ratio of **b** to **e** to itself (by the third proposition), which is done by multiplying **b** by itself. Since **a**, **b**, and **c** are in continued proportion, by the twenty-sixth proposition of the second book, this will produce **l**. By multiplying **e** by itself, and by the same twenty-sixth proposition, it will produce **m**. Therefore, the doubled ratio of **b** to **e** equals the ratio of **l** to **m**, which was proven to be equal to the ratios of **g** to **h** and **h** to **l**—that is, the ratios of **a** to **d** and **c** to **f** joined together. This is what was proposed.

## ¶ If four numbers in continued proportion are matched with another four numbers in continued proportion, the ratios of the extremes will be equal to the ratios of the means joined together.

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be four numbers in continued proportion, and **e**, **f**, **g**, and **h** be four others likewise in continued proportion. I say that the ratio composed of **a** to **e** and **d** to **h** is equal to the joined ratios of **b** to **f** and **c** to **g**. When the ratios of **b** to **f** and **c** to **g** are joined, just as was done in the preceding proof, this is understood through the seventh and eighth propositions of the second book, and the third and twenty-fourth of this book, just as the preceding proof relied on the twenty-sixth.
