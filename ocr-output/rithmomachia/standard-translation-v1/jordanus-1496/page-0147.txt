¶ I have set down only a few of the **Lesser Victories**, so that I might leave you like hunters, who usually take more pleasure in making a discovery on their own than in a hunt provided for them. However, Bathillus, the understanding of these lesser victories depends on three precepts [[notes: In the game of Rithmomachia, a "victory" is achieved by arranging pieces to form mathematical progressions (Arithmetic, Geometric, or Harmonic). A "Lesser Victory" involves forming one of these sequences.]].

¶ **First.** Join two given numbers from the spaces [[notes: *Campi* refers to the squares on the board]]; half of the joined sum is the mean number between them in an **Arithmetic proportion** [[notes: This describes the Arithmetic Mean: $(a + b) / 2$. For example, between 2 and 4, the mean is 3.]].

¶ **Second.** Likewise, given two numbers, multiply the first by the second, and take the **tetragonal side** [[notes: The square root]] of the product; that is the mean number of their **Geometric proportion** [[notes: This describes the Geometric Mean: $\sqrt{a \times b}$. For example, between 4 and 9, the mean is 6.]].

¶ **Third.** Add two given numbers and save the sum. Multiply the first by the second and **double** the product; then divide that doubled product by the saved sum. The result is the mean of those numbers in a **Harmonic proportion** [[notes: This describes the Harmonic Mean: $2ab / (a + b)$. This was considered the most difficult and "musical" of the proportions.]]. And if, following these three precepts, you do not find **whole numbers**, then the given numbers have no means in that specific ratio or proportion. In this way, you may find not only the Lesser Victories—such as those arising from the Arithmetic and Geometric, or Arithmetic and Harmonic, or Geometric and Harmonic proportions—but even the **Great Victories** [[notes: A "Great Victory" or *Victoria Maxima* was achieved when a player formed a sequence of four pieces that embodied multiple types of proportions simultaneously]]. But in truth, Bathillus, others are accustomed to arrange the spaces, battle lines, leaders, and companions [[notes: The various types of game pieces: rounds, triangles, and squares]] differently: so that the area consists of sixty-four spaces, or seventy-two if a middle boundary line is added to divide the two battle lines.

¶ From the spaces arranged in this way, those who are still unskilled—whom the assembly and the silence of the **most pure Father** [[notes: A reference to Pythagoras, the semi-mythical founder of this mathematical tradition]] have not yet admitted to the mysteries—compete with one another using the simpler rules above. But soon, setting aside childish deeds and games, and becoming accustomed to the silence and the hymns, they enjoy the presence of the teacher as if he were some divine power, find solace...
