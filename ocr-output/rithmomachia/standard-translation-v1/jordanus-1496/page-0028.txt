### ¶ If there are any numbers that are the least in their proportion: whatever divides any of them will be commensurable to the other terms of that proportion.
[[margin: 18]]
¶ The "defined terms" are those that are the least in some proportion [[notes: "Least in their proportion" refers to a ratio reduced to its simplest form, such as 2:3 instead of 4:6]]. Let there be any numbers **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** in continuous proportion, being the least in their proportion. Let **e** and **f** be the [simplest] terms in the proportion of **a** to **b**, and let **g** divide the number **a**, **b**, **c**, or **d**. For example: I say **g** is commensurable [[notes: "Commensurable" means they share a common divisor/factor]] to **e** or **f**. I take three least terms in the proportion of **a** to **b** by the sixth [proposition] of this [book], which are **h**, **l**, and **z**. Since, from the corollary of that same sixth [proposition], **c** is produced from **e** into **l**, then by the fifth [proposition] of the third [book], **g** will share a factor with **e** or with **l**. If it is with **e**, the proposition is proven. If it is with **l**, since by the same corollary **l** is produced from **f** into itself, then by that same fifth [proposition] of the third [book], it will share a factor with **f**: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If there are any numbers that are the least in their proportion: any one of them will be prime to the sum of the remaining ones.
[[margin: 19]]
¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be any numbers in continuous proportion, being the least in their proportion. Let **e** and **f** be the terms of the proportion of **a** to **b**. I say that the sum of **a**, **b**, and **c** is prime to **d** [[notes: "Prime to each other" (relatively prime) means they have no common divisor other than 1]]. For if some number divided the sum of **a**, **b**, **c** and also divided **d**, then by the preceding [proposition], it would share a factor with **e** or **f**. Therefore, there would be some common number dividing **e** or **f** and the sum of **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d**. Let this [common divisor] be **g**. Since **g** divides **e** or **f**, and it divides the sum of **a**, **b**, and **c** as well as the number **d**, and both **e** and **f** divide **b** and **c** by the nineteenth [proposition] of the third [book], then by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], **g** will divide **b** and **c**. Thus, **g**, by the fifth [proposition] of the second [book], will divide **a** as a remainder. But **g** was also stated to divide **d**, and **a** and **d**, by the fifth [proposition] of this [book], are prime to each other. Therefore, **g** divides numbers that are prime to each other, which is impossible. Thus, the sum of **a**, **b**, and **c** is not composite to **d**, but necessarily prime: which is what was proposed. And you may argue the same way for any other term compared to the sum of the rest.

### ¶ If any numbers are the least in their proportion: the number composed of all of them will be prime to any one of them.
[[margin: 20]]
¶ This is because a number composed of two numbers that are prime to each other is itself prime to each of them, according to the ninth [proposition] of the third [book]. And by the preceding [proposition], any of the numbers in their least proportion is prime to the sum of the others. Therefore, the sum composed of them all, by that same ninth [proposition], is prime to each of those numbers and to the sum of the others. Wherefore it will be prime to any one of them: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If two numbers are prime to each other: it is impossible to add a third to them in continuous proportion.
[[margin: 21]]
¶ For if a third were added to them in continuous proportion, then by the first [proposition] of this [book], the first and second would be commensurable [[notes: Jordanus is stating that you cannot extend a sequence of integers in a specific ratio if the first two are already in their simplest form and do not form a perfect square/power relationship]]. Therefore, they would not be prime to each other, which is against the hypothesis and the proposition.

### ¶ If two numbers prime to each other divide two other numbers by a factor that is not divided by either of them: a third will also not be added to those others in continuous proportion.
[[margin: 22]]
¶ Let **a** and **b** be numbers prime to each other, and **r** be a number divided by neither **a** nor **b**, by which **a** divides **c** and **b** divides **d**. I say there is no number to be added to **c** and **d** in continuous proportion. For if there were one, let it be **f**. Since by the eighth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **c** to **d** is as **a** to **b**, then **d** to **f** would be as **a** to **b**. Since **a** and **b** are prime to each other, they are the least in their proportion by the twentieth [proposition] of the third [book]. By the nineteenth of the same, as many times as **a** divides **d**, so many times **b** divides **f**. Let them divide equally by **g**. Since **b** divides **d** by **e** and **f** by **g**, then by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **d** to **f** is as **e** to **g**. Thus, **a** to **b** is as **e** to **g**. And **a** and **b** are proven to be the least in that proportion. Therefore, by the nineteenth [proposition] of the second [book], **a** and **b** divide **e** and **g** equally. Thus, one of the numbers **a** or **b** will divide the number **e**, which is against the hypothesis.

### ¶ If proportional numbers are the least in their proportion: there will be no ratio of the extreme to another that is the same as the first to the second.
[[margin: 23]]
¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be in continuous proportion and the least in their proportion. I say that the ratio of **c** to no other [number] is the same as **a** to **b**. For if there were one, let it be **d**. Since **a** is to **b** as **c** is to **d**, then by permutation, **a** is to **c** as **b** is to **d**. And because **a**, **b**, and **c** are the least in their proportion, then by the fifth [proposition] of this [book], **a** and **c** are prime to each other. Therefore, by the twentieth [proposition] of the third [book], they are the least in their proportion. Thus, by the nineteenth of the same, **a** will divide **b**, and **c** will divide **d** equally. But if **a** divides **b**, then **b** also divides **c** (for the proportion is the same). Therefore, by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], **a** will divide **c**. However, **a** and **c** were prime to each other. This is therefore impossible, and the proposition holds.

### ¶ If any numbers in their least proportion divide just as many others by a factor divided by neither of that proportion: no other number will be in continuous proportion with them.
[[margin: 24]]
¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be numbers in continuous proportion and the least in their proportion, which divide just as many [others] **d**, **e**, and **f** by **k**, which is divided by neither **g** nor **h** in the proportion of **a** to **b** in least terms. I say that no other number is to be added to **d**, **e**, and **f** in continuous proportion. For if there were one, let it be **l**, added after **f**. Then **a**, **b**, and **c** (the three least terms) would divide the three last terms of that proportion (**e**, **f**, **l**) equally. Let this be by **m**. Since **c** divides **f** by **k** and **l** by **m**, then by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], **k** is to **m** as **f** is to **l**. Therefore, it is as **a** to **b** by the nineteenth [proposition]. Thus, the third [book] terms **g** and **h**, being the least in that proportion, will divide **k** and **m** equally, which is against the hypothesis. For it was posited that neither **g** nor **h** divides **k** and **m**.
