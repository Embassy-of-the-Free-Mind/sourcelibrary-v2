## 53 ¶ Every prime number is deficient.
¶ For every [prime] number has only unity [[notes: the number 1]] as its part; which, as is certain, constitutes less than the number itself. Therefore, every such number is, by definition, deficient [[notes: A "deficient" (*diminutus*) number is one where the sum of its proper divisors is less than the number itself. Since a prime's only proper divisor is 1, and 1 is less than any prime $p > 1$, all primes are deficient]]. Which is what was proposed.

## 54 ¶ Every even-times-even number is proved to be deficient.
¶ For by the twenty-ninth [proposition] of this book, every such number is among the doubles starting from unity [[notes: the powers of 2: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16...]]. By the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the fourth book, no number will divide it except those taken in that same sequence. But by the thirty-second [proposition] of this book, all the preceding numbers in that sequence, when added together, constitute less than the number itself [[notes: The sum of the powers of 2 up to $2^{n-1}$ is always $2^n - 1$. For example, for the number 8, the divisors are 1, 2, and 4. $1+2+4 = 7$, which is less than 8]]. It is therefore established by definition that such a number is deficient. Which is what was proposed.

## 55 ¶ Every number that is a multiple of an abundant or perfect number must itself be abundant.
<image-desc>A mathematical diagram consisting of horizontal lines of varying lengths. On the left, a set of lines labeled with letters and numbers: a=6, e=12; b=1, h=2; c=2, g=4; d=3, f=6. To the right, a vertical alignment of labels a, e, h, g, f, and I.</image-desc>
¶ Let **a** be a perfect or abundant number having three parts **b, c, d**, which are its second, third, and sixth [[notes: i.e., 1/2, 1/3, and 1/6]]. Let **a** be multiplied by any number, such as **e**, so that the product is [also] **e** [[notes: the text uses 'e' as both the multiplier and the product here, a common shorthand in medieval geometry for the result of the operation]]. There will be three proportional parts, which we call **f, g, h**, and these are the same second, third, and sixth parts [of the product]. Because the proportion of **b** to **a** is the same as **f** to **e**, then by permutation, **b** is to **f** as **a** is to **e**. Likewise, **c** is to **a** as **g** is to **e**; therefore by permutation, **c** is to **g** as **a** is to **e**. Likewise, **d** is to **a** as **h** is to **e**; therefore by permutation, **d** is to **h** as **a** is to **e**. Therefore, the three numbers **b, c, d** are in the same proportion to the three **f, g, h** as **a** is to **e**.
Therefore, by the twentieth [proposition] of the second book, **b, c, d** together are in the same proportion to **f, g, h** together as **a** is to **e**. Then it follows: **b, c, d** together are to **f, g, h** together as **a** is to **e**. Therefore, by permutation, **b, c, d** together are to **a** as **f, g, h** together are to **e**.
Thus, if **b, c, d** together are abundant over **a** [[notes: their sum is greater than a]], then **f, g, h** will also be abundant over **e**. And if **b, c, d** perfectly complete **a** [[notes: as in a perfect number like 6, where 1+2+3=6]], then likewise **f, g, h** perfectly complete **e**. But if **f, g, h** perfectly complete **e**, since **e** by definition has another part distinct from these which is unity, that unity added to the aforesaid parts makes the total abundant over **e**. Therefore, any such number that is a multiple of an abundant or perfect number is abundant. Which is what was proposed.

## 56 ¶ Whatever number divides a perfect number is deficient.
¶ For if the number that divides a perfect number were not deficient, by division it would be either perfect or abundant. But if it were perfect or abundant, then by the preceding proposition, the number it divides would be abundant—yet it is stated to be perfect. Therefore, it is not perfect or abundant. It remains, therefore, as proposed, that it must be deficient. Which is what was proposed.

## 57 ¶ To find an abundant odd number whose greatest of the taken parts is named by a given odd number.
<image-desc>A mathematical calculation diagram. A column of horizontal lines labeled f, e, b, c, a. To the right, a vertical list of odd numbers: 5, 7, 9, 11, 13. Above the total result is the text "It arises from multiplication" (Surgit ex multiplicatione) and the number "45045".</image-desc>
¶ This seems to assume that some odd number can be abundant, which is indeed true [[notes: The smallest odd abundant number is 945. These were rare and difficult for early mathematicians to find]]. Let **a** be the given odd number which ought to name the greatest of the taken parts of an abundant odd number. Let **b** be any abundant odd number. If **b** is smaller than **a**, I multiply it by another odd number until an odd number larger than **a** results, which by the fifty-fifth [proposition] of this book will be abundant. If it is already larger than **a**, I seek no other. And so it stands in the proposition.
Then I take as many odd "denominators" [[notes: divisors]] as I wish above **a**, so that **a** is the smallest denominator. Otherwise, the property of the proposition could not hold. Let these be **c, d, e**. I multiply **a** by **c**, and the result is an odd number by the eleventh [proposition] of this book; then multiply **d** by that product, and **e** by that final product. That final product, by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first book, will have parts named by **a, c, d, e**, and **b**. Multiplying the whole by **b** results in **f**, which by the eleventh [proposition] will be odd, but by the fifty-fifth will also be abundant. Because **b** is abundant and has parts precisely named by **a, c, d, e**, and **b**. And because the part named by **a** has the smallest denominator, it is established that the part named by **a** is the greatest of all those taken, namely among **a, c, d, e**, and **b**. Which is what was proposed.
