The **harmonic rule** [[notes: Also known as the monochord, a single-stringed instrument used to measure mathematical intervals]] is an instrument by which (with the judgment of reason applied) consonances and the parts of consonances are sought out upon a string. There are three genera [[notes: Classes or styles]] of melodies: **Diatonic**, **Chromatic**, and **Enharmonic**. 

The **Diatonic** genus is a melody whose division proceeds continuously by a minor semitone and two whole tones. The **Chromatic** ascends by two unequal semitones and a **trihemitone** [[notes: An interval of three half-tones, or a minor third]]. The **Enharmonic**, however, ascends by two **dieses** and a **ditone** [[notes: An interval of two whole tones, or a major third]]. In this context, a **diesis** is half of a minor semitone; it arises from the division of the difference between its outer limits, and it is also called a **tetrartemeria** [[notes: A Greek term meaning "fourth part," referring here to a quarter-tone]]. It is necessary that the always larger part be found as the higher pitch, and the smaller as the lower pitch. Voices, strings, chords, and spaces are named in this order, striving from the low pitch to the high, in each genus of melody.

$\mathbb{C}$ A **Monochord** is that which contains a melody on a single string. A **Tetrachord** is that which contains it on four strings. A **Polychord**, however, is that which contains it on more than four strings: such as a **pentachord** which has five, a **hexachord** which has six; and so it is to be understood for the heptachord, octochord, enneachord, decachord, hendecachord, dodecachord, tridecachord, tessaradecachord, and the **pentadecachord**, which is the last and consists of fifteen strings in all. 

$\mathbb{C}$ The **Proslambanomenos** is, in every genus, the lowest string added to the first of the tetrachords; it is distant from the first string of the first tetrachord by the interval of a whole tone. A **conjunct tetrachord** is one whose beginning is the end of the preceding tetrachord. A **disjunct tetrachord**, however, is one whose first string in every genus of melody is separated from the final string of the preceding tetrachord by one whole tone.
