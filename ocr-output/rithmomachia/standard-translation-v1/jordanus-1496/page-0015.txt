[[margin: 8]]
¶ **If two numbers multiply the same [number]: the proportion of the multipliers and the products will be the same.**

¶ As if, in the illustration of the preceding [proposition], **b** multiplies **a** to produce **d**, and likewise [**c**] multiplies **a** to produce **e**. I say that the proportion of the multiplier **b** to the multiplier **c** is the same as that of the product **d** to the product **e**. For by the eighth proposition of the first book, the same result comes from multiplying **a** by **b** as from **b** by **a**, which is **d**; and similarly, the same result comes from multiplying **a** by **c** as from **c** by **a**, which is **e**. But by the preceding proposition, when **b** and **c** are multiplied by **a**, the proportion of **b** to **c** is the same as **d** to **e**. Therefore, for the multipliers **b** and **c**, the proportion of **b** to **c** and **d** to **e** will be the same. Which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 9]]
¶ **If two numbers are compared to a third: the proportion of the greater to it will be greater, and the lesser [to it] will be lesser; conversely, the proportion of the third to the greater will be lesser, and to the lesser [it will be] greater. From this it is manifest that if two numbers compared to the same value preserve the same ratio, they must be one and the same number.**

¶ Let **b** and **a** be compared to a third, **c**, and let **a** be greater than **b**: I say that the proportion of **a** to **c** is greater than that of **b** to **c**; I also say that the proportion of **c** to **a** is lesser than that of **c** to **b**. The first part is clear, and let **c** first be a third number greater than both **a** and **b**. Since **c** is greater than **a** and greater than **b**, **a** will be a part or parts of **c** by the first proposition of the first book [[notes: In this context, "part or parts" refers to the fractional relationship of a smaller number to a larger one]]; and by the same, **b** will be a part or parts of it. And since **a** is set as greater than **b**, it will be more parts of **c** than **b** is. Therefore, there is a greater proportion of **a** to **c** than **b** to **c**. Secondly, let **c** be a third number smaller than both **a** and **b**: if **a** contains **c** more times than **b** contains **c**, the proposition stands. And if **a** and **b** contain **c** an equal number of times: because **a** is greater than **b**, it will contain more parts of it beyond [the whole] than **b** contains. Wherefore the same is concluded, namely that the proportion of **a** to **c** is greater than **b** to **c**. Thirdly, let **c** be a third number smaller than **a** and greater than **b**: because the denomination [[notes: The "denomination" is the specific name or value of a ratio, such as "double" (2) or "sesquialter" (1.5)]] of the proportion of **a** to **c** is from a whole multiplier, or a whole and a part or parts, and the denomination of the proportion of **b** to **c** is from a part or parts; it is clear that in this way also the proportion of **a** to **c** is greater than **b** to **c**. And since the third **c** cannot be greater than **a** and smaller than **b**—because whatever is greater than the greater is also greater than the lesser—the first part is proved firm in all its modes. 

The second part: let **c** first be a third number greater than **a** and **b**; since **c** is greater than **a** and **b**, and **a** is greater than **b**. If **c** contains fewer parts of the greater **a** than of the lesser **b**, it is clear that the proportion of **c** to **b** is greater than **c** to **a**. And if **c** contains **b** and **a** equally: it will moreover contain more parts of the smaller **b** than of **a**. Wherefore the same is concluded again. Secondly, let **c** be smaller than both. Taking the same number of units, there will be as many parts of the greater as of the lesser. But by conception, there will be just as many smaller parts of the greater, since those parts are denominated by a larger number [[notes: e.g., 1/9 is a smaller "part" than 1/6]]. Wherefore, its proportion to the greater is again lesser. Thirdly, let **c** be smaller than **a** and greater than **b**: then it is only a part or parts of **a**, but exceeds **b**: thus its proportion to **b** will be greater than to **a**. And since it cannot be greater than **a** and smaller than **b**, as was said, the second part is demonstrated in all its parts. The corollary can be easily recognized from the first part.

[[margin: 10]]
¶ **If the same number is compared to two [others]: that to which it has a greater proportion must be the lesser, and that to which it has a lesser proportion must be the greater.**

¶ This is the converse of the preceding. Let **a** and **b** be two numbers to which a third, **c**, is compared. And let the proportion of **c** to **a** be the lesser, and the proportion of **c** to **b** be the greater. I say that **a** is the greater and **b** is the lesser. For they will not be equal, because then the proportion of **c** to each would be the same. Nor will **a** be less than **b**. For by the first part of the preceding [proposition], the proportion of **a** to **c** would be less than **b** to **c**, and by its second part, the proportion of **c** to **a** would be greater, and **c** to **b** lesser; which is against the hypothesis. Therefore, what was proposed remains true.

[[margin: 11]]
¶ **If the proportion of the first to the second is greater than that of the third to the fourth: the proportion of the second to the first will be lesser than that of the fourth to the third.**

¶ This is easy to demonstrate in multiples: as if **a** to **b** in the genus of multiples is a greater proportion than **c** to **d**, I say that **b** to **a** is a lesser proportion than **d** to **c**. For I will take **e** as an equal multiple of **d** as **a** is of **b**. Because **a** to **b** and **e** to **d** are equal multiples, the proportion of **a** to **b** and **e** to **d** will be the same. But the proportion of **a** to **b** is set as greater than **c** to **d**: therefore the proportion of **e** to **d** is also greater than **c** to **d**. By the second part of the ninth [proposition], therefore, the proportion of **d** to **c** is greater than **d** to **e**. But because the proportion of **a** to **b** and **e** to **d** is the same: by the first [proposition] of this book, the proportion of **b** to **a** and **d** to **e** will be the same. But **d** to **c** has been proved to be greater than the proportion of **d** to **e**: therefore it will also be greater than its equal, **b** to **a**. Which is what was proposed. 

But because this demonstration is not equally suited to other genera [of ratios], another more universal one must be applied. Let therefore the proportion of **a** to **b** be greater than **c** to **d**: I say that **b** to **a** is a lesser proportion than **d** to **c**. For I multiply **c** by **b** and let **e** be produced; and the same **c** by **a** and let **f** be produced. By the seventh [proposition] of this book, whatever is the proportion of **a** to **b**, the same will be **f** to **e**. Again, I multiply **d** by **a** and let **g** be produced. Since **c** and **d** have multiplied **a**, by the eighth [proposition of Book I], the proportion of the product **f** to the product **g** will be the same as the multiplier **c** to the multiplier **d**. Therefore, the proportion **f** to **e** is as **a** to **b**, and **f** to **g** is as **c** to **d**. But the proportion of **a** to **b** is set as greater than the proportion of **c** to **d**: therefore the proportion of **f** to **e** is greater than the proportion of the same **f** to **g**. By the preceding [proposition], therefore, **e** is lesser and **g** is greater. Therefore, by the first part of the ninth [proposition] of this book, the proportion of **e** to **f** is lesser than **g** to **f**. But the proportion of **e** to **f** is the same as the proportion of **b** to **a**, and the proportion of **g** to **f** is the same as the proportion of **d** to **c**, as you can easily recognize by the first [proposition] of this book; therefore the proportion of the second **b** to the first **a** is lesser than the proportion of the fourth **d** to the third **c**. Which was to be demonstrated.
