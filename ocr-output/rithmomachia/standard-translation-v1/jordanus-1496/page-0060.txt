# Every prism [[notes: *Serratilis*, literally "saw-like," refers to a prism or a column of equal figurate numbers stacked on top of each other. In this context, it is a triangular prism.]] higher by one than the pyramid of its base adds upon it a pyramid whose base is square and equilateral to the base of the other. <margin>31</margin>

¶ For if the triangular pyramid is taken away—namely, such that the base is removed from the base, and from the second of the equal triangles the second [layer] of the pyramid is subtracted, and from the third the third, and so on—since the prism was set one unit higher than the pyramid of its base, the highest unit (the vertex of the pyramid) will be taken from the second-to-last of the equal triangles. Thus, the entire final triangular layer of the prism is left untouched. And because the base of the triangular pyramid was taken from the base of the prism, by the second [proposition] of this book, the side of the second triangle is left; and similarly in all the higher layers. Since there are as many of these as there are units in that side, it is established that the side itself, with the sum of the sides of the following ones, completes a square. Furthermore, because the side of the third triangle of the prism remains, along with the smaller one following it, and so on in the following layers, it will be that those are the sides of the following square. In this manner, the number of squares grows all the way to unity. It will be, therefore, that in the highest triangle, a single unit remains, which is the first square [[notes: *Tetragonus*, or square number]]. Therefore, by the twenty-eighth [proposition] of this book, it is established that a square pyramid is left behind. And since the side of its base is the same as the side of the equal triangles and the side of the base of the subtracted triangular pyramid, it is certain that the remaining pyramid is equilateral to the base of the prism, and that the prism itself adds that square pyramid onto the pyramid of its base; which is what was proposed.

# Every prism higher by two than the pyramid of its base is proved to be triple to that pyramid. <margin>32</margin>

¶ For I subtract from the prism itself the last of the triangles, and there will remain a prism one unit higher than the pyramid; which, by the preceding [proposition], can be cut into two pyramids: one of which is the triangular pyramid of the prism's base, and the other is a square pyramid whose base is square and equilateral to the base of the other. But this same square pyramid, by the twenty-ninth [proposition] of this book, can again be cut into two triangular pyramids: one of which is equal to the first triangular pyramid, and the other is smaller than it by the amount of the first triangular pyramid’s base. Therefore, by adding the triangular layer previously taken away (which is equal to the base of the first triangular pyramid) to the smaller one, that one becomes equal to the other. In this way, three equal triangular pyramids are made, constituting the whole prism. It is therefore established that every such prism is triple the pyramid of its base; which is what was proposed.

# Given the side of a triangular base, to find the sum of the triangular pyramid built upon it. <margin>33</margin>

¶ Let the triangular number of the given side be found by the sixth [proposition] of this book; let the side itself be multiplied by this, and we will have a prism of equal height to the pyramid. To this, if two equal triangular numbers are added, a prism results that is higher by two than the pyramid of its base. Take a third part of this, and by the preceding [proposition], you have the sum of the pyramid built upon that side, which is the sum of the triangular number of the given side together with all its lower triangular numbers; which is what was proposed.

# To find the sum of a whole pyramid whose base is square, when the side of that base is given. <margin>34</margin>

¶ Just as the preceding taught us to find the sum of a triangular number and all those contained below it given the triangular side, so the present [proposition] instructs us to find the sum of a square and all the squares below it given the square side. For this is to find the sum of a square pyramid, as is shown. For given the side, I seek its triangular number by the sixth [proposition] of this book, by which (as was done in the preceding) I multiply the proposed side, and there results a prism as high as its pyramid. To this I add one of the similar triangles, and a prism results that is one unit higher than the pyramid of its base. Therefore, when the triangular pyramid found by the preceding [proposition] is subtracted, by the thirty-first [proposition] of this book, there remains a square pyramid whose base is square and equilateral to the triangle, which is the sought sum and the proposition.

# If two prisms whose triangular bases are adjacent are joined, and if their height is one and equal to the side of the larger, a cube is formed. <margin>35</margin>

¶ For the bases in them are multiplied equally, namely according to the number of the side of the larger prism; and the base of one joined with the base of the other by the fifth [proposition] of this book produces a square whose side is equal to the side of the larger. And the second triangle of one joined to the second of the other produces a similar square. And so on, so that as many equal squares are placed one upon the other and fitted to each other as there are units in the side. But since a figure is constituted from these having a quadrangular base, and extended equally in length, breadth, and height, by definition it is concluded to be a cube.

# If to any cube its base is added along with a triangular number equilateral to its base, it will be made triple to its pyramid. <margin>36</margin>

¶ I take two adjacent triangular numbers, the larger of which is equilateral to the square of the assigned cube, and I construct upon them (by the thirtieth [proposition]) prisms whose height is equal to the side of the larger base; these two prisms joined together will equal the assigned cube by the preceding [proposition]. And because the larger of those prisms is of equal height to its pyramid, by adding twice its base to it, by the thirty-second [proposition] of this book, it becomes triple the pyramid of its base. But because the other prism is of equal height to the first, it will be one unit higher than its own pyramid. Therefore, by adding its base once, it becomes triple its own pyramid. But its base with the base of the other, by the fifth [proposition] of this book, makes the square of the cube; wherefore, if to the aforesaid cube its base is added along with the base of the larger prism (which is the triangular number equilateral to its base), a number is produced triple to its pyramid; which was to be demonstrated. ¶ There are, besides, "columns" [[notes: *Columnae*, another type of solid number]] which, like the prisms, are easily formed by the thirtieth [proposition], and the sum of the fourth [type] is known by multiplying the base by the "edge" [[notes: *Costa*, likely referring to the height or lateral edge]], but because these are easy, the author has omitted them.
