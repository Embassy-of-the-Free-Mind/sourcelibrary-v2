### ¶ If oblong numbers are taken starting from unity, by joining each to its neighbor, half of the joined sums results in all the squares.

[[notes: An "oblong number" (*altera parte longior*) is a number of the form n(n+1), such as 2, 6, 12, 20. This theorem states that if you take two consecutive oblong numbers, like 6 and 12, their sum (18) divided by two is a square (9).]]

As with the figured oblong numbers in the manner done above: I say that half the sum of **l z** and **l** (the oblong numbers taken) is a square. Similarly, half of **l** and **m** taken together is a square, and so on for any pairs taken continuously: the half will be a tetragon [[notes: "Tetragonic" or "tetragon" is the Greek-derived term Jordanus uses for a square number.]]. For since it was shown by the second part of the preceding [proposition] that the difference of the smaller **l z** to **f** is **b**, and the larger **l** to **f** is also **b**: **l z** and **l** are placed around and equidistant from **f**. Therefore, by the second [proposition] of the first [book], **f** is the half of the numbers **l z** and **l** taken together. And since **f** is known to be a square, the proposition is established as true for **l z** and **l**. And it will be established similarly for **l** and **m**: because their difference to **g** by the preceding [proposition] is **c**, the side of **g**; wherefore by the second of the first it is concluded that the square **g** is the half of the numbers **l** and **m** joined together.

### ¶ No oblong number is a square or a cube.

For if any oblong number, such as **a**, were a square: by its definition its sides would be equal. And because it would be an oblong number, its sides would differ only by unity. If therefore those sides are given and **b** is the greater and **c** the smaller: because **a** would also be a square, let its tetragonic side be **d**. And because that which is produced from **b** by **c** is equal to that which is produced from **d** by itself: therefore by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], **b**, **d**, and **c** are continuous proportionals, which is impossible. For since **b** and **c** differ only by unity, no mean number falls between **b** and **c** [[notes: Jordanus is arguing that there is no integer between two consecutive integers like 3 and 4; therefore, their product cannot be a perfect square because that would require a non-integer square root.]]. 

But if an adversary should say that **d** is equal to **b** or **c**: since it is necessary for **b**, **c**, and **d** to be in continuous proportion, then **b** and **c** would have to be equal, which is against the hypothesis. Or if he says **d** is greater than both **b** and **c**: then the ratio of **b** to **d** is the ratio of a smaller number to a larger, and **d** to **c** is the ratio of a larger to a smaller; and these are posited as the same, which is impossible. Finally, if he says **d** is smaller than both **b** and **c**: then the ratio of **b** to **d** is larger to smaller and **d** to **c** is smaller to larger; and again these are posited as the same, which is impossible. Therefore **a** is not an oblong square, which is the first part.

I next show that the oblong **a** is not a cube. For if it were possible, let the oblong **a** be a cube and let its sides be: the greater **b** and the smaller **c**; and let the square of **b** be **d** and the square of **c** be **e**: and let **f** be the side of **a** insofar as it is a cube, whose square is **l z**. For by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book] there will be one proportional mean between **d** and **l z**, which shall be **g**. And by the same, one between **e** and **l z**, which shall be **h**: and by the seventh of the second, the ratio of **b** to **f** is the same as **d** to **g**, and **g** to **l z**. And similarly the ratio of **c** to **f** will be as **e** to **h** and **h** to **l z**. 

And because **e** and **l z** in **f** make **a**, and the same is made by **b** by the hypothesis from **b** in **c**: therefore by the second part of the twenty-fourth [proposition] of the second [book], **l z** will be to **b** as **c** is to **f**. And also permuted, **l z** to **c** as **b** to **f**. There will therefore be between **d** and **c** the continuous proportionals **g** and **l z**, and let there be between **e** and **b** likewise two, **h** and **l z**. And because **b** and **e** will either be commensurable or prime to each other: if they are commensurable, since **b** numbers **d** (its square): then by the twenty-third of the first, whatever number numbers **b** will also number **d**. Wherefore **d** and **e** will be commensurable. But yet because **b** and **c** are prime to each other, for **d** and **e** to be commensurable is impossible by the twelfth of the third. 

But if **b** and **e** are incommensurable, by the second of this [book] **b** and **e** are cubes. Similarly, if **c** and **d** were commensurable, by the same as before **d** and **e** would be commensurable, which is impossible by the twelfth of the third. But if **c** and **d** are incommensurable, then by the second of this [book] each is a cube; wherefore between **b** and **c** there will be two continuous proportional means by the corollary of the fourth of this [book]. But that not only two, but even one mean should fall between them (as shown in the first part) is impossible. It is therefore established that the proposition is true.

### ¶ Given an oblong number: to find another oblong number whose ratio to the first is as a square to a square.

¶ Let **a** be the given oblong number and its greater side be **b**: and its smaller be **c**: and let **d** be double to **b** and **e** double to **c**: and the square of **d** be **f**: and the first square below it be **g**, than which the number **h** is less by unity; and let the oblong number between **f** and **g** be **l z**. For since by the second part of the twenty-fourth [proposition] of this [book] the difference between **f** and **l z** is **d**: and the difference between **l z** and **g** is the number smaller than it by unity. Therefore the difference of **l z** to **h** is equal to **d**. 

And since the difference of **d** and **e** is two: because it is double the difference of **b** to **c**. For by the corollary of the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the differences of doubled things are doubled. And since from **d** by itself comes **f**, and from **d** by two (which is the difference of it to **e**) comes the difference of **f** to **h**: because let it be the difference of **f** to **l z** and **l z** to **h**, which is double to **d**, as was shown. Therefore **h** will be the remaining number which is produced from **d** into **e**. 

I multiply therefore **g** by **h**, which numbers were posited to differ only by unity: and let the oblong number **l** be produced, which I say is the one we seek. For let **b** be multiplied by **h** and make **m**: and **c** by **g** and make **n**. And by the eighth of the second, the ratio of **l** to **m** will be as **g** to **b**. And by the same, the ratio of **n** to **a** is as **g** to **b**. Wherefore the ratio of **l** to **m** is the same as **n** to **a**. And because from **b** into **h** comes **m**: and from **c** into **g** comes **n**: therefore by the ninth of the fifth, the ratio of **m** to **n** is composed of the ratios of **b** to **c** and **h** to **g**. And because **d** into itself makes **f**: and **d** into **e** makes **h**, the ratio of **f** to **h** will be as **d** to **e**. Wherefore there will also be one similar proportional mean between **m** and **n**, which shall be **o**. 

Since therefore **o** is to **n** as **m** is to **o**, and **n** is to **a** as **l** is to **m**. Therefore by equal indirect proportionality, **l** will be to **o** as **o** is to **a**. I take therefore in the ratio of **l** to **o** three numbers in continuous proportion, the first being **r**, **q**, **p**: it is manifest that **r** and **p** are squares: then thus, the ratio **r** to **q** is as **l** to **o**: and as **q** to **p** is as **o** to **a**: therefore by equal proportionality, the ratio of **r** to **p** (square to square) is as **l** to **a** (oblong to oblong): which is the proposition.

### ¶ If proportional numbers are arranged from unity: it is necessary that the third from unity is a square, and skipping one, the third is always a square; the fourth from unity a cube, and skipping two, the fourth is always a cube; the seventh however a square-cube, and skipping five, the seventh is always a square-cube.
