¶ Therefore, having formed this **Table of Pythagoras** [[notes: A standard multiplication table, traditionally attributed to Pythagoras in medieval textbooks]] and having arranged the rows of numbers as the proposition sets forth: I say first that if each individual row is compared to the first row, they result in a sequence of multiples. For since unity is the head of the first row, and the head and first number of the second row is two, the first number of the second row is double the first number of the first, and thus the first multiple. And because two multiplies all the numbers of the first row, and the second row is created by that continuous multiplication, then by the definition, as many times as unity exists in two, so many times does each number of the first row exist in each of the second. Therefore, each number of the second row is double to the corresponding number of the first, and equally multiple. And because the first number of the third row is triple the first of the first (which is unity), and by the continuous multiplication of each of the first row by it, each number of the third row is produced: therefore, by the same reasoning as before, each number of the third row is triple to each in the first row. By this same logic, you will prove each of the fourth to be quadruple to each of the first, and the fifth quintuple, and so on.

Secondly, I say that if a comparison is made of the third row to the second, and the fourth to the third, and the fifth to the fourth, and the sixth to the fifth, and so on, then rows of **superparticulars** [[notes: A superparticular ratio is one where the larger number contains the smaller number plus exactly one part of it, such as 3:2 (1 + 1/2) or 4:3 (1 + 1/3)]] continually arise. For we see that each of these and each of those are born from the multiplication of the first two numbers taken in sequence descending from unity. But by the preceding [logic], those numbers continually complete a series of superparticulars. Therefore, each number of a later row will hold a similar proportion to the row before it. This is the second point. Thirdly, I say the numbers that stand along the **diameter** [[notes: The diagonal of the table]] are **squares**. For those are they whose head is unity, and the highest is the greatest of all, like the longest line, cutting the rows of numbers through the middle as if equally. We call these squares. For you will see them produced continually from equal sides, increasing always by one according to the series of numbers. Therefore, all will be squares. And if you draw lines parallel to their sides, you will always enclose a square figure. Fourthly, I say the numbers on either side, lying adjacent to the squares on both sides, are **oblongs** [[notes: Latin *altera parte longiores*, literally "longer by another part." These are numbers of the form $n(n+1)$, like 2, 6, 12, 20... which in the table sit right next to the squares 1, 4, 9, 16...]]. For all those are produced according to the series of numbers from sides differing by one: so that one of their sides is the side of a square, and the other is less than the side of that same square by one. Thus the whole proposition is known. And from this it can also be known that if individual oblongs are compared to the individual squares that flank them on either side, all species of superparticulars are created from a double proportion: as two, the first oblong, compared to one, the first square, is double. And six, the second oblong, related to four, the second square, is sesquialter [[notes: 1.5 or 3:2]] by the second part of this. And twelve, the third oblong, to nine, the third square, is sesquitertial [[notes: 1.33 or 4:3]] by the same, and in this manner subsequently. ¶ And the same occurs regarding the species of superparticulars if you compare the first oblong to the second square, and the second oblong to the third, and the third to the fourth, and so on in this manner. ¶ And again, if you place the first oblong between the first square and the second, a similar relationship of proportions is preserved, and the proportion will be continually double. ¶ And if you place the second oblong between the second square and the third, a sesquialter relationship will be continually preserved from the first and second part of this. and so you may find the remaining joined relationships of superparticulars if you insert the remaining "long-sided" numbers [[notes: oblongs]] between their squares; from which that worthy contemplation (having momentum for Pythagorean and Platonic understanding) which **Divine Severinus** [[notes: Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, the 6th-century philosopher whose *De Arithmetica* was the standard medieval textbook]] brings forth most elegantly in the 33rd chapter of the second book of his *Arithmetic*, can be seen as most perfect.

[[margin: 39]] ¶ If superparticulars are taken starting from unity: when joined two by two separately, it happens that the first of the multiples and subsequently all species of superparticulars result.

¶ Let **a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k** be superparticular numbers taken continually from unity [[notes: Here meaning the sequence of integers 2, 3, 4, 5... as they form ratios with the numbers preceding them]], and let them be joined separately two by two, namely **c** to **a**, **e** to **c**, **g** to **e**, and **k** to **g**. I say that **c** to **a** is the first of the multiples, and **e** to **c**, **g** to **e**, and **k** to **g** continually establish species of superparticulars. For by the penultimate [proposition], **a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k** will be numbers taken continually from unity; but by always skipping one in this way, taking them separately two by two, all the even numbers from unity are taken. Wherefore, by the 18th [proposition] of the seventh [book], the proportions between them are the same as those of numbers arranged continuously from unity. But the ratio of two to unity is the first of the multiples, and the series of those following to their predecessor is superparticular. Therefore, what was to be demonstrated is established.
