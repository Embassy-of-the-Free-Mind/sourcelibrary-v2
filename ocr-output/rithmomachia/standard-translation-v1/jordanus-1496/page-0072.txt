### ¶ Only the sesquialtera among the superparticulars produces a multiple-superparticular ratio. <page-num>59</page-num>

<margin>
[[image-desc: Line diagram showing proportions.]]
a ——— 9
b ——— 6 ——— 4 (c)
d ——— 3
e ——— 2
[[notes: The lines connect 9 to 6 (a 3:2 ratio) and 6 to 4 (a 3:2 ratio). The lines for d and e represent the remainders or differences.]]
</margin>

¶ Let **a** to **b** be a *sesquialtera* [[notes: A ratio of 3:2, where the larger number is 1 and 1/2 times the smaller]] and let **b** to **c** be the same. It is manifest that the ratio of **a** to **c** is composed of two *sesquialteras*. **a** contains **b** and its half, which is **d**; and **b** contains **c** and its half, which is **e**. Because by the fifth [proposition] of the second [book] [[notes: Likely referring to Euclid’s Elements or a similar mathematical text]], **d** is to **e** as **b** is to **c**: therefore **d** contains **e** and its half. Thus, **d** and **e** together are exactly as much as **c** plus one part named from **c** [[notes: This means d + e = c + (1/4)c in the specific case of 9:4]]. Therefore **a** contains the number **c** twice, plus one part of it. Since **a** contains **c** and **d** [and] **e**, the whole difference of **a** to **c** [is accounted for]: wherefore two *sesquialteras* constitute a multiple-superparticular [[notes: Here, (3/2) × (3/2) = 9/4, which is 2 and 1/4]]. Secondly, he shows that no other doubled superparticular constitutes a multiple-superparticular. Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be other proportional superparticulars, such that the ratio of **a** to **c** is the ratio of **a** to **b** doubled. **a** contains **b** and its part, which is **d**; which, when taken twice, is less than the whole, or itself once if it were a third; or twice if it were a fourth; or more times according to how much smaller the part was. Similarly, **b** contains **c** and a similar part of it, which is **e**. Since **d** is to **b** as **e** is to **c**, then **d** is to **e** as **b** is to **c**. Therefore **d** contains **e** once and its one similar part: thus **d** and **e** together are less than **c**. Wherefore **a** to **c** is not a multiple [[notes: Meaning it doesn't result in a clean integer ratio like 2:1 or 3:1]]; but since **d** and **e** are prime to each other as we suppose, **d** and **e** together, by the ninth [proposition] of the third [book], are prime to **e**. And by the eleventh of the same, **c** will also be prime to **d** and **e** together. Therefore **d** and **e** together will not measure **c**. Thus, the ratio of **a** to **c** is a *superpartient* ratio [[notes: A ratio where the larger number contains the smaller plus more than one part, like 5:3]], which was the thing proposed.

### ¶ A multiple ratio cannot be distributed into any equal ratios except for multiples. <page-num>60</page-num>

<margin>
[[image-desc: Line diagram showing a geometric progression.]]
a ——— 16
c ——— 8
d ——— 4
b ——— 2
[[notes: Vertical lines connect these labels, illustrating a doubling sequence: 2, 4, 8, 16.]]
</margin>

¶ Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be a multiple [[notes: An integer ratio like 2:1, 4:1, etc.]], which is divided into any number of equal ratios, which are **a** to **c**, **c** to **d**, and **d** to **b**. Since **b** to **d**, **d** to **c**, and **c** to **a** are continuously proportional, and **b**, being first by hypothesis, measures the last, **a**: therefore by the seventeenth [proposition] of the fourth [book], **b** will measure the second, **d**. Wherefore **d** will also measure **c**, and **c** likewise **a**. Therefore the ratio of **a** to **b** is divided into multiple ratios, which was the intent.

### ¶ No superparticular ratio is divisible into any number of equal ratios. <page-num>61</page-num>

<margin>
[[image-desc: Line diagram with labels and brackets grouping terms.]]
a —— b (c) —— h
d —— f (g) —— k
i —— l
</margin>

¶ Let **a** to **b** be any superparticular ratio, which is divided, if possible, into equal ratios **a** to **b**, **b** to **c**, and **c** to **d**. I take **e**, **f**, **g**, **h** to be ratios in the smallest terms of the ratio **a** to **b**: and by equal proportionality, the ratio of **a** to **d** is that of **e** to **h**. But **a** contains **b** and its part; therefore **e** will contain **h** and its part, which is **l**. If **l** were a number, it would measure **h** and also **e**. Therefore **h** and **e** would not be the smallest terms, which contradicts the hypothesis by the fifth of the fourth [book]. Otherwise, if **l** is unity, it is the part denominated by **h**. I add unity to **h**, and let the sum be **l**: the ratio of **a** to **d** will be as **l** to **h**. Therefore, by the twelfth of the fourth [book], as many proportional means fall between **a** and **d**, so many fall between **l** and **h**, which are distant only by a single unit; which is impossible. [[notes: This is a fundamental proof in Pythagorean music theory. It proves that you cannot mathematically split a ratio like 9:8 (a whole tone) into two equal rational parts (half-tones).]] From this, it is known that the **tone** in music, which arises from the *sesquioctave* ratio [[notes: 9:8]], cannot be partitioned into two equal parts.

### ¶ To find a superpartient ratio which, when doubled, constitutes a superpartient. <page-num>62</page-num>

¶ I take any ratio that is less than double, which I partition by the fifty-fourth [proposition] of this [book] into two superpartients; for if the smaller is doubled, a ratio less than double will be produced: wherefore it is less than any multiple. Since by the preceding [proposition] it will not be a superparticular (otherwise a superparticular would be cut into two equal parts), it is established that it is a superpartient: and the proposition is accomplished.

### ¶ To find a multiple-superparticular ratio which is composed of two equal superpartients. <page-num>63</page-num>

¶ According to the requirement of the tenth [proposition] of the eighth [book], I take the squares **a** and **b**, which are prime to each other, such that **a** is one unit more than double **b**. It is manifest that the ratio of **a** to **b** is a multiple-superparticular [[notes: In this example, 289:144. 289 is (2 × 144) + 1, so it is "double-superparticular" or 2 and 1/144]]. But from that tenth proposition, you will easily know that the ratio of the side [[notes: square root]] of **a** to the side of **b** is a superpartient. I take, therefore, according to the requirement of the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of the sixth [book], the proportional mean between **a** and **b** [[notes: which is 204]], and by the third of the same sixth [book], the ratio of **a** to **c** is as the ratio of the side of **a** to the side of **b**. Wherefore it is established that the multiple-superparticular ratio **a** to **b** is composed of the ratios **a** to **c** and **c** to **b**, namely two equal superpartients: which is the thing proposed.

¶ Let **a** be any odd number and **b** the next larger even number, whose half is **c**. I take the superpartient formed from the parts denominated by **a** and numbered by **c** [[notes: e.g., if a=3 and c=2, the ratio is 1 and 2/3, or 5:3]]: which, when doubled, I say is the one sought. For if that is multiplied by itself, there will come a number and parts numbered by **a** and the square of **c**, which is **e**: and denominated by the square of **a**, which is **d**. But since **a** and **c** are prime to each other (otherwise **a** and **b** would share a common factor), therefore by the eleventh of the third [book], **a** is prime to **c**: and by the ninth of the same, **a** and **e** together are prime to **a**. Wherefore **a** and **e** together, by the eleventh of the same, are prime to **d**. Therefore the parts numbered by **a** and **e** will not measure **d**. Wherefore the assigned superpartient ratio, when doubled, constitutes a multiple-superpartient. 

Alternatively, if this demonstration seems somewhat obscure to you, take this declaration for this and the following [propositions]. For in the present case, the superpartient found was a *superbipartiens tertias* [[notes: 1 and 2/3]], which is denominated from one and two-thirds. Thus, you double the denomination of the *superbipartiens tertias* by the eighth [proposition] of this [book], namely by multiplying two-thirds by two-thirds. By the fourth of this [book], four-ninths result. Secondly, [multiply] two-thirds by one, and two-thirds result. Thirdly, [multiply] one by two-thirds, and again two-thirds result. And fourthly, [multiply] one by one, and one results. You will have four-ninths, [plus] two [and] two-thirds, and one. But two and two-thirds—that is, four-thirds—make one whole and one-third. This one, added to the other one, constitutes the number two; and one-third is three-ninths, which you may easily know by the second [proposition] of this [book]. If these three-ninths are added to the other four-ninths first found, they become seven-ninths. It is thus clear that the *superbipartiens tertias* ratio, when doubled, constitutes a double and *superseptupartient nonas* [[notes: 2 and 7/9]], which is a multiple-superpartient, as the proposition proposed: which is the proposition declared.
