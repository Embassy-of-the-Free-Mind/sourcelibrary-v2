[[margin: 16]]
## If unequal numbers are added to equal numbers, the ratio of the resulting sums will be less than the ratio of the original parts.

<image-desc>Geometric diagram showing two systems of arches. The first system consists of a large arch labeled "8" spanning two smaller arches labeled "6" and "2" respectively, situated over points a, b, and c. The second system consists of a large arch labeled "5" spanning two smaller arches labeled "3" and "2", situated over points c and d.</image-desc>

Let **a** and **c** be unequal numbers; let **b** and **d** be equal, and let the sums be **a + b** and **c + d**. Let **a** be greater than **c**. I say that the ratio of **a** to **c** (the part to the part) is a greater ratio than the sum **a + b** to the sum **c + d**. For since **b** and **d** are the same number, and **a** is greater than **c**, then by the ninth [proposition], the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than **c** to **d**. Therefore, by the twelfth, the ratio of **a** to **c** (the subtracted part to the subtracted part) is greater than the ratio of **b** to **d** (the remainder to the remainder). [[notes: The author here treats the equal additions as "remainders" in the context of the larger sums to apply earlier proofs.]] Therefore, the ratio of **a** to **c** is greater than the ratio of the whole **a + b** to the whole **c + d**. For if the ratio of **a** to **c** and **a + b** to **c + d** were the same, then by the fifth [proposition], **a** to **c** and **b** to **d** would be the same ratio—the opposite of which has been demonstrated. If, however, the ratio of **a + b** to **c + d** were greater than **a** to **c**, then by the thirteenth, the ratio of the remainder **b** to **d** would be greater than the ratio of the part **a** to **c**, the opposite of which was just shown. Therefore, there remains only that the ratio of **a** to **c** (of the added part to the added part) is greater than **a + b** to **c + d** (of the sum to the sum), which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 17]]
## If the ratio of the first to the second be greater than the third to the fourth, and the first is less than the third, then the second must be less than the fourth.

<image-desc>Geometric diagram with two systems of arches. The first shows a large arch "8" over two smaller arches "6" and "2" at points a, b, and c. The second shows a large arch "12" over two smaller arches "8" and "4" at points b, c, and d.</image-desc>

Let there be four numbers: **a** (the first), **b** (the second), **c** (the third), and **d** (the fourth). Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be greater than **c** to **d**, and let **a** be less than **c**. I say that **b** is less than **d**. For since **c** is greater than **a**, therefore by the ninth [proposition], the ratio of **c** to **b** is greater than **a** to **b**. Wherefore, by much greater force, the ratio of **c** to **b** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. Therefore, by the tenth [proposition], **d** is greater than **b**, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 18]]
## When the ratio of the first to the second is greater than the third to the fourth, and the sum of the first and second is equal to the sum of the third and fourth, it is necessary that the first be greater than the third.

<image-desc>Geometric diagram with two systems of arches. Both have a large arch labeled "10". The first covers two smaller arches "8" and "2" at points a, b, and c. The second covers two smaller arches "6" and "4" at points b, c, and d.</image-desc>

Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be greater than **c** to **d**, and let the sum **a + b** be equal to the sum **c + d**. I say that **a** is greater than **c**. For first, it will not be equal; because if **a** were equal to **c**, then **b** would also be equal to **d** by common notion [[notes: "Conceptio" refers to a common notion or axiom, specifically that if equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal]]. Otherwise, if unequals were added to equals, the totals would be unequal. In that case, the ratio of **a** to **b** and **c** to **d** would be equal, which is against the hypothesis. Nor will **c** be greater than **a** (meaning **a** is less). For by the preceding [proposition], **b** (the second) would then be less than **d** (the fourth); thus, by common notion, **a** and **b** (the two smaller values) taken together would be less than **c** and **d** (the two larger values) taken together. This is again against the hypothesis. Therefore, it remains that **a** is greater than **c**, which was proposed.

[[margin: 19]]
## If the first be greater than the third, and the sum of the first and second be equal to the sum of the third and fourth, then the ratio of the first to the second will be greater than the third to the fourth.

This is the converse of the preceding. Let **a** (the first number) be greater than **c** (the third), as before, and let the sum **a + b** be equal to the sum **c + d**. I say the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than **c** to **d**. For because **a** is greater than **c**, and **a + b** and **c + d** are equal, then **b** must be less than **d**. For **b** cannot be equal to **d**, because if they were added to the unequals **a** and **c**, the totals would not be equal. Nor is **b** greater than **d**, for by common notion, the sum of **a** and **b** (two larger numbers) would be greater, which contradicts the hypothesis. And since **a** is greater than **c**, then by the ninth [proposition], the ratio of the greater **a** to **d** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. But since **b** was demonstrated to be less than **d**, by the tenth [proposition], the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than **a** to **d**. Wherefore, by much greater force, **a** to **b** is a greater ratio than **c** to **d**, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 20]]
## If any number of quantities have the same ratio to as many others, then as one is to one, so will the sum of all be to the sum of all taken together.

Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any numbers, and **d**, **e**, and **f** be as many others. Let the ratio of **a** to **d**, **b** to **e**, and **c** to **f** be the same. I say that whatever the ratio of **a** to **d** is, the same is the ratio of the whole **a + b + c** to the whole **d + e + f**. For you may easily recognize from the second [proposition] of this [book] that the three antecedents [[notes: The "antecedent" is the first term in a ratio (e.g., the numerator).]] are either all greater than, all less than, or all equal to their consequents [[notes: The "consequent" is the second term in a ratio (e.g., the denominator).]]. If they are less, then whatever part or parts **a** is of **d**, the same part or parts will **b** be of **e**, and **c** of **f**. Therefore, by the fourth and fifth [propositions] of the first [book], repeated through logical cases as often as necessary, the whole **a + b + c** will be the same part or parts of the whole **d + e + f** as **a** is of **d**. Wherefore the ratio of **a** to **d** and the whole **a + b + c** to **d + e + f** will be the same. But if the three antecedents are equally greater, because the ratio of **a** to **d** is the same as **b** to **e** and **c** to **f**, then by the first [proposition] of this [book], the ratio of **d** to **a** is the same as **e** to **b** and **f** to **c**. Wherefore, whatever part or parts **d** is of **a**, the same is **e** of **b** and **f** of **c**. By the fourth and fifth of the first [book], repeated as needed, whatever part or parts **d** is of **a**, the same part or parts is the sum **d + e + f** of the sum **a + b + c**. Wherefore, whatever the ratio of **d** to **a**, the same will be the whole **d + e + f** to the whole **a + b + c**. Thus, by the first [proposition] of this [book], the ratio of **a** to **d** is the same as the whole **a + b + c** to the whole **d + e + f**, which was proposed. If, on the other hand, the individual numbers are equal to each other, you will achieve the same result immediately with the help of the common notion that if you add equal numbers to equal numbers, the totals become equal. The entire proposition is thus known.

## If there are any number of quantities, and others equal in number taken in the same ratio continuously, it is necessary that the ratio of the extremes be the same.

This declares the "equal proportionality" [[notes: Known in Greek mathematics as *ex aequali* proportionality]] which occurs whenever, having taken any number of quantities compared to as many others in continuous proportion, we conclude that as the ratio of the first extreme to the last extreme is in the first set, so it is in the second set. How continuous proportionality is formed has already been said. This equal proportionality can be formed in two ways: first, directly and in the right order—as if I were to say: as **a** is to **b**, so is **d** to **e**; and as **b** is to **c**, so is **e** to **f**. It follows that as **a** is to **c**, so is **d** to **f**, for which type of equal proportionality the present proposition is set forth. Secondly, it is formed indirectly and in a reversed order—as if I were to say: the ratio that **a** has to **b**, **e** has to **f**; and the ratio **b** has to **c**, **d** has to **e**.
