# Every smaller number is either a *part* or *parts* of a larger number. <margin>1</margin>

¶ Let **a, b, c,** and **d** be any numbers: **a** being the larger, and **b, c,** and **d** being the smaller. I say that **b** is either a *part* or *parts* of **a**, and the same for **c** and **d**. For either the smaller number measures [[notes: "Measures" here means to divide evenly, as a factor]] the larger, as **d** measures **a**, and then by definition it is a *part* of **a**. Or the smaller does not measure the larger, just as **b** does not measure **a**, but there is another number that measures them both, which we shall call **e**. Then, however many times **e** is found in the smaller **b**, that is how many *parts* the smaller **b** will be of the larger **a**, according to the final definition [[notes: This refers to the definition of "parts" as a non-unit fraction, like 2/5]]. But if the smaller does not measure the larger, as **c** does not measure **a**, and no common number measures them both, then—since by the fifth common notion [[notes: A "common notion" is an axiom; here it refers to the idea that "one" is the universal measure of all integers]] unity is a part of every number named after it—you achieve the same result through the final definition. It is therefore established that every smaller number is either a *part* or *parts* of a larger one.

# Every number is the *mean* [[notes: Here meaning the arithmetic average]] of those positioned around it and equally distant from it. <margin>2</margin>
# ¶ And if it is the mean of those same numbers, it follows that they are equidistant from it.

¶ Let **a** be any number, and **b** and **c** be numbers placed around it and equidistant from it: **b** being the larger and **c** the smaller. Let **d** be the common difference, the same between **b** and **a** as between **a** and **c**. Let **e** be the number composed of **b** and **c**. I say that **a** is the half (the mean) of **e**. And if **a** is the half of **e**, I say that the surrounding numbers **b** and **c** are equidistant from **a**. This is first shown as follows: for as much as **a** exceeds **c**, **b** exceeds **a**. Therefore, when the common difference **d** is taken away from **b**, the remainder of **b** equals **a**. But also, when that same difference **d** is added to **c** (making it **f**), it equals **a**. Therefore, the remainder of **b** and **f**, which both equal the same number **a**, will be equal to each other. But the remainder of **b** plus **d** and **c** (which is **f**) equals **e**. Thus, the remainder of **b** and **f** together also equal **e**. Since the remainder of **b** and **f** were shown to be equal, **f** is half of **e**. But **f** was shown to be equal to **a**; therefore, **a** is the half of **e**. This is the first part. In all other cases, let the argument proceed similarly. The second part is proven thus: let **a** be the half of numbers **b** and **c** joined together. If **b** and **c** are not equidistant from number **a**, their differences to **a** are not equal by definition. Let **d** be the difference of **a** to **c**, which is the smaller of those numbers. I add **d** to **a**, and the sum is **g**. Since the difference of **a** to **c** is **d**, and the difference of **g** to **a** is also **d**, then by definition **g** and **c** are equidistant from **a**. Because **g** and **c** are equidistant from **a**, then by what was just demonstrated, **a** is the half of those two joined together. But **a** is also set as the half of **b** and **c** joined together. Therefore, **b** and **c** together and **g** and **c** together are equal. If the common number **c** is removed from both, the remainders **b** and **g** are equal to each other. Therefore, whatever the difference of **g** to **a**, the same will be the difference of **b** to **a**. Thus, **b** and **c** will be equidistant from number **a**. This would be contrary to the initial hypothesis [[notes: A proof by contradiction: if we assume they aren't equidistant, we end up proving they must be]], and thus the proposition stands.

# If two numbers are equally distant from two numbers positioned around them, they will be equal to those joined together. <margin>3</margin>
# ¶ And if they are equal to them, it is necessary that they are equidistant from them.

¶ The demonstration is the same as in the preceding one. Let **a** and **b** be between **c** and **d** and equidistant from them: **c** being the largest and **d** the smallest, and **f** being the common difference. Let **e** be composed of **c** and **d**, and **g** be composed of **a** and **b**. I say that numbers **a** and **b** joined together are equal to **c** and **d** joined together. Conversely, if **c** and **d** joined together are equal to **a** and **b**, then **a** and **b** are equidistant from **c** and **d**. The first part is clear: for since the difference of **a** to **c** is **d** [[notes: Lefèvre’s text here uses variables in a way that maps to arithmetic progressions, essentially showing that if $c, a, b, d$ are in progression, then $c+d = a+b$]], the sum of the extremes **c** and **d** will equal the sum of the means **a** and **b**. Since **f** is the common difference, adding it to the smaller number results in the next. Thus, **a** and **b** joined and **c** and **d** joined are equal to the same third value **e**.

Again, I say if **c** and **d** together equal **a** and **b** together, then **a** and **b** are equidistant from **c** and **d**. For I take **h**, which is equidistant to **c** as **b** is to **d**. Because they are thus equidistant, **h** and **b** together equal **c** and **d**. But **a** and **b** are also set as equal to those same **c** and **d**. Therefore, **h** plus **b** and **a** plus **b** are equal to the same third sum. Subtracting the common **b** from both, there remain **h** and **a** equal to each other. Therefore, the difference of **a** to **c** and **b** to **d** is equal. Thus, by definition, they will be equidistant to each other. This is the second part and the whole proposition.

# If the first number is the same *part* of the second as the third is of the fourth, then the first and third together will be the same *part* of the second and fourth as the first is of the second. <margin>4</margin>

¶ Let **a** be the first number, **b** the second, **c** the third, and **d** the fourth. Because **a** is the same part of **b** as **c** is of **d**, I understand **b** and **d** to be divided into those similar parts. And because the first part of one with the first part of the other is as **a** and **c**, and similarly the second with the second. This joining can be done as many times as the first is in the second. Therefore, a number equal to **a** and **c** combined can be taken from **b** and **d** combined as many times as **a** is taken from **b**. Thus, **a** and **c** together (the first and third) will by definition be the same part of **b** and **d** (the second and fourth) as the first was of the second. This is the proposition.

# If the first number is the same *parts* [[notes: i.e., a fraction like 2/3 or 3/4]] of the second as the third is of the fourth, then the first and third together will be the same *parts* of the second and fourth as the first is of the second. <margin>5</margin>

¶ Let, as before, the first number be **a**, the second **b**, the third **c**, and the fourth **d**. Let **a** be the parts of **b** taken according to **e** and denominated in **b** according to **f**. Let **g** be one of those parts of **a**, and **h** one of the parts of **c**. Since the first is the same part of the second as the third is of the fourth, by the previous rule, the sum of **g** and **h** will be the same part of **b** and **d** combined, denominated according to **f**. Since the parts of **a** and **c** are taken according to **e**, the parts of **a** and **c** combined will be related to **b** and **d** combined according to **e**, and denominated according to **f**. Therefore, they are the same *parts* as **a** was of **b**. This is the proposition.
