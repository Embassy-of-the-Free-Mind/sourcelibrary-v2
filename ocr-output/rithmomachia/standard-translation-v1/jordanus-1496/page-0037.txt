**19** **If squares are commensurable: their sides will also be commensurable. If indeed the squares are incommensurable: their sides will also be incommensurable.**

[[notes: "Commensurable" (cōmunicant) means that two numbers share a common divisor or measure. In modern terms, if the ratio of two squares is a rational number, the ratio of their square roots is also rational.]]

Let **a** and **b** be two squares, and **c** the side of **a**, and **d** the side of **b**: I say first that if the squares **a** and **b** are commensurable, then their sides **c** and **d** are also commensurable. For if **c** and **d** do not commute [are not commensurable]: then by the twelfth [proposition] of the third [book] neither do the squares **a** and **b** commute. Therefore, from the opposite of the present [statement], if the squares commute, then their sides also commute. I say secondly that if the squares **a** and **b** do not commute, neither do their sides commute. For if the sides **c** and **d** commute, there is some number measuring them commonly which is **e**: because therefore **e** numbers **c**, therefore by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book] it also numbers **a**. And because **e** also numbers **d**: by the same twenty-third it will also number **b**. If therefore the sides are commensurable, the squares are also commensurable: whereby from the opposite of the present [statement], if the squares are not commensurable, neither will the sides be commensurable: which is the second part and the whole proposition.

**20** **If cubes are commensurable: [so are] their sides. And if cubes do not commute: neither will their sides commute.**

The first part of this is evident from the first part of the preceding [proposition], and the second part from the second part of the preceding.

**21** **If there is a proportion of a square to some [number] as a square to a square: it is necessary that that [number] be a square.**

Let **a** be a square which has that proportion to some number **b** that square **c** has to square **d**: I say **b** is a square number. For because **c** and **d** are squares, by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book] there is one mean proportional between **c** and **d**, which is **e**. And because the proportion of **c** to **d** is the same as **a** to **b**: therefore by the twelfth [proposition] of the fourth [book] there will be one mean proportional between **a** and **b**, which is **f**. Because therefore **a**, **f**, and **b** are proportionals and the first, **a**, is posited as a square: therefore by the tenth [proposition] of this [book] **b**, the third, is also a square: which is what was proposed.

**22** **Whatever relates itself to a cube as a cube to a cube: the same will of necessity be a cube.**

If some number **b** relates itself to a cubic number **a** as cube **d** relates to cube **e**: I say **b** is a cube. For by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book] there will be two mean proportionals between **c** and **d**. And since the proportion of **c** to **d** is as **a** to **b**: therefore by the twelfth [proposition] of the fourth [book] there will similarly fall two mean proportionals between **a** and **b**: and since **a**, the first, is posited as a cube, **b** will therefore also be a cube by the eleventh [proposition] of this [book], which was to be demonstrated.

**23** **If a square is multiplied by a square or a cube by a cube: the side of the product will be the number produced from the side of one [multiplied] by the side of the other.**

[[notes: This is a geometric proof of the algebraic rules $(x^2 \cdot y^2) = (xy)^2$ and $(x^3 \cdot y^3) = (xy)^3$.]]

First let there be two squares **a** and **b**: and let the side of **a** be **c**: and the side of **b** be **d**. Let **a** be multiplied into **b**: and let **e** be produced, which by the fourteenth [proposition] of this [book] will be a square: and let **c** be multiplied into **d**: and let **f** be produced. I say **f** is the tetragonal [square] side of **e**. For by the seventh and eighth [propositions] of the second [book] **f** is the mean proportional between **a** and **b**. And by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the same, that which is made from **a** times **b** is equal to that which is made from **f** times itself. Therefore **f** is the side of square **e**, which was to be proved. Secondly, let **g** and **h** be cubes of the sides **c** and **d**, and let **g** multiplied into **h** produce **k**: which by the sixteenth [proposition] of this [book] will be a cube. I say **f** is the cubic side of **k**. For since **c** times **a** produces **g**, and **f** times itself produces **e**, by the ninth [proposition] of the fifth [book] the proportion of **g** to **e** is composed of the proportions of **c** to **f** and **a** to **f**. And because again from **c** times **d** comes **f**: and from **d** times **b** comes **h**: by the same ninth [proposition] the proportion of **f** to **h** will be composed of the proportions of **c** to **d** and **d** to **b**: but **c** to **d** is as **a** to **f**: and by permutation **a** to **b** is as **c** to **d**: by the same the proportion of **c** to **d** and **f** to **b** would be the same. Therefore the proportion of **f** to **h** is equal to the proportions of **a** to **f** and **c** to **f**: whereby [it is equal] to the proportion of **g** to **e**. Having arranged the four numbers **g**, **e**, **f**, **h**: because the proportion of **g** to **e** is the same as **f** to **h**, then by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book] the number produced from **g** times **h** is the same as from **f** times **e** (which is its square); whereby **f** is the side of the produced cube **k**: which is the whole proposition.

**24** **If square numbers are arranged starting from unity: [and] proportional means are taken between any adjacent ones: they will all become "longer on the other side" [oblong]. And it will be established that the difference of any of those to those [squares] is equal to its side.**

[[notes: An "oblong number" (altera parte longior) is a number of the form $n(n+1)$. Jordanus is proving that the geometric mean of two consecutive squares $n^2$ and $(n+1)^2$ is the oblong number $n(n+1)$.]]

Let **e**, **f**, **g**, **h** be any number of square numbers arranged in order from unity: their sides being unity [1]: and **b**, **c**, **d** numbers taken in order from unity. I say the mean proportionals taken between any adjacent squares are oblong numbers. For I multiply unity [1] by **b** [2] and let **l** be produced: which is the mean proportional between **e** [1] and **f** [4]. And because its sides, unity and **b**, are unequal, differing only by unity: by definition **l** is longer on the other side [oblong]. Similarly, I multiply **b** into **c** and let **l** [the second **l**] be produced, which will be the mean proportional between the adjacent squares **f** and **g**. And by the same as before, it is an oblong number. And so for the remaining proportional means of adjacent squares, however many squares were assigned in order. I say furthermore that the difference of **l z** from square **f** is equal to **b** (the side of **f**): and the difference of **l** from square **g** is equal to **c** (the side of **g**): and so on. For because **b** exceeds unity by only unity: I understand **b** to be divided into two unities. And because **b** times itself makes **f**: therefore by the thirteenth [proposition] of the first [book] **b** multiplied into the first and second unity generates an equal **f**: but having subtracted that which is made from **b** times one unity, **l** remains. Therefore, that which is subtracted (from **b** times one unity) is the difference of **l z** to **f**: but that same subtracted part is equal to **b**: because unity multiplied into any number makes the same. It is therefore established that this is true of **l z**. Concerning **l**, however, it is shown similarly: because **c** exceeds **b** by only unity. I divide therefore **c** (the side of square **g**) into **b** and unity. And because **c** times itself makes **g**, and the same **c** into **b** and unity makes an equal **g**: by the same thirteenth [proposition] of the first [book]: and having subtracted that which is made from **c** times unity, **l** remains. The difference therefore of **l** and **g** is that which is made from **c** times unity: but that also is equal to **c** by the same as before. It is therefore established what was proposed concerning the oblong number **l**. And thus you will argue concerning any others. Similarly, you will also show the difference of **l z** to **e** to be unity: and the difference of **l** to **f** to be **b**, and **m** to **g** to be **c**. For since **b** exceeds unity by only unity: having understood **b** therefore to be divided into two unities, by the fourteenth [proposition] of the first [book] that which is made from **b** times one of the unities is equal to that which is made from that [unity] into itself: and that [unity] into the other, but...
