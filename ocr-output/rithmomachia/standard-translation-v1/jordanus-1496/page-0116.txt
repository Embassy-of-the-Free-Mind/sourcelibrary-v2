### ¶ III. To demonstrate the same regularly in the enharmonic genus.

->Enharmonic Monochord.<-

[[notes: The Enharmonic genus was the most complex of the three Greek scales, utilizing "quarter-tones" which are difficult for the modern ear to distinguish but were prized in antiquity for their subtle shading.]]

<image-desc>A horizontal diagram representing an "Enharmonic Monochord." It consists of a line with endpoints 'a' and 'b'. Above the line, lowercase letters 'c' through 'r' are spaced out. Between these letters, symbols 'D' and 'LL' denote intervals. The sequence is | D | D | LL | repeated four times across the segments. Below this diagram, 'D. Diesis tetratemeria' and 'LL. Ditonus' provide the legends for the interval symbols.</image-desc>

->D. Quarter-tone (*Diesis tetratemeria*).<- ->LL. Major Third (*Ditonus*).<-

¶ Let there be, as before, a string $a$ $b$ and a ruler $c$ $r$ equal to it as in the other cases. From point $c$ to $d$, I extend a tone; from $d$ to $f$, a minor semitone; and I divide the space $d$ $f$ into equal parts at mark $e$. These will be two quarter-tones (*dieses*). From $f$ to $g$, I make two tones, which complete a major third (*ditonus*). From $g$, $h$, $i$, I make two quarter-tones as before; from $i$ to $k$, a major third; from $k$ to $l$, a tone; from $l$, $m$, $n$, two quarter-tones; from $n$ to $o$, a major third; from $o$, $p$, $q$, two quarter-tones; and from $q$ to $r$, a major third. I apply this ruler, divided in this manner, equally to the line $a$ $b$, and I mark the same signs and equal intervals on line $a$ $b$ through the intermediate letters between $a$ and $b$: $d, e, f, g, h, i, k, l, m, n, o, p, q, r$. 

And because $a$ to $d$ is a tone, and $d, e, f$ are two quarter-tones completing a minor semitone, and $f$ to $g$ is a major third—therefore $a$ to $g$ contains three tones and a minor semitone, sounding a perfect fifth (*diapente*) according to the thirteenth [proposition]. But since $g, h, i$ are two quarter-tones and $i$ to $k$ is a major third, containing two tones and a minor semitone, they form a perfect fourth (*diatessaron*) according to the sixth [proposition] of the third [book]. Therefore, as before, by the twenty-third [proposition], $a$ to $k$ contains an octave (*diapason*). In this same way, it is shown that $k$ to $r$ contains a fifth, and $k$ to $b$ an octave; thus $a$ to $r$ sounds an octave plus a fifth, and $a$ to $b$ sounds a double octave. Since this melodic progression proceeds by two quarter-tones and a major third, it is established by definition that the enharmonic monochord is regularly divided, and that the musical consonances are situated within it as proposed.

### ¶ IV. To divide the Hypaton tetrachord in the diatonic melody.

| | | |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| Proslambanomenos [[notes: The "added note" at the bottom of the scale.]] | **A** | **A** |
| Hypate hypaton [[notes: "Lowest of the low" notes.]] | **b** | **L B** |
| Parhypate hypaton | **c** | **S C** |
| Lichanos hypaton | **d** | **L D** |
| Hypate Meson [[notes: "Lowest of the middle" notes.]] | **e** | **L E** |

¶ The previous sections taught how to arrange Monochords. These following sections, however, teach the Tetrachords [[notes: Four-note scales]], Pentachords [[notes: Five-note scales]], Hexachords, and all other Poly-chords up to the fifteen-note scale (*Penthadecachordum*); once these are understood, the reader will very easily recognize whatever else might be desired. I therefore establish $b, c, d, e$ as the *hypaton* tetrachord [[notes: The lowest group of four notes in the Greek system]], so that $b$ is the *hypate hypaton*, $c$ the *parhypate hypaton*, $d$ the *lichanos hypaton*, and $e$ the *hypate meson*. To these I join at the lower end the string $a$, the *proslambanomenos*, which is not counted within the tetrachords themselves, but when added to the first tetrachord, it creates a pentachord. 

I make the interval of string $a$ to $b$ a 9:8 ratio (*sesquioctave*); from $a$ to $d$ a 4:3 ratio (*sesquitertial*); and from $a$ to $e$ a 3:2 ratio (*sesquialter*). Likewise, I make $c$ to $d$ a 9:8 ratio. I say, therefore, that the *hypaton* tetrachord is divided in the diatonic genus (as was said). For by definition, from $a$ to $b$ (*proslambanomenos* to *hypate hypaton*) will be a tone. And because $a$ to $d$ is a 4:3 ratio, likewise by definition, the *proslambanomenos* to the *lichanos hypaton* sounds a fourth (*diatessaron*). But since $a$ to $e$ (*proslambanomenos* to *hypate meson*) is a 3:2 ratio, by the same rule $a$ to $e$ sounds a fifth (*diapente*). By the thirteenth [proposition] of the third [book], therefore, $d$ is separated from $e$ (*lichanos hypaton* from *hypate meson*) by a tone. Again, since $c$ to $d$ is a 9:8 ratio, $c$ to $d$ sounds a tone. And because $a$ to $d$ is a fourth, and $a$ to $b$ is a tone and $c$ to $d$ is a tone, therefore by the sixth [proposition] of the third [book], the interval $b$ to $c$ is left as a minor semitone. 

Thus, from *hypate hypaton* to *parhypate hypaton* is a minor semitone; from *parhypate hypaton* to *lichanos hypaton* is a tone; and from *lichanos hypaton* to *hypate meson* is a tone. The *hypaton* tetrachord is established through $b, c, d, e$, proceeding by a minor semitone and two subsequent tones. By definition, this is in the diatonic genus, as is the pentachord $a, b, c, d, e$. 

But perhaps a doubt arises: why do musicians of our time place two tones at the lower part of the tetrachord, while we place only the single tone that exists between the *proslambanomenos* and the *hypate hypaton*? The answer is straightforward and ready: musicians in our time have prefixed another string a tone away from the *proslambanomenos*, and they record that Gregory was the first to do this. [[notes: A reference to the "Gamma Ut" added to the bottom of the medieval hexachord system, attributed here to Pope Gregory the Great.]]
