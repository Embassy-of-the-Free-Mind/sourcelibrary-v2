## To demonstrate the relationships of each of the enharmonic modes to one another.

¶ The demonstration of this can be made clear from the preceding section. First, the **Hypophrygian** is a tone’s distance from the **Hypodorian**; the **Hypolydian** is a tone and a diesis [[notes: In the enharmonic genus, a *diesis* is a quarter-tone, the smallest interval used in ancient Greek melody]]; the **Dorian** is a minor third [[notes: *sesquitonum*, literally "one and a half tones"]]; the **Phrygian** is a fifth [[notes: *diapente*]]; the **Lydian** is a fifth and a diesis; the **Mixolydian** is a fifth and a semitone. 

¶ Second, the **Hypolydian** is distant from the **Hypophrygian** by a diesis; the **Dorian** by a semitone; the **Phrygian** by a fourth [[notes: *diatessaron*]]; the **Lydian** by a fourth and a diesis; the **Mixolydian** by a fourth and a semitone. 

¶ Third, the **Dorian** is a diesis from the **Hypolydian**; the **Phrygian** is two tones and a diesis; the **Lydian** is a fourth; the **Mixolydian** is a fourth and a diesis. 

¶ Fourth, the **Phrygian** is two tones [[notes: *ditono*, a major third]] from the **Dorian**; the **Lydian** is two tones and a diesis; the **Mixolydian** is a fourth. 

¶ Fifth, the **Lydian** is a diesis from the **Phrygian**, and the **Mixolydian** is a semitone. 

¶ Sixth, the **Mixolydian** is distant from the **Lydian** by a diesis.
