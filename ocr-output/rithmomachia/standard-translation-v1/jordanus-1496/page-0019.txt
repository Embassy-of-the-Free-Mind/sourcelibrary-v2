¶ Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be greater than that of **c** to **d**: I say first that the product of **a** times **d** is greater than the product of **b** times **c**. Second, if the product of **a** times **d** is greater than the product of **b** times **c**, then the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. 

The first part is clear: I multiply **a** by **d** to produce **e**, and **b** by **c** to produce **f**: I say that **e** is greater than **f**. I also multiply **b** by **d** to produce **g**. Because **a** times **d** produces **e** and **b** times **d** produces **g**: therefore, by the eighth [proposition] of this book, the ratio of **a** to **b** is the same as **e** to **g**. And since **f** is produced from **b** times **c**, and **g** from **b** times **d**: by the seventh [proposition] of this book, the ratio of **c** to **d** is the same as **f** to **g**. It has thus been shown that as the ratio of **a** to **b** is to **e** to **g**, so the ratio of **c** to **d** is to **f** to **g**. But the ratio of **a** to **b** was assumed to be greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**: therefore, the ratio of **e** to **g** is greater than the ratio of **f** to **g**. Therefore, by the ninth [proposition] of this book, **e** is greater than **f**, which is the first [point]. 

Conversely, I say if **e** is greater than **f**, then the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. For because **e** is greater than **f**, by the ninth [proposition], the ratio of **e** to **g** is greater than **f** to **g**. But the ratio of **e** to **g** is the same as **a** to **b**, and the ratio of **f** to **g** is the same as **c** to **d**, as was shown. Therefore, the ratio of **a** to **b** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**, which is the second [point] and the whole of the proposal.

[[margin: 28]] ¶ Given two numbers, to investigate whether there is a third in continuous proportion with them [[notes: A "continuous proportion" exists when a:b = b:c. This proposition seeks to find the value of 'c']].
¶ Therefore, given two numbers, set whichever of them you wish as the second: multiply this one by itself. If the remaining number divides the product by some number, the number by which it divides the product will be the third number to be added to them in continuous proportionality, according to the twenty-sixth [proposition]. But if the remaining number does not divide the product by any [whole] number, by the same reasoning, no continuous proportional can be applied to them.

[[margin: 29]] ¶ Given three numbers, to investigate whether there is a [fourth] which stands to the third according to the ratio of the first to the second.
¶ This is clear with the same ease. For given three numbers, multiply the second by the third: and if the first divides the product by some number, that number by which it divides the product is, by the twenty-fifth [proposition], the one to which the third has the same ratio as the first has to the second. But if the first does not divide the product by any [whole] number, by the same twenty-fifth [proposition], no number will be added to the third in the ratio of the first to the second.

[[margin: n]] A **prime number** is said to be that which has no part [[notes: "Part" here refers to a factor or divisor]] other than the unit [[notes: The "unit" is the number 1]]. A **composite number** is that which has another number dividing it. They are called **commensurable** or **communicating** if some common number divides them. But they are said to be **prime to one another** if they are divided by no common number except the unit alone.
The **terms of a ratio** are said to be the smallest numbers between which that ratio is found.

[[margin: 1]] ¶ Every prime number is prime to every number that it does not divide.
¶ Let **a** be any prime number: I say **a** is commensurable and communicating with any number it divides, and that both it and any other number it does not divide are prime to each other. The first is clear, for if **a** divides any number, it will divide it as itself taken a certain number of times: wherefore **a** itself will divide both itself and the other; it will certainly be, by definition, commensurable and communicating with the other. The second is clear: for if **a** does not divide some number, no number will divide both of them together; otherwise, it would divide **a**, and **a** would not be prime, which is against the hypothesis. Therefore, by definition, **a** and the number it does not divide are prime to each other/and **a** is prime to it, which is the second [point].

[[margin: 2]] ¶ Every composite number is divided by some prime number.
¶ Let **a** be any composite number produced from **b** times **c**. Either **b** or **c** is prime; and thus the proposition is held regarding it. Or both are composite. If they are: then let **b** be from **d** times **e**. If again both **d** and **e** are composite, let **d** again be from **f** times **g**. If again both of them are composite, by this reasoning the numbers would decrease into infinity, which is against the petition and impossible [[notes: This is an early use of the "Infinite Descent" argument; since you cannot have an infinite sequence of strictly decreasing positive integers, you must eventually reach a prime factor]], unless one eventually comes to a number which no other divides. We shall therefore eventually reach some number which no other divides and which is for this reason prime; which, since it will be a part of a part, therefore by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], it will also be a part of the whole. Wherefore, by the definition of a part, it will divide the whole itself, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 3]] ¶ Every number is either prime or is divided by a prime.
¶ For every number is either prime or composite. But by the preceding [proposition], every composite number is divided by a prime. Therefore, every number is either prime or is divided by a prime, which the proposition intends.

[[margin: 4]] ¶ If some prime number divides a number produced from two others, it is necessary that it divides one of them.
¶ Let **a** be a number produced from **b** times **c**, which the prime number **d** divides according to **e**: I say the prime number **d** divides one of the two, **b** or **c**. And suppose it does not divide **b**: I say it divides **c**. For I understand the first number to be **d**, the second **b**, the third **c**, and the fourth **e**. Because that which is contained under the extremes **d** and **e** is equal to that which is contained under the means **b** and **c** [[notes: Jordanus is using the property that if $d \times e = b \times c$, then $d:b = c:e$]]. Therefore, by the second part of the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book]: **d, b, c, e** are proportional, so that as the ratio of **d** is to **b**, so is **c** to **e**. If, therefore, **b** were greater than **d**, then **c** is also greater than **e**, as is known from the demonstration of the second [proposition] of the second [book]. Therefore, subtracting **d** and **c** from **b** and **e** as many times as possible, let **f** and **g** be left over. It will be, by the thirtieth [proposition] of the first [book], that whatever part or parts **f** is left as of **d**, the same part or parts **g** is of **c**. If **f** is left as a part of **d**, since **d** is a prime number, **f** is the unit [[notes: Since the only "part" of a prime number is 1]]. Therefore, as many units as are in **d**, so many parts **g** is in **c**. Therefore, by the seventh [proposition] of the first [book], as much as the unit is a part of **g**, so much the part **d** will be in **c**. Therefore **d** will divide **c** itself. But if **f** is left as parts of **d**, it consists of units as was shown: and **g** will be as many parts of **c**. I will therefore take one unit from **f** which is **h**, and one part from **g** which is **k**, and I will argue as before by the seventh of the first. As much as the unit **h** is a part of **d**, so much is the part **k** to **c**. Therefore, by the seventh of the first, as much as **h** is a part to **k**, so much is the whole **d** to **c**.
