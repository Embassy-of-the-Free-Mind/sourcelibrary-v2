...is contained under the middle and the smallest: this is beyond doubt through the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], whenever the ratio of the greatest to the smallest is double. Otherwise, through the same it may not hold, but through the same always whatever the proportion of the greatest to the smallest, such will be that of what is contained under the greatest and the middle to that which is contained under the middle and the smallest. Indeed, this is common to every mediety [[notes: Jordanus is explaining a general property of means: the relationship between the products of the terms (the "rectangles" they form) mirrors the ratios of the terms themselves.]].

[[margin: 58]]
¶ **To investigate the terms of the Seventh Mediety.**
¶ This is derived from the geometric [mediety]. Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in geometric mediety [[notes: A geometric mediety is a sequence where the ratio of the terms is constant, like 4, 6, 9 (ratio 1.5).]]. From the greatest, **a**, I subtract the difference of the smaller terms, and let the remainder be **d**. It will be evident, as before, that whatever the proportion of **a** to **b** (the greatest to the middle), such is the proportion of the difference of the extremes to the difference of the larger ones. Therefore, by definition, the proposition is established.

[[margin: 59]]
¶ **Finally, it remains to explain the Eighth Mediety.**
¶ This also is derived from the geometric. Let several different numbers **a**, **b**, and **c** be in geometric mediety; let the difference of the smaller terms be added to the middle, and let the resulting sum be **d**. I say that **a**, **d**, and **b** are in this final mediety. For whatever the proportion of **a** to **b** (the greatest to the smallest), it is—with the help of the twenty-fifth [proposition] of this book—the proportion of the difference of **a** and **b** (the extremes) to the difference of **d** and **b** (the smaller terms). For this equals the difference of **b** and **c**. Therefore, by definition, **a**, **d**, and **b** are in this mediety. Thus, the eight collateral medieties shown are established.

[[margin: 60]]
¶ **It is impossible for the proportion of the greatest of three numbers to the middle to be the same as the proportion of the difference of the greatest and smallest to the difference of the middle to the smallest.**
¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any three different numbers, where **a** is the greatest, **b** the middle, and **c** the smallest. Let the difference between **a** and **c** be **d**, and the difference between **b** and **c** be **e**. I say it is impossible for the proportion of **a** to **b** (the greatest to the middle) to be the same as **d** to **e** (the difference of the greatest and smallest to the difference of the middle and smallest). For if the proportion of **a** to **b** were as **d** to **e**—that is, the ratio of the wholes is the same as the ratio of the parts removed—then the ratio of the remainders (which is **c** to **c**) would also be the same as the ratio of **a** to **b**. Since **c** to **c** is a ratio of equality (1:1), then **a** and **b** would have to be the same number and equal. This, however, contradicts our starting assumption [[notes: This is a proof by contradiction, a classic logical tool used to show that a certain mathematical arrangement is impossible because it leads to a logical absurdity.]].

[[margin: 61]]
¶ **If three means are taken between two numbers—one according to the arithmetic mediety, one according to the geometric, and a third according to the harmonic—it is necessary that the arithmetic mean be the greatest and the harmonic the smallest, and that the geometric mean be positioned proportionally between them.**
¶ Let the proportion of **a** to **c** be any ratio that can be divided into two equal parts (for example, a quadruple ratio [4:1]). Through the thirty-ninth [proposition] of this book, let **b** be found in harmonic mediety between the extremes... [Jordanus proceeds to construct the three means]. I say that **d** is the greatest of these means and **e** the smallest, and **g** is the geometric proportional mean between **h** and **e**. Because **h** is the mean between **d** and **f** according to arithmetic mediety and **e** is so according to the harmonic, then by the forty-first [proposition] of this book, **h** is greater than **f** and **e**. And because the ratio of **d** to **h** is as **g** to **f**, then by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second book, the product of **d** times **f** is equal to the product of **h** times **e**. But since **g** is the geometric mean between **d** and **f**, then by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the same book, the product of **d** times **f** equals the square of **g**. Therefore, the square of **g** is equal to the product of **h** times **e**. Consequently, **h**, **g**, and **e** are in proportion, where **h** is the greatest, **g** the middle proportional, and **e** the smallest. This is what the proposition intended to show [[notes: This is one of the most famous results in classical arithmetic: the Geometric Mean is itself the Geometric Mean between the Arithmetic Mean and the Harmonic Mean ($G^2 = A \times H$).]].

[[margin: 62]]
¶ **To investigate two numbers whose ratio is not greater than a given ratio, between which it is possible to assign means according to the three said medieties.**
¶ Let us divide any given proportion according to the sixty-ninth [proposition] of the Ninth Book into as many proportions as desired, two of which are equal, and which are not greater than the given proportion. According to that proposed proportion, I seek the harmonic mean according to the requirement of the thirty-ninth [proposition] of this book. By operating in an entirely similar way as was done at the beginning of the preceding [proposition], I will find three means in the manner described between two numbers whose ratio will not be greater than the given ratio. Thus, the proposal is established.
