### 13 ¶ If each of two numbers is prime to each of two other numbers, the product of the first two will also be prime to the product of the remaining two.

¶ Let **a** and **b** be prime to **c** and prime to **d**: let **e** be produced from **a** multiplied by **b**, and let **f** be produced from **c** multiplied by **d**. I say that **e** and **f** are prime to each other. Since each of the numbers **a** and **b** is prime to **c**, then through the tenth [proposition] of this [book], **e** is prime to **c**. And since again each of **a** and **b** is prime to **d**, therefore by the same [proposition], **e** is prime to **d**. Thus, each of the two numbers **c** and **d** has been shown to be prime to **e**; for which reason, by the same tenth [proposition], the product **f** produced from them is prime to **e**: which was to be demonstrated.

[[notes: "Prime to each other" (or "coprime") means the numbers share no common factors other than 1. This proposition states that if $gcd(a,c)=1$, $gcd(a,d)=1$, $gcd(b,c)=1$, and $gcd(b,d)=1$, then $gcd(ab, cd)=1$.]]

### 14 ¶ If two numbers are prime to each other, and the smaller is subtracted from the larger several times: either unity will remain, or a number prime to the one subtracted.

¶ Let **a-b** and **c** be prime to each other [[notes: The OCR text uses "a b et c," but the logic implies the comparison of a whole and its parts]]; let **a-b** be the larger and **c** the smaller. Let **c** be subtracted from **a-b** several times; let the part subtracted be **a** and the remainder be **b**. I say that **b** is either unity [[notes: "Unity" is the number 1]] or a number prime to **c**. For if **b** were commensurable with **c**, let **d** be their common measure. Since **d** measures **c**, then by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], it will also measure **a**. And since it also measures **b**, by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book], it will measure the whole **a-b**. Therefore, **a-b** and **c** would not be prime to each other, which is contrary to the hypothesis.

### 15 ¶ When two numbers are prime to each other, and the smaller is subtracted from the larger as much as possible, and the remainder is then subtracted from the previous one, and this process of subtraction is continued: it is necessary that unity is left. Because if unity is the remainder, it follows that the original numbers are incommensurable.

¶ Let **a** and **b** be two numbers prime to each other, with **a** being the larger and **b** the smaller. Let **b** be subtracted from **a** as many times as possible, and let **c** remain; then let **c** be subtracted as many times as possible from the number **b**, and so on with this continuous subtraction as much as possible. I say, first, that unity will finally be left. I say, second, that if unity is left by continuing the subtraction in this way, the given numbers are prime to each other. The first point is clear: let **b** be subtracted from **a** as many times as possible, and let the remainder be **c**; this **c** is less than **b**. By the preceding [proposition], it is either unity or a number prime to **b**. If it is unity, the proposition is established. If it is a number prime to **b**, I subtract it as many times as I can from **b** itself, and there remains a remainder smaller than **c**, which let be **d**. By the preceding [proposition], this **d** is likewise either unity or a number prime to **c**. If it is a number prime to **c**, I subtract it from **c**. Thus, either the number will always decrease, yielding a smaller number prime [to the previous] according to the requirement of the preceding [proposition], or unity will be left, which is the first point. The second point is also clear: for if **a** and **b** were commensurable, the same number would measure the whole and the part subtracted. Therefore, by the twelfth [proposition] of the first [book], the same number would measure the remainder—namely, unity—which is impossible. To perceive this more clearly, consider it thus: if **a** and **b** are commensurable with **d**, then **d** measures them both. Subtract **b** from **a** as many times as possible, and let **c** remain. If **c** is unity, since the number **d** measures **b**, it will measure everything measured by **b** per the 23rd of the first; therefore, it measures the whole subtracted from **a**, and it also measures **a** as was stated. Therefore, by the 12th of the first, **d** will also measure the remainder, which is **c** (unity), which is impossible. And if **c** is a number, since **d** measures the whole **a** and the part subtracted as proven, then by the 12th of the first, **d** will also measure the remainder **c**. Then subtract **c** from **b** as often as possible and let **e** remain. If **e** is unity, argue as before that the number **d** measures **e** (unity). If **e** is a number, **d** will likewise measure it. And by proceeding thus, you will find that whenever unity is given, **d** will measure it, which is impossible. And from this demonstration, deduce that if the smaller of two commensurable numbers is subtracted from the larger as often as possible, the remainder is commensurable to them.

[[notes: This is the first half of the "Euclidean Algorithm." It shows that for two numbers to be "coprime," the process of repeated subtraction (or division) must eventually result in the number 1. If it resulted in any other number, that number would be a common factor.]]

### 16 ¶ Given two commensurable numbers, to find the greatest common measure. From this, it is manifest that every number measuring any two numbers will also measure the greatest number that measures them.

¶ Let **a** and **b** be two commensurable numbers, with **a** the larger and **b** the smaller; the goal is to find the greatest number measuring them. If **b** measures **a**, since **b** also measures itself, it is the greatest common measure. But if **b** does not measure **a**, let **b** be subtracted from **a** as often as possible, and let **c** be left. If **c** measures both, it is the number sought. If it does not measure them, then again subtract **c** from **b** as often as possible, and let **d** be left, which will be smaller than **c**. If it measures both **b** and **a**, it is the one sought. But if it does not measure them—since the number cannot decrease infinitely nor fall to unity (because by the preceding proposition, **a** and **b** would then be prime to each other)—it will finally reach some number that measures the one left before it, which we shall have as the first [common measure]. I say this is the greatest number measuring them commonly. For if a larger one is proposed, it would happen by the twelfth [proposition] of the first [book] that a larger number measures a smaller one, which is impossible. From this, the corollary is known. For let **a** and **b** be commensurable numbers, and **b**, the smaller, be subtracted from **a** as often as possible, and **c** remains; and **c** subtracted from **b** as often as possible, and **d** remains, which, when subtracted from **c**, leaves nothing. Therefore, **d** will be the greatest measure of **a** and **b** through the present [proposition]. Let **g** be any common measure of **a** and **b**. I say that **g** measures **d**, the greatest measure. For if **g** were the same number as **d**, since every number measures itself, the proposition is true. If it is different from **d**, it cannot be larger than **d**, for **d** would not then be the greatest measure of **a** and **b**, but rather **g**. It must therefore be smaller. Since it measures **b**, then by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], it will measure the whole subtracted from **a**. and since it also measures **a**, by the twelfth [proposition] of the first [book], it will measure the remainder **c**. And since it measures **c**, by the twenty-third of the first, it will measure the whole subtracted from the number **b**. and since it measures **b**, therefore by the twelfth of the first, it will measure the remainder **d**: which is what was proposed.

[[notes: This proposition provides the method for finding the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD). The "corollary" mentioned is a vital principle in number theory: any common divisor of two numbers must also be a divisor of their GCD.]]

### 17 ¶ Given any number of proposed numbers, to find the greatest number that measures them commonly.

¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any number of proposed numbers; the goal is to find the greatest number measuring them. I find, through the preceding [proposition], the greatest measure of **a** and **b**, which let be **d**. If **d** measures **c**, then it is the one sought. If you were to propose a larger one, it would result through the twelfth of the first that a smaller number measures a larger one. But if **d** does not measure **c**: since whatever measures **a**, **b**, and **c** also measures **a** and **b**, and whatever measures **a** and **b**, by the corollary of the preceding [proposition], measures **d** (the greatest number measuring them), therefore **d** and **c** are commensurable. I then find, by the preceding [proposition], the greatest number measuring **c** and **d**, which let be **e**, which I say is the greatest measure of **a**, **b**, and **c**. For if there were any other number **f** larger than **e** measuring them commonly: since **f** measures **a** and **b**, by the corollary of the preceding [proposition], it measures **d**, their greatest measure. and since it measures **d** and also **c** as proposed, therefore by the same corollary, it measures **e**, the greatest measure of those. But **f** is proposed to be larger than **e**. Thus, a larger number measures a smaller one, which is impossible. It remains, therefore, that **e** is the greatest number measuring **a**, **b**, and **c**, as proposed.
