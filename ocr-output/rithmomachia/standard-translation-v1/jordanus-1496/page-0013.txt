¶ If the first is the same number of parts of the second as the third is of the fourth—the first terms always being smaller—then as many times as the first is contained in the second, so many times will the third be contained in the fourth. Furthermore, whatever part or parts of the first remain in the second, the same part or parts of the third will remain in the fourth. [[notes: This proposition describes a proportional relationship during the process of "anthyphairesis" or repeated subtraction, similar to the Euclidean algorithm. If two ratios are equal, their remainders after subtracting the smaller from the larger must also be proportional.]]

¶ Let **a** be the first number, **bc** the second, **d** the third, and **ef** the fourth. Let the first numbers **a** and **d** be smaller [than their counterparts]. Let the first **a** be as many parts of **bc** as the third **d** is of the fourth **ef**. I say that however many times **a** is in **bc**, so many times **d** is in **ef**; and whatever part or parts of the first **a** remain in the second **bc**, that same part or parts of the third **d** remain in the fourth **ef**. For I subtract the first **a** from the second **bc** as many times as I can; let the whole amount subtracted be **br** and the remainder be **c**. Let **d** be as many times in **g** as **a** is in **br**. Since it is positioned this way, I ask: what parts of **ef** are **bc**? By the twenty-eighth [proposition], whatever part or parts **a** is of **d**, the same is **bc** of **ef**. Again, I consider **a** as the first number, **br** as the second, **d** as the third, and **g** as the fourth. By a similar argument from the twenty-seventh [proposition], because **a** is the same part of **br** that **d** is of **g** by hypothesis, then by the twenty-seventh, whatever part or parts **br** is of **g**, the same part or parts **a** is of **d**. But it was shown that **a** is the same part or parts of **d** as **bc** is of **ef**; therefore, whatever part or parts **bc** is to **ef**, the same part or parts is **br** to **g**. Since **a** is the same number of parts of **bc** that **d** is of **ef**, and **a** is the same parts of **br** that **d** is of **g**—and since **br** and **g** are multiples, and **a** is fewer parts of **br** than of the whole **bc**—it follows that **d** is fewer parts of **g** than of **ef**. Therefore, let **g** be subtracted from **ef**, and let the remainder be **f**. It will be shown first by the positions... [[notes: The proof continues using the properties of multiples and remainders to show that the ratios are preserved through subtraction.]] namely, that as many times as **a** is in **bc**, so many times **d** is in **ef**, since **e** is equal to **g**. From this it is also manifest that **bc** is the same part or parts of **ef** as its segments are. For **er** and **g** are equal; the ratio of the part subtracted to the part subtracted is the same as the whole **bc** to the whole **ef**. If the number **bc** was a single part [a unit fraction], then **g** by the twenty-first will be the remainder **c**; the remainder **f** will be the same part of the whole as the whole is of the whole. If it was "parts" [a non-unit fraction], it will be by the twenty-second that the remainder **c** is the same parts of the remainder **f** as the whole **bc** is of the whole **ef**. But let it be that **bc** is the same part of **ef** as the whole **bc** is of the whole **ef**. I understand **c** as the first, **f** as the second, **bc** as the third, and **ef** as the fourth: I argue by the twenty-seventh. Because whatever part **c** is of **f**, the same part **bc** is of **ef** as has been shown: therefore, whatever part or parts **c** is to **bc**, the same part or parts **f** is to **ef**. And if **bc** were parts of **ef**, by a similar argument through the twenty-eighth, I conclude again through **c** that **c** is the same part or parts of the whole **bc** as **f** is of the whole **ef**. And this is the whole proposition.

¶ If, when the first is taken from the second as many times as the third is from the fourth, the same part or parts of the first remain in the second as those of the third remain in the fourth: then the first is necessarily the same parts of the second as the third is of the fourth.

¶ This is the converse of the preceding, which, like the preceding, is understood with the first two numbers being smaller. Let **a** be the first number as before, **bc** the second, **d** the third, and **ef** the fourth. Let the two first be smaller, and let the first **a** be taken from the second as many times as the third **d** is from the fourth; let the subtracted part of the second be **b** and the subtracted part of the fourth be **e**. Let it be that whatever part or parts of the first remain in the second, the same part or parts of the third remain in the fourth; let the remaining part be **c** in the second and **f** in the fourth. I say, therefore, that the first **a** is the same parts of the second **bc** that the third **d** is of the fourth **ef**. I take **g**, which is the same parts of **ef** that **a** is of **bc**. By the preceding [proposition], however many times **a** is in **bc**, so many times **g** will be in **ef**; and whatever part or parts of **a** remain in **bc**, the same part or parts of **g** will remain in **ef**. But it was stated that **d** is in **ef** as many times as **a** is in **bc**, and that the same part or parts of **d** remain in **ef** as parts of **a** remain in **bc**. Therefore, **g** and **d** are equal to each other. Consequently, whatever parts **a** is of **bc**, the same parts **d** is of **ef**. Which is what was proposed.

### ¶ End of the First Book of Jordanus's Elements of Arithmetic.

<margin>P</margin>
->**Ratio** (Proportion) is a definite relation in quantity of two quantities of the same kind, one to the other.<- [[notes: Jordanus defines *proportio* (ratio) here. In medieval Latin, *proportio* usually means the relationship between two terms, while *proportionalitas* means the equality of two ratios.]]
->The ratio of a number to a number is called that of the smaller to the larger<-
->in that it is a **part** or **parts**; and of the larger to the smaller according to<-
->how it contains the smaller and its part or parts.<-
->**Proportionality** is the similarity of ratios.<-
->**Continuous proportionality** is when the terms are connected without<-
disjoining the means. This consists of at least three terms, since at least one mean must be taken. **Discontinuous** (discrete) proportionality is that in which an interruption of the means occurs. This requires at least four terms because of the use of a second mean. [[notes: A continuous proportion is like $a:b = b:c$, whereas a discontinuous one is $a:b = c:d$.]] ¶ When three numbers are continuously proportional, the ratio of the first to the third is said to be the **duplicate** [squared] ratio of the first to the second, and to the fourth, **triplicate** [cubed]. ¶ When ratios are continued, whether they are the same or different, the ratio of the first to the last is said to be **composed** of them all. The **denomination** of a ratio of a smaller to a larger is the part or parts that it is of it; but for a larger to a smaller, it is the number of times it contains it and the part or parts of the smaller that are in excess in the larger. **Similar** ratios, or those that are the same as one another, are those that receive the same denomination. A **greater** ratio is that which has a greater denomination, and a **lesser** ratio that which has a lesser.

¶ If the ratio of the first to the second is the same as that of the third to the fourth, then conversely, the ratio of the second to the first will be the same as the fourth to the third.

¶ This demonstrates **inverse proportionality**, which is placed by Euclid in the fifth book of his *Geometry* as the first species of proportionality. It occurs whenever we conclude the consequent is to the antecedent as the consequent is to the antecedent, because the antecedent related to the consequent as the antecedent to the consequent. [[notes: Jordanus is explaining $a:b = c:d \implies b:a = d:c$.]] For the **antecedent** is the first extreme of the ratio, and the **consequent** is the second. Let there be four numbers: **a** the first, **b** the second, **c** the third, and **d** the fourth. Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be the same as **c** to **d**. I say that conversely, the ratio of **b** to **a** is the same as **d** to **c**. For since the ratio of **c** to **d** is the same as **a** to **b**, they will have the same denomination by definition. If, therefore, **a** is smaller than **b**, then by the definitions **a** will be a part or parts of **b**...
