[[meta: This page marks a transition in the manuscript. It concludes the fifth book of Jordanus de Nemore’s *Arithmetica*, which focused on the general properties of ratios, and begins the sixth book, which applies these ratios to geometry-based number theory—specifically "plane" and "solid" numbers like squares and cubes.]]

In two numbers, it must first be seen in the [following] form. Let there be **a** and **b**, two numbers, and **c** and **d**, two others which are compared to the first two. I say that the compounded ratio [[notes: A "compounded ratio" in medieval mathematics is what we would now call the product of two ratios (e.g., (a/b) × (c/d)).]] is the same whether it is formed from **a** to **b** and **c** to **d**, or from **c** to **d** and **a** to **b**. For if those numbers were equal, it would be immediately known of itself. Similarly, if the first two were equal to each other, and the second two were equal to each other; and likewise if three were equal and one unequal. 

But let the ratio of **a** to **b** be greater than the ratio of **c** to **d**. I make **a** to **b** the first ratio, **c** to **d** the second, and again **a** to **b** the third, and **c** to **d** the fourth. And because as much as **a** to **b** (the first) adds over **c** to **d** (the second), so much does **a** to **b** (the third) add over **c** to **d** (the fourth). Therefore, by the third [proposition] of this [book], the ratio compounded from **a** to **b** and **c** to **d** (the first and fourth) equals the ratio compounded from **c** to **d** and **a** to **b** (the second and third), which is what was intended. 

You may achieve the same in the case of three ratios. Let there be **a/b/c** and three numbers compared to them, **b/d/f**. I say the same compounded ratio results from **a** to **b**, **c** to **d**, and **e** to **f**, as from **c** to **d**, **a** to **b**, and **e** to **f**. For by what was just demonstrated, the compounded ratio of **a** to **b** and **c** to **d** is the same as **c** to **d** and **a** to **b**; therefore, when the ratio of **e** to **f** is added to both, the ratios will be equal, which was to be proven. I say likewise that the compound of **a** to **b**, **e** to **f**, and **c** to **d** is the same as the compound of **e** to **f**, **a** to **b**, and **c** to **d**. For by what was just shown, the compounded ratio of **a** to **b** and **e** to **f** is the same as **e** to **f** and **a** to **b**. Therefore, with the ratio of **c** to **d** added to both, the ratios of **a** to **b**, **e** to **f**, and **c** to **d** are mutually equal to **e** to **f**, **a** to **b**, and **c** to **d**, which was proposed to be proven. And thus you may prove other combinations; and having helped with three, you may expedite four, and so on consequently.

->¶ The end of the fifth [book] of the Arithmetical Elements of Jordanus.<-

---

Another [classification] of numbers is mentioned: those by whose multiplication numbers are produced. A **plane** (superficial) number is called that which is contained by two sides [[notes: Think of this as the area of a rectangle, $Length \times Width$]]. A **solid** number, however, is that which is under three sides, created by their continuous multiplication [[notes: Like volume, $Length \times Width \times Height$]]. A **square** is a plane number contained by equal sides. An **oblong** (*altera parte longior*) [[notes: Literally "longer in the other part," referring to a number like $n \times (n+1)$]] is one whose sides are unequal but differ by only a single unit. A **cube** is a solid number consisting of equal sides. Numbers are called **similar**, whether plane or solid, if their sides are proportional.

[[margin: 1]]
¶ If three numbers are the smallest in their proportion, the two extremes will be squares.
¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in continuous proportion and the smallest in their ratio [[notes: Meaning the ratio is in its simplest form, like 4:6:9]]. I say that **a** is a square number and likewise **c** is a square number. For by the seventh [proposition] of the fourth [book], there will be one mean proportional between **a** and unity (1), which is **d**; and similarly one between **c** and unity, which is **e**. Because, therefore, as many times as 1 is in **d**, so many times is **d** in **a**, it follows that **d** multiplied by itself produces **a**. Therefore, **a** is a square, for **d** and **d** (its sides) are equal. And since also as many times as 1 is in **e**, so many times is **e** in **c**, therefore **e** multiplied by itself produces **c**. Wherefore **c** is also a square, which is what was proposed.

---

[[margin: 2]]
¶ If there are four numbers the smallest in their proportion, the two extremes will be cubes.
¶ Let **a/b/c/d** be four numbers in continuous proportion, the smallest in their ratio. I say **a** is a cube and **d** is a cube. For by the seventh [proposition] of the fourth [book], there will be two mean proportionals between **a** and unity, which are **e** and **f**; and similarly two between **d** and unity, which are **g** and **h**. And because as many times as 1 is in **e**, so many times is **e** in **f**, therefore **e** by itself produces **f**. And as many times as 1 is in **e**, so many times is **f** in **a**, therefore **e** [multiplying] **f** (which is its own square) produces **a**. Thus **a** is a cube. By the same logic, because as many times as 1 is in **g**, so many times is **g** in **h**, **g** by itself produces **h**. And as many times as 1 is in **g**, so many times is **h** in **d**, therefore **g** [multiplying] **h** (its square) produces **d**. Wherefore **d** is also a cube, which is what was proposed.

---

[[margin: 3]]
¶ The ratio of any square to another square is the duplicate ratio of the side to the other side.
¶ Let there be two squares **a** and **b**, and the side of **a** be **c**, and the side of **b** be **d**. I say the ratio of **a** to **b** is as the ratio of **c** to **d** duplicated [[notes: In modern terms, if the sides are $c:d$, the squares are $(c:d)^2$]]. For I multiply **c** by **d** and let it become **e**. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], **a** is to **e** as **c** is to **d**. And by the eighth of the same, **e** is to **b** as **c** is to **d**. Therefore **a** is to **e** as **e** is to **b**. But the ratio of **a** to **b** consists of the ratios of **a** to **e** and **e** to **b**, each of which equals **c** to **d**. Therefore the ratio of **a** to **b** is as the ratio of **c** to **d** duplicated, as was proposed.

---

[[margin: 4]]
¶ The ratio of a cube to a cube is as the ratio of side to side triplicated; from which it is manifest that between any two squares one mean proportional exists, and between two cubes two mean proportionals exist.
¶ Let there be two cubes **a** and **b**, let the side of **a** be **c** and the side of **b** be **d**. Let **e** be the square of **c** and **f** be the square of **d**. Between these, from the preceding demonstration, there is one mean proportional, which is **g**. Then I multiply **c** by **e**, and it is certain that **a** results; and **c** by **g**, and **h** results; and **d** by **f**, and **b** results; and **d** by **g**, and **k** results. It is manifest from the preceding that the ratio of **e** to **g** is the same as **c** to **d**, and the same for **g** to **f** and **c** to **d**. But by the seventh of the second [book], **a** is to **h** as **e** is to **g**, and therefore as **c** to **d**. By the eighth of the same, **h** is to **k** as **c** to **d**. And by the seventh of the same, **k** is to **b** as **g** to **f**, and therefore as **c** to **d**. Therefore each of the three ratios (**a** to **h**, **h** to **k**, **k** to **b**) is equal to the other and equal to **c** to **d**. But the ratio of **a** to **b** consists of those three. Therefore the ratio of **a** to **b** is as the ratio of **c** to **d** triplicated. And the corollary is known from the method of demonstration and was proposed.

---

[[margin: 5]]
¶ Those whose sides are proportional have proportional squares; and if squares are proportional, it is necessary that the sides be proportional.
¶ Let **a/b/c** be three numbers in continuous proportion, whose squares are **d/e/f**. I say the squares **d/e/f** are in continuous proportion. Likewise, I say if **d/e/f** are in continuous proportion, then **a/b/c** (their sides) are proportional. The first part is immediately known from the tenth of the fifth. The second is shown thus: because the ratio of **d** to **e** (by the ninth of the fifth) is compounded of the ratios **a** to **b** and **a** to **b**, and similarly the ratio of **e** to **f** is compounded of the ratios **b** to **c** and **b** to **c**, these ratios **a** to **b** and **b** to **c** are equal. Otherwise, by the fifth of the same, when equal things are added to each, they would not constitute equal ratios of **d** to **e** and **e** to **f**, which is against the hypothesis.
