[[meta: This page continues the mathematical proof regarding the accumulation of musical tones—specifically the sesquioctave ratio (9:8). The author is demonstrating how these intervals relate to the larger perfect intervals of the Fourth (sesquitertius), the Fifth (sesquialter), and the Octave (duplex).]]

...three eighth-parts and one sixty-fourth part of one unit. And three eighth-parts are more than a third part of those [eight units]. For three eighth-parts exceed a third part of the same by one-third of one: that is, by a third part of one unit. Therefore, by even stronger reasoning, three eighth-parts and three eighths of one and one sixty-fourth are thus more than a third part of eight. Eleven, three-eighths of one, and one sixty-fourth therefore contain eight once and more than a third part of it. For which reason, **a** contains **d** and more than its third part. Thus, the interval from **a** to **d** is greater than a sesquitertius interval [[notes: A sesquitertius is a ratio of 4:3, which is 1 plus 1/3. The author is proving that three "whole tones" (9:8) are larger than a perfect Fourth]]. Second, I say that the interval from **a** to **d** is less than a sesquialter interval. For eleven [units] and three eighths and one sixty-fourth contain eight and three-eighths; therefore, one eighth is lacking to complete four eighths, which are half of eight. But indeed, the remainder—three eighths of one and one sixty-fourth—make up less than half of one-eighth. For which reason, they make up much less than one-eighth. Eleven, therefore, and three eighths, and one sixty-fourth of one, contain eight once and less than half of eight. Therefore, by the tenth proposition of this book, **a** contains **d** once and less than its half part. Thus, the interval from **a** to **d** is less than a sesquialter interval [[notes: A sesquialter is 3:2 or 1.5. He is showing that three tones are smaller than a perfect Fifth]].

¶ Four sesquioctaves [[notes: A "sesquioctave" is the ratio 9:8, representing a major whole tone]] joined together exceed the sesquialter interval [[notes: A "sesquialter" is the ratio 3:2, representing a perfect Fifth]].

| 6561 | | | | 4096 | ¶ Four sesquioctaves |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **a** | **b** | **c** | **d** | **e** | **joined together.** |

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, **d**, and **e** be four joined sesquioctaves: **a** to **b** is the first, **b** to **c** the second, **c** to **d** the third, and **d** to **e** the fourth. I say that the interval **a** to **e** is greater than a sesquialter interval. For as was seen in the preceding [argument], eight of **a** are equal to eleven of **e**, three-eighths, and one sixty-fourth of one. And eleven of **d**, three eighths of one, and one sixty-fourth are equal to twelve of **e**, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth part [[notes: The author is using long-form fractional expansion to track the compounding of the 9:8 ratio]]. Therefore, eight of **a** are equal to twelve of **e**, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth. But indeed, twelve, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth contain eight once and more, and half of eight; since they contain eight once and a half [which is four], and moreover six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth of one. Therefore, by the tenth proposition of this book, **a** contains **e** once and more than its half part. Thus, the interval **a** to **e**, joined from four sesquioctaves, exceeds the sesquialter interval. This was what was to be shown.

[[margin: 20]]
¶ Five sesquioctaves joined together: they join to less than a double interval [[notes: A "double interval" is the ratio 2:1, representing a perfect Octave]].

¶ For by the eleventh proposition of this book, a double interval is joined from the two largest subparticulars: namely, the sesquialter and the sesquitertius [[notes: A Fifth (3/2) + a Fourth (4/3) = an Octave (2/1)]]. But with five sesquioctaves set forth, the first three sesquioctaves (by the eighteenth proposition of this book) are less than a sesquialter interval. And the two following sesquioctaves, which with the three prior ones complete five, are (by the seventeenth proposition of this book) less than a sesquitertius. Therefore, five sesquioctaves join to less than a double interval.

[[margin: 21]]
¶ Six sesquioctave proportions are greater than one double interval.

| 531441 | | | | | | 262144 | ¶ Six sesquioctaves |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| **a** | **b** | **c** | **d** | **e** | **f** | **g** | **joined together.** |

¶ Let **a** to **g** be the interval of six joined sesquioctaves, so that **a** to **b** is the first sesquioctave, **b** to **c** the second, **c** to **d** the third, **d** to **e** the fourth, **e** to **f** the fifth, and **f** to **g** the sixth. I say that **a** to **g** is more than a double interval. For since **a** to **b** is sesquioctave, then by the eighth proposition of this book, eight of **a** are equal to nine of **b**. And nine of **b**, by the same, are equal to ten of **c** and an eighth of one. And ten of **c** and an eighth of one are equal to eleven of **d**, three eighths, and one sixty-fourth of one. And eleven of **d**, three eighths, and one sixty-fourth of one are equal to twelve of **e**, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth. Moreover, twelve of **e**, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth are equal to thirteen of **f**, ten eighths, ten sixty-fourths, five five-hundred-and-twelfths, and one four-thousand-and-ninety-sixth. But indeed, ten eighths contain one whole unit and moreover two eighths. Thus it happens again that twelve of **e**, six eighths, four sixty-fourths, and one five-hundred-and-twelfth are equal to fourteen of **f**, two eighths, ten sixty-fourths, five five-hundred-and-twelfths, and one four-thousand-and-ninety-sixth. Indeed, fourteen of **f**, two eighths, ten sixty-fourths, five five-hundred-and-twelfths, and one four-thousand-and-ninety-sixth are equal to fifteen of **g** and eight eighths of it (that is, they are equal to sixteen of **g**), twelve sixty-fourths, fifteen five-hundred-and-twelfths, six four-thousand-and-ninety-sixths, and one thirty-two-thousand-seven-hundred-and-sixty-eighth. But sixteen and twelve sixty-fourths and the remaining following small parts contain eight twice and more [[notes: Since the base number was 8, doubling it makes 16; anything over 16 proves the ratio is greater than 2:1]]. Therefore, by the tenth proposition of this book, **a** to **g** is greater than a double interval. Thus, six sesquioctaves are greater than one double interval, as the proposition intends [[notes: This is the mathematical proof of the "Pythagorean Comma"—the fact that six whole tones do not perfectly equal one octave, but slightly exceed it]]. [[gloss: ¶ And if this proposition, as far as its demonstration goes, seems somewhat difficult to some, this happens because they lack the readiness and skill in using the eighth, ninth, and tenth propositions of this book. Therefore, it will be fitting and agreeable for those who wish to exercise themselves in musical melodies and their contemplations to collect many sesquialters, then sesquitertials, and other following subparticulars, until the habit of collecting these proportions has become easy for them, clear, and as it were, second nature.]]
