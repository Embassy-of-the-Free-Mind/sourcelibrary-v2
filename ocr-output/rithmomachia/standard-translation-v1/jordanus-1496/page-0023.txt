# ¶ **Given any number of numbers: to find the least number measured by them.**
[[notes: This proposition describes how to find the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of a set of numbers.]]

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be any number of numbers: it is proposed to find the least number measured by them [[notes: In this context, "measured by" means the smallest number that all these numbers can divide into without a remainder]]. First, I look for the least number that **a** and **b** measure. If **a** measures **b**, then it is certain that **b** is the least number measured by them. If **a** and **b** are prime to each other, I multiply one by the other; and because they are prime, then by the twentieth [proposition] of this book, they will be the least in their proportion. Therefore, by the preceding [proposition], the product of them will be the least they measure. But if they share a common factor [[notes: "Communicating" numbers share a common divisor other than 1]], I take the least they measure by the preceding [proposition], which shall be **e**. Similarly, I shall take the least measured by **c** and **e**, which shall be **f**, and the least measured by **d** and **f**, which shall be **g**. I say that **g** is the least number that **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** measure. For they will measure it by the 23rd [proposition] of the first book. That they do not measure any smaller number is clear: for if there were a number **h** smaller than **g** that **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** measure: since **a** and **b** measure **h**, then by the corollary of the preceding [proposition], **e** (the least they measure) measures **h**. And because **e** and **c** measure **h**, then by the same corollary, **f** (the least they measure) measures **h**. And because **f** and **d** are also posited to measure it, then by the same corollary, **g** (the least measured by **f** and **d**) will measure **h**. But **h** is posited to be smaller than **g**; thus a larger number measures a smaller one, which is impossible.

# ¶ **If a prime number measures the least number measured by any numbers, it is necessary that it also measures at least one of those numbers.**

¶ In the case of two numbers, this is easily weighed. Let **a** and **b** be two numbers, **e** the least they measure, and **d** a prime number measuring **e**. I say **d** measures **a** or **b**. For let **a** measure the least **e** according to **e** [[notes: Here, the text uses "e" as both the product and a multiplier; it likely refers to a quotient here]]; therefore by the 24th of this book, **e** is one of the smaller numbers of the proportion of **a** to **b** measuring **b**. Then thus: **d**, a prime number, measures **e**, which is the product of the two numbers **a** and **e**. Therefore, by the fourth [proposition] of this book, **d** measures one of the numbers **a** or **e**. If it measures **a**, the proposition stands; if **e**, likewise, for by the twenty-fourth [proposition] it will measure **b**. In the case of more numbers, it is shown similarly. Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any numbers and **d** the least measured by them; let **e** be a prime number measuring **d**. I say **e** measures **a**, **b**, or **c**. For I take, according to the requirement of the preceding [proposition], the least number measured by **a** and **b**, which shall be **f**; and the least number measured by **f** and **c**, which from the preceding is clearly **d**. And let **e** measure the least **d** according to **g**. By the 24th of this book, it is one of the numbers of the proportion of **f** to **c**, and **g** measures **f**. Thus, since the prime number **e** measures **d**, which is the product of two numbers **c** and **g**, then by the fourth of this book, **e** measures one of the numbers **c** or **g**. If it measures **c**, the proposition stands; if it measures **g**, then by the twenty-fourth [proposition] it measures **f**. Because **f** is the least number measured by **a** and **b**, let **a** measure the least **f** according to **h**, which by the often-cited twenty-fourth [proposition] will be one of the smaller numbers of the proportion of **a** to **b** and measuring **b**. Since **e** is proven to measure **f**, which is the product of the two numbers **a** and **h**, then by the fourth of this book, it measures **a** or **h**. If it measures **a**, the proposition stands. If **h**, likewise: for **h** measures **b**, whereby **e**, by the twenty-third of the first [book], will also measure **b**. And thus the proposition is proved.

# ¶ **When any numbers are prime to some other number, the least number they measure will also be prime to that same number.**

¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any numbers prime to **d**, and let **e** be the least number measured by **a**, **b**, and **c**. I say **e** is prime to **d**. For if **d** is commensurable to **e** [[notes: "Commensurable" means they share a common divisor]], then there is some number common to both, which shall be **f**. If **f** is a prime number, since it measures **e**, then by the preceding [proposition] it will measure **a**, **b**, or **c**. And since it also measures **d**, then **a**, **b**, and **c** would not be prime to **d**, which is against the hypothesis. But if **f** is a composite number, then by the second [proposition] of this book, **f** is measured by some prime, which shall be **g**, which by the twenty-third of the first [book] will measure **d** and **e**. Because **g** is prime and measures **e**, then by the preceding [proposition] it will measure **a**, **b**, or **c**, and also measures **d**. Therefore **a**, **b**, and **c** are not prime to **d**, which is against the hypothesis.

# ¶ **If the least number measured by several assigned prime numbers is taken, it is impossible for any prime number other than those to measure it.**

¶ Let **e** be the least number measured by any given prime numbers **a**, **b**, and **c**. I say no prime other than these measures **e**. For if there were another, such as **d**, which measures **e**, then by [Proposition] 26 it would also measure **a**, **b**, or **c**, which is against the hypothesis [[notes: Because a, b, and c are prime, only they themselves or 1 could measure them]].

# ¶ **To find the least number having parts of the proposed denominations.**
[[notes: This is the practical application of the LCM: finding a common denominator for fractions. For example, if you have parts called "a half" and "a third," the least number containing both is 6.]]

¶ If it is proposed to find the least number having parts of the proposed denominations—for example, one half, one third, one fourth, and one sixth—I take numbers **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** as the denominators of those parts, of which the least they measure is **e**. Let **a** measure the least **e** according to **f**, and **b** measure the same according to **g**, and **c** the same according to **h**, and **d** the same according to **k**. Alternately, therefore, by the eighth of the first [book], **f**, **g**, **h**, and **k** will measure **e** according to the given numbers **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d**. Therefore, in **e** there will be the parts denominated by **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d**, and I say **e** is the least in which those parts may be found. That it has those parts is already known; but if it were not the least, let **l** be smaller than it, having those parts, and let its parts denominated by those be **m**, **n**, **o**, and **p**. Therefore, alternately by the eighth [proposition], **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** would be the parts of **l** denominated by **m**, **n**, **o**, and **p**. Thus **e** was not the least measured by **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d**, for **l** is posited as smaller, which is against the hypothesis.
