¶ Let **a, b, c, d, e,** and **f** be any number of numbers: and let **d, e,** and **f** be equimultiples [[notes: "Equimultiples" are numbers that have been multiplied by the same factor; for example, if a=2 and d=4, then b=3 and e=6 are equimultiples because both were doubled]] of **a, b,** and **c** respectively. I understand **a** as the first, **d** as the second, **b** as the third, and **e** as the fourth: and by the penultimate proposition [[notes: Referring to Proposition 6 from the previous page]], the sum of **a** and **b** (the first and third) is the same part of the sum of **d** and **e** (the second and fourth) as **a** is of **d**. Therefore, the sum of **d** and **e** is an equimultiple to the sum of **a** and **b**, just as **d** is to **a**. I then make the sum of **a** and **b** the first number, the sum of **d** and **e** the second, **c** the third, and **f** the fourth: and I argue in the same way. Since **a** is the same part of **d** as **c** is of **f**, the sum of **a, b,** and **c** (the first and third) will be the same part of the sum of **d, e,** and **f** (the second and fourth) as the sum **a** and **b** is of the sum **d** and **e**. Therefore, the sum **d, e,** and **f** is an equimultiple to the sum **a, b,** and **c**, just as **d** is to **a**. And so, if more numbers were to be added, you would always use the sum of the preceding ones until you reach the very last; and the proposition will be achieved.

## 7 If as many times as unity is in the first, so many times is the second in the third: then as many times as unity is in the second, so many times is the first in the third.
¶ Let **a, b,** and **c** be three numbers: **a** the first, **b** the second, and **c** the third. Let it be that however many times unity [[notes: The number 1]] is in **a**, that is how many times **b** is in **c**. I say, therefore, that however many times unity is in **b**, that is how many times **a** is in **c**. For I divide the first number **a** into its units, and the third number **c** into just as many parts, each of which will be equal to the second number **b**. Because as many times as the first part of **a** is in the first part of **c**, so many times is the second in the second, and the third in the third, and so on. Therefore, by the preceding proposition, the whole sum **a** will be the same part of the whole sum **c** as its first part is to the first part of **c**; and it is the same multiple as its first part is to the first part of **a**. But as many times as unity is in **b**, so many times is the first part of **a** in the first part of **c**. For unity equals the first part of **a**, and **b** equals the first part of **c**. Therefore, however many times unity is in the second number **b**, that is how many times the first number **a** is in the third number **c**. Which was to be demonstrated.

## 8 If the multiplication of two numbers is performed alternately, the same number will result in both cases.
¶ But if **a** multiplies **b** and results in **c**, I say that if **b** multiplies **a**, **c** likewise results. For if **a** multiplies **b** and results in **c**, by definition, however many times unity is in **a**, that is how many times **b** will be in **c**. Therefore, by the previous proposition, as many times as unity is in the second number **b**, so many times the first number **a** is in the third number **c**. By definition, therefore, if **a** is added to itself as many times as there is unity in **b**, then by definition **a** is multiplied by **b**. But when this is done, **c** results. For it has been proved that **a** is added up in **c** as many times as unity is in **b**. The proposition is therefore established. [[notes: This is the Commutative Law of Multiplication: a × b = b × a]]

## 9 What results from the drawing [[notes: "Ductu" (drawing or leading) is the traditional term for multiplication, imagining a line being extended to create an area]] of any number into any others, is as much as what results from the drawing of the same into the sum of those others.
¶ Let **a** be a number which is multiplied by **b** to produce **d**, and by **c** to produce **e**. I say, therefore, that the sum of **d** and **e** is produced by the multiplication of **a** by the sum of **b** and **c**. For since **b** multiplies **a**, **b** (by definition) measures **d** according to **a**; and by the same logic, **c** measures **e** according to **a**. Therefore, **d** and **e** are equimultiples of **b** and **c**. Thus, by the sixth proposition, the sum of **d** and **e** is an equimultiple to the sum of **b** and **c**. Let **f** be the product of the multiplication of **a** by the sum of **b** and **c**. Since **b** multiplies **f** according to **a**, the number **b** and **c** [[notes: meaning the sum of b and c]] will measure this **f** according to **a**. Therefore, **f** is the same multiple of the sum **b** and **c** as **d** is of **b**. But it was proved that the sum **d** and **e** is the same multiple of that same sum **b** and **c**. Therefore, the sum **d** and **e** and the product **f** are equimultiples of the same sum **b** and **c**; and by the third common notion [[notes: An axiom stating that things equal to the same thing are equal to each other]], they are equal to one another. Therefore, what results from the multiplication of a number into any others is the same as what results from the multiplication of the same into their sum, just as was proposed. [[notes: This is the Distributive Law: a(b+c) = ab + ac]]

## 10 What results from the drawing of any numbers into one is equal to that which results from the sum of those into the same.
¶ Let the hypothesis be the same as the preceding one but in reverse order: so that **b** and **c** are multiplied by **a**, producing **d** and **e**. I say that what results from multiplying **b** and **c** by **a** individually is equal to what results from the sum of **b** and **c** multiplied by **a**. For by the preceding proposition, what results from multiplying **a** by **b** and **c** individually is equal to what results from multiplying **a** by the sum of **b** and **c**. Alternately, by the eighth proposition, what results from multiplying **b** and **c** by **a** is equal to what results from the sum of **b** and **c** into the same. This is the proposition.

## 11 What results from the drawing of any numbers into any numbers is equal to that which results from the sum of those into the sum of the others.
¶ Let **a, b,** and **c** be any numbers which are multiplied by **d, e,** and **f**, any other numbers, so that each of the first is multiplied by each of the second. I say that what results from the multiplication of **a, b,** and **c** individually into each of the others, **d, e,** and **f**, is equal to what results from multiplying the sum of **a, b,** and **c** by the sum of **d, e,** and **f**. For what results from the multiplication of **a, b,** and **c** individually into **d** is equal to what results from their sum multiplied by **d**. And you shall argue similarly for the multiplication of **a, b,** and **c** individually into **e** and **f**, which is nothing other than what results from the multiplication of **a, b,** and **c** into **d, e,** and **f** individually: by the preceding proposition, this is equal to what results from the sum of **a, b,** and **c** into **d, e,** and **f** individually. But by the ninth proposition, what results from the sum of **a, b,** and **c** individually multiplied by **d, e,** and **f** is equal to what results from their sum multiplied by the sum of **d, e,** and **f**. Therefore, it is established that what results from the multiplication of any numbers into any numbers is equal to what results from the sum of those into the sum of the others. And these four propositions—the eighth, ninth, tenth, and eleventh—clearly demonstrate the method of multiplication and its parts.

## 12 Whatever number measures the whole and the part taken away, also measures the remainder.
¶ Let **ab** be the whole, **a** the part taken away, and **b** the remainder. I say that if **c** measures [[notes: "Measures" means c is a divisor of the number]] **ab** and **a**, it also measures **b**. For let **c** measure **ab** according to the number **de**, and let it measure **a** according to **d**. Because, by the ninth proposition, however much **c** is in **de**, it is that much in **d** and in **e**. But **a** is equal to what results from **c** in **d**; therefore **a** with what results from **c** in **e** is equal to **ab**. Thus, what results from **c** in **e** is equal to **b**. But **c** measures what results from **c** in **e** by definition; therefore it will also measure its equal, **b**. This is the proposition.

## 13 As much as results from the drawing of a number into itself is as much as results from the drawing of the same into all its parts.
¶ Let **a** be any number, which I set down once as divided into all its parts (which are **b, c, d,** and **e**) and once as undivided. Then, what results from **a** into **b, c, d,** and **e** is, by the ninth proposition, equal to what results from **a** into the sum of **b, c, d,** and **e**. But the sum of **b, c, d,** and **e** is **a**. Therefore, what results from the multiplication of a number by all its parts is equal to what results from multiplying the same number by itself. Therefore, alternately by the eighth proposition, what results from multiplying a number by itself is as much as what results from multiplying the same by all its parts.
