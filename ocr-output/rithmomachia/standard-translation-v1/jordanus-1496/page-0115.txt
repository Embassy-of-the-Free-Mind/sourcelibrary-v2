[[meta: This page continues the mathematical and structural demonstration of how to divide the monochord—a single-stringed instrument used for scientific tuning—into the specific intervals of the Diatonic and Chromatic Greek musical systems. It relies on geometric propositions established in earlier sections of the treatise.]]

I take a very smooth ruler, $c$, that does not wobble at any point, equal to the line $ab$. Following the first and eighteenth [propositions] of the second [book] of this work, I extend a tone from $c$ to $d$; a minor semitone from $d$ to $e$; and from $e$ to $f$ and $f$ to $g$, I extend two tones. From $g$ to $h$, a minor semitone; from $h$ to $i$ and $i$ to $k$, two tones. Again, from $k$ to $l$, a tone; and from $l$ to $m$, a minor semitone; from $m$ to $n$ and $n$ to $o$, two tones; from $o$ to $p$, a semitone; from $p$ to $q$ and $q$ to $r$, two tones. Thus, two tones are continually joined to a minor semitone, except for $cd$, which is the tone placed before the first semitone for the **proslambanomenos** <note>The "added note," the lowest note in the Greek musical system</note>, and the tone $kl$, which is the beginning of the **disjunct tetrachord** <note>The diezeugmenon; a set of four notes separated by a whole tone from the middle notes</note>.

Again, I apply the whole harmonic ruler to the whole string $ab$, so that $c$ aligns with $a$ and $r$ with $b$. On the string $ab$, where the points $d, e, f, g$ and the rest of the ruler's marks are applied, I mark them as $d, e, f, g, h, i, k, l, m, n, o, p, q$. I say that the monochord $ab$ is regularly divided in the **diatonic genus** <note>The most common musical "genus," using a sequence of tones and semitones</note>. Since its intervals correspond to the intervals of the ruler $cr$, they turn out to be equal: $ab$ and $db$ sound a tone, $db$ and $eb$ a semitone, and again $eb$ and $fb$ contain a tone. Therefore, $ab$ and $fb$, containing two tones and a minor semitone, sound a **diatessaron** <note>A perfect fourth</note> by the sixth [proposition] of the third [book]. And because $fb$ and $gb$ contain a tone (as on the ruler), and $gb$ and $hb$ a minor semitone, and $hb, lb,$ and $kb$ two tones, therefore by the tenth [proposition] of the third [book], $fb$ and $kb$, containing three tones and a minor semitone, sound a **diapente** <note>A perfect fifth</note>.

But it has also been shown that $ab$ and $fb$ modulate a diatessaron; by the twenty-third [proposition] of the third [book], $ab$ and $kb$—which is formed from the joining of the consonances of the diatessaron and diapente—sound a **diapason** <note>An octave</note>. Again, $kb$ and $lb$ sound a tone, $lb$ and $mb$ a minor semitone, $mb, nb,$ and $ob$ two tones; wherefore $kb$ and $ob$, containing three tones and a minor semitone, sound a diapente by the tenth [proposition] of the third [book]. But $ab$ and $kb$ have been shown to be the diapason consonance; therefore $ab$ and $ob$ sound a **diapason and a diapente** <note>An octave plus a fifth, or a compound twelfth</note>. Furthermore, since $ob$ and $pb$ is a semitone and $pb, qb,$ and $rb$ are two tones, therefore $ob$ and $b$ sound a diatessaron. Thus $kb$ and $b$, consisting of a diapente and a diatessaron, sound a diapason by the twenty-third [proposition] of the third [book]. And indeed, $ab$ and $kb$ were shown to sound a diapason. Therefore, $ab$ and $b$ sound a **bis diapason** <note>A double octave</note>. And because this partition of the monochord proceeded through semitones and two tones, and these harmonic consonances just shown were investigated by the support of the ruler, the regular partition of the monochord in the diatonic genus is demonstrated by definition, which was the goal.

¶ In every Monochord and **Polyphord** <note>A multi-stringed instrument</note>, this property is perpetually observed: that sometimes the eighth note, sometimes the string of the first, and the fifteenth of the eighth, rings back in the consonance of the diapason [octave]. And everywhere a tone is prefixed for the **proslambanomenos**, and a tone is inserted everywhere between the **mese** and **paramese** <note>The central notes where the scale's two halves are disconnected</note> for the disjunct song. This is to be found in monochords from the first note to the second and from the eighth to the ninth.

I take the very smooth ruler $cr$ and assign it to be equal to the string. I make $cd$ a tone by the first [proposition] of the second [book]; and by the eighteenth of the same, $e$ a minor semitone; and again by the first [proposition], $df$ the interval of a tone. Therefore $ef$ will be a **major semitone** <note>The difference between a whole tone and a minor semitone, often called an apotome</note>; and $de$ and $ef$ are two semitones. From $f$ to $g$ I measure a tone and a minor semitone, which will be equal to a **trihemitone** <note>Literally "three half-tones"; a minor third, characteristic of the chromatic genus</note>; and I make $gh$ and $hi$ two semitones (the minor semitones); and $no$ a trihemitone. And again $o, p, q$ two semitones; $qr$ however a trihemitone.

And I apply the ruler divided in this way equally to the line $ab$, and on the line $ab$ I mark notes at similar and proportional distances: namely $a, d, e, f, g, h, i, lz, l, m, n, o, p, q, b$. In this way, I say the line $ab$ is regularly divided in the **chromatic melody**. For $ad$ is a tone and $df$ is a tone and $fg$ is a tone and a minor semitone. Wherefore $a$ and $g$ contain three tones and a minor semitone; therefore, by the tenth [proposition] of the third [book], $a$ to $g$ sounds a diapente. And $g, h, i$ contain a tone, and $i, lz$ a trihemitone; therefore $g$ to $lz$, containing two tones and a minor semitone, modulates a diatessaron by the sixth [proposition] of the third [book]. But $ag$ has also been proven to be a diapente; therefore $alz$, consisting of $ag$ and $glz$, sounds a diapason by the twenty-third [proposition] of the third [book]. And by following the exact same reasoning, proving $lzo$ sounds a diapente and $lzb$ a diapason, wherefore $ao$ sounds a diapason and diapente, and $ab$ will ring back a **bis diapason** [double octave]. The authority of the **Pythagoreans** <note>Followers of Pythagoras, who believed the universe and music were governed by whole-number ratios</note> forbids us to exceed this consonance. He who wishes to proceed further from these things which have now been demonstrated...
