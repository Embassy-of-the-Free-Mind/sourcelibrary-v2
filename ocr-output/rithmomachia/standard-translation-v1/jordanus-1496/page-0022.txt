## ¶ Any two numbers that are the least in their proportion are prime to each other. <margin>18</margin>

[[notes: "Least in their proportion" refers to a ratio reduced to its lowest terms, such as 2:3 rather than 4:6.]]

Let **a** and **b** be two numbers that are the least in their proportion: I say that they are prime to each other. For if they were composite [[notes: "Composite" here means "not prime to each other," implying they share a common factor]], let their common measure be **c**, which measures **a** according to **d** and **b** according to **e**. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book] [[notes: Likely referring to a previous book's rule on ratios]], the proportion of **d** to **e** is the same as **a** to **b**. But since **d** is less than **a** and **e** is less than **b**, then **a** and **b** were not the least in their proportion, which contradicts what was posited.

## ¶ Any numbers that are the least in their proportion measure any others taken in the same proportion—the smaller the smaller, and the larger the larger—an equal number of times. <margin>19</margin>

Let **a** and **b** be the least in their proportion, where **a** is the larger and **b** the smaller. Let **c** and **d** be in that same proportion, where **c** is larger and **d** is smaller. I say that **a** measures **c** and **b** measures **d** equally [[notes: "Equally" means they have the same multiplier; for example, if a goes into c three times, b goes into d three times]]. For since as **a** is to **b**, so **c** is to **d**, then by permutation (the third [proposition] of the second [book]), as **a** is to **c**, so **b** is to **d**. Therefore, whatever part or parts **a** is of **c**, the same part or parts **b** is of **d**. If it is a single part [[notes: i.e., a unit fraction like 1/3]], the proposition stands: **a** measures **c** and **b** measures **d** equally. If it is multiple parts [[notes: a non-unit fraction like 2/3]], then by the thirtieth [proposition] of the first [book], **a** will be in **c** as many times as **b** is in **d**. And whatever part or parts of **a** remain in **c**, the same part or parts of **b** will remain in **d**. I subtract **a** from **c** as many times as I can, let the subtracted part be **e** and the remainder **f**. Similarly, I subtract **b** from **d** as much as I can, let the subtracted part be **g** and the remainder **h**. Because **a** measures **e** and **b** measures **g** equally and according to the same number, then by the eighth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **a** to **b** is the same as **e** to **g**. Therefore, as the proportion of the whole **c** to the whole **d**, so is the subtracted **e** to the subtracted **g**. Thus, by the fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of the whole **c** to the whole **d** is the same as the remainder **f** to the remainder **h**. Therefore, again, the proportion of **a** to **b** is the same as **f** to **h**. But since the remainders **f** and **h** are smaller than **a** and **b**, then **a** and **b** were not the least in their proportion, which contradicts the hypothesis. It is therefore established that whatever numbers are least in their proportion measure the others as stated.

## ¶ If two numbers are prime to each other, they will be the least in their proportion. <margin>20</margin>

Let **a** and **b** be numbers prime to each other, with **a** larger and **b** smaller. I say they are the least in their proportion. For if there were others that were the least in that proportion, let them be **c** and **d**. Since **c** and **d** are the least in that proportion, then by the preceding [proposition], they will measure any others in the same proportion—the smaller the smaller and the larger the larger—equally. Therefore, let **c** measure **a** according to **e**, and let **d** measure **b** according to the same number **e**. It is manifest that **e** will measure both **a** and **b**; for which reason, **a** and **b** are not prime to each other, which contradicts the hypothesis.

## ¶ Given two numbers prime to each other, if one is multiplied by a number which the other does not measure, the product will also not be measured by that other. <margin>21</margin>

Let **a** and **b** be numbers prime to each other, and let **a** be multiplied by **c** to produce **d**. If **b** does not measure **c**, I say that **b** does not measure **d**. For if **b** were to measure **d**, let it measure it according to **e**. I understand the first number to be **a**, the second **b**, the third **e**, and the fourth **c**. Since the product of the extremes **a** and **c** is equal to the product of the means **b** and **e**, then by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **a** to **b** is the same as **e** to **c**. Because **a** and **b** are prime, they are (by the preceding [proposition]) the least in their proportion. Therefore, by the one before the preceding [[notes: Prop 19]], **a** will measure **e** and **b** will measure **c** equally. This contradicts the hypothesis, for it was posited that **b** does not measure **c**.

## ¶ If a third number measures a product of two numbers, it will either measure one of them or be commensurable to both. <margin>22</margin>

Let **a** be the number produced from **b** multiplied by **c**, and let **d** measure **a**. I say that **d** measures **b** or **c**, or is commensurable to both. For if it were incommensurable to one, for example **b**: since **b** and **d** are prime to each other and **b** multiplied by **c** produces **a**, then by the preceding [proposition], if **d** does not measure **c**, then **d** will not measure **a**. But **d** was posited to measure **a**. Therefore, **d** measures the other of them. If it is incommensurable to one, it necessarily measures the other; therefore it cannot be incommensurable to both in such a way that it neither measures one nor is commensurable to both, as proposed.

## ¶ To find the least numbers in the proportion of assigned numbers. It is clear from this that the greatest common measure of two numbers measures them according to the least numbers of that proportion. <margin>23</margin>

[[notes: This proposition explains how to reduce a ratio like 12:9 to its simplest form, 4:3, by dividing both by their Greatest Common Divisor (3).]]

Let **a** and **b** be in some proportion; the task is to find the least numbers of the proportion of **a** to **b**. Either **a** and **b** are prime to each other, and then by the twentieth [proposition] of this [book] they are the least in their proportion and no others need be sought. Or they are composite, and then by the sixteenth [proposition] of this [book] I seek the greatest number measuring them, which is **c**. Let **c** measure **a** according to **d**, and **b** according to **e**. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **a** to **b** is the same as **d** to **e**. I say that **d** and **e** are the least numbers in that proportion. For if there were others, let them be **f** and **g**, which are the least in that proportion. Therefore, by the nineteenth [proposition] of this [book], they measure **a** and **b**—the larger the larger and the smaller the smaller—according to the same number, let it be **h**. I consider four numbers: **c** the first, **f** the second, **h** the third, and **d** the fourth. Since **a** is produced from the extremes **c** and **d**, and the same **a** from the means **f** and **h**, then by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the proportion of **c** to **f** is as **h** to **d**. Then by permutation (the third [proposition] of the second [book]), the proportion of **c** to **h** is the same as **f** to **d**. But **f** was posited to be less than **d**, therefore **c** is less than **h**. But then **c** is the greatest number measuring **a** and **b** by hypothesis, and **h** is also posited to measure **a** and **b**. Therefore, by the corollary of the sixteenth [proposition] of this [book], **h** will measure **c**—the larger measuring the smaller—which is impossible. Therefore, no others will be given as the least in that proportion besides **d** and **e**, which was the goal; and in this the corollary is also made known.

## ¶ Any two numbers multiplying the least numbers of their proportion—the larger multiplying the smaller and the smaller the larger—produce the least number measured by them. From which it is clear that the least number which two numbers measure, measures any number measured by them. <margin>24</margin>

[[notes: This describes finding the Least Common Multiple (LCM). If you have numbers 6 and 8, their "least proportion" is 3:4. $6 \times 4 = 24$ and $8 \times 3 = 24$. Thus 24 is the LCM.]]

Therefore, by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the product of the extremes **a** and **d** is equal to the product of the means **b** and **c**, let it be **e**, which I say is the least number measured by them.
