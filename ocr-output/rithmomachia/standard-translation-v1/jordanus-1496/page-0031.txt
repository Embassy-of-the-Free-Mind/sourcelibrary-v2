### ¶ Smaller ratios added to smaller or equal ratios constitute a smaller ratio.

Let ratio **a** be smaller than **b**: and **c** smaller than **d**: and **e** smaller than **f**. I say that the ratio composed of **a** and **c** is smaller than the ratio composed of **b** and **c**, and the ratio from **e** is smaller than the ratio from **d** and **e**. Likewise, let ratio **g** be equal to ratio **f**. I say the ratio from **e** and **g** is smaller than the ratio from **f** and **g**. The first part is clear: for since the ratios **a** and **b** are unequal and **b** is greater than **a**, let **h** be the difference by which **b** exceeds **a**. Let the ratio from **b** added to **c** be **l**, and from **a** added to **c** be **k**. By the preceding [proposition], the same difference will exist between **l** and **k** as between **b** and **a**. But **b** is greater than **a** in ratio **h**; therefore, **l** is also greater than **k** in that same ratio. By the same reasoning, you will show the ratio composed of **d** and **e** to be greater than the ratio composed of **c** and **e**. Which is the first part. The second part is also clear: for if **f** is greater than **e**, let **m** be the difference of **f** to **e**. Let the compound of **f** and **g** be **n**, and of **e** and **g** be **o**. By the preceding, the difference of **f** to **e** is the same as **n** to **o**. But **f** is greater than **e** in ratio **m**; therefore, ratio **n** (composed of **f** and **g**) is greater than ratio **o** (composed of **e** and **g**) in that same ratio **m**. Which is the second part.

### ¶ To continue given ratios in their lowest terms.

¶ Let **a** to **b**, **c** to **d**, and **e** to **f** be the given ratios in their lowest terms: which we are commanded to assign as continuous proportions in the smallest numbers. I take the least [number] which **b** and **c** measure [[notes: This is the Least Common Multiple, or LCM]], which is **g**. And however many times **b** is in **g**, let **a** be in **h** that many times. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of **a** to **b** is then the same as **h** to **g**. And however many times **c** is in **g**, let **d** be in **k** that many times. By the same seventh [proposition], the ratio of **c** to **d** will be the same as **g** to **k**. Thus, there will be two ratios, **a** to **b** and **c** to **d**, in a continuous proportionality in the numbers **h**, **g**, and **k**, and these are in their lowest terms. For if they could exist in smaller numbers, let them be as in **t**, **v**, and **r**. By the nineteenth [proposition] of the third [book], **b** would measure **v**. Therefore, by the corollary of the twenty-fourth [proposition] of the third [book], **g** (the least number measured by them) would also measure **v**. Which shows they are continued in this way. And if **e** were to measure **l** some number of times, I would take **l** which **f** would also measure. Thus, by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of **e** to **f** is the same as **l** to **m**: and so a third ratio will be added in continuous proportionality and in lowest terms. This is in lowest terms for the reason just given. But because **b** does not measure **k**, I take the least number which **e** and **k** measure, which is **m**. And however many times **k** is in **m**, let **g** be in **n** and **h** be in **o** the same number of times. Likewise, however many times **e** is in **m**, let **f** be in **p** that many times. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], **o** will be to **n** as **h** is to **g**, which is as **a** is to **b**. And **n** to **m** is as **g** to **k**. And **m** to **p** is as **e** to **f**. Therefore, the three given ratios are in continuous proportionality in the numbers **o**, **n**, **m**, and **p**. These are also in their lowest terms. For if smaller ones were assigned, such as **q**, **r**, **s**, and **y**, by the nineteenth [proposition] of the second [book], **b** and **c** would measure **r**. Therefore, **g** (the smallest measured by them) also measures **r**. And since the ratio of **g** to **k** is the same as **r** to **s**, then by permutation, the ratio of **g** to **r** is the same as **k** to **s**. But since **g** measures **r**, then **k** measures **s**. And also by the nineteenth [proposition] of the third [book], **e** measures **s**. Therefore, by **m** (the smallest measured by them), it would measure **s**. Therefore, **q**, **r**, **s**, and **y** are not the smallest terms retaining those ratios continuously, as was assumed. Thus, no such terms will exist except **o**, **n**, **m**, and **p**. Which is what was proposed.

### ¶ To divide a proposed number into unequal parts according to assigned ratios taken continuously.

¶ The given number must be such that it can be divided by the number containing the assigned ratios in their lowest terms: otherwise, it would be impossible to find parts in it responding to the assigned ratios. Therefore, let the given number be **a**, which is proposed to be divided into four unequal parts: of which the first to the second is *dupla* [double, 2:1], the second to the third is *sesquialtera* [one-and-a-half, 3:2], and the third to the fourth is *sesquitertia* [one-and-a-third, 4:3]. By the preceding [proposition], continue the three assigned ratios (double, one-and-a-half, and one-and-a-third) in their lowest terms, which let be **b**, **c**, **d**, and **e**; and let the whole composed of them be **be**. Because **a** by hypothesis can be divided by **be**, let it be divided by **be** according to **f**; and **f** will be the number resulting from the division. I multiply **b**, **c**, **d**, and **e** by **f**, and let there result **g**, **h**, **l**, and **i**, which by the eighth [proposition] of the second [book] will contain the same ratios as **b**, **c**, **d**, and **e**, so that **g** is to **h** as **b** is to **c**, and **h** to **l** as **c** to **d**, and **l** to **i** as **d** to **e**. And since by the tenth [proposition] of the first [book] that which is produced by multiplying **be** by **f** is equal to that which is produced by multiplying **b**, **c**, **d**, and **e** by **f**. And by the sixth petition of the first [book], the product of **be** and **f** makes **a**, and **a** is returned. Therefore, **a** is divided into four unequal parts **g**, **h**, **l**, and **i**, containing the assigned ratios in continuous proportionality. Which is what was proposed.

[[notes: Example from the text: to divide the number 50 into parts with ratios 2:1, 3:2, and 4:3. 
1. Find the lowest terms: 12, 6, 4, 3 (12:6 is 2:1; 6:4 is 3:2; 4:3 is 4:3).
2. Sum them: 12 + 6 + 4 + 3 = 25.
3. Divide target number 50 by 25: 50 / 25 = 2.
4. Multiply parts by 2: 24, 12, 8, 6.
5. Result: 24+12+8+6 = 50.]]

### ¶ Any number of ratios continued in whatever order constitute the same [total] ratio.

¶ This must first be shown in two ratios. Let **a** and **b** be any two ratios, such as **a** being *dupla* [double] and **b** being *sesquialtera* [3:2]. Let the double be continued with the *sesquialtera* first in the numbers **c**, **d**, and **e**, so that the double ratio precedes and the *sesquialtera* follows. For **c** will be double to **d**, and **d** will be *sesquialtera* to **e**. Second, let the same ratios be continued in the numbers **f**, **g**, and **h**, so that the *sesquialtera* precedes and the double follows. For **f** will be *sesquialtera* to **g**, and **g** will be double to **h**. I say that **c** to **e** and **f** to **h** constitute the same ratio. For the ratio **g** to **h** is the same as **c** to **d**, and **f** to **g** is the same as **d** to **e**. Therefore, by the twenty-second [proposition] of the second [book] and by equal indirect proportionality: the ratio **f** to **h** is the same as **c** to **e**. Which is proposed and concluded for two ratios. In the case of three ratios, it is shown using the two that help...
