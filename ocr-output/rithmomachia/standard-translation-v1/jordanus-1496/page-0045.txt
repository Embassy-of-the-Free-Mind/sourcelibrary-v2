**f** is the mean between **a** and **b**. Since **a** adds unity over **c** (its difference from unity **b**), and **f** similarly adds unity over **c**, then **a** is composed of the portions **e** and **f**, where **f** (the middle number) is the greater portion and **e** the smaller. It is therefore established that between an odd number and unity there is one mean, and that this same mean is the greater portion [[notes: or "half" in a rounded-up sense]] of the odd number, which was the first point to be proved.

The second point follows similarly. For if between **a** and unity **b** there is a mean **f**, by definition the ratios of **a** to **f** and **b** to **f** are equal. But since, by perception, the difference of **a** to **b** is composed of the differences of **a** to **f** and **b** to **f**, and that difference can be divided into two equal parts, it must be even. However, the number **a** exceeds that difference by unity. Therefore, by definition, **a** is odd. This is the second point and the completion of the proposition.

[[margin: 24]] ¶ **Between an even number and unity, there will either be no mean, or there will be two means which are less than half of the even number.**
**Furthermore, if there are two means between a number and unity, that number must be even.**

¶ Let **a** be any even number and **b** be unity. If **a** is the first even number following unity [[notes: i.e., the number 2]], since they are separated by only a single unit, there will be no mean between **a** and unity. But if they are separated by more than a unit, I say first that there are two means between **a** and **b**, and they are smaller than half of the even number. Second, I say that if two means exist between any number **a** and unity **b**, then **a** must be even.

The first part is shown thus: for an even number **a**, the difference from unity **b** is odd. Because it is odd, it can be partitioned according to the ninth proposition of this book into two portions that differ by one unit. Let the difference be partitioned into two such portions. Then I take number **c**, whose smaller portion is its difference to **b**. By perception, the larger portion will be the difference of **a** to **c**. But the larger portion exceeds the smaller by one unit. I then take the number immediately following **c**, which is **d**. The difference of **a** to **d** will be one unit less than the difference of **a** to **c**. Thus, the difference of **a** to **d** and **c** to **b** are equal. Therefore, **c** and **d** are the means between **a** and **b**. And because, by the second proposition of the first book, **c** is the half-point of the sum of **d** and the difference of **c** to **b**, and **d** and the difference of **c** to **b** together equal **a**, it follows that **c** is the smaller of the means and less than half of the even number **a**. This is the first part.

The second part is declared thus: if between **a** and unity **b** there are two means, **c** and **d** (where **c** is the smaller and **d** the larger), the difference of **c** to **b** is one unit less than the difference of that same **c** to **a**. Therefore, one of these differences is even and the other is odd. When added together, according to the second proposition of this book, they produce an odd number, which by perception is the difference between **a** and **b**. If you add unity to this difference, you produce **a**. Therefore, **a** is even. This is the second point.

[[margin: 25]] ¶ **If all odd numbers are arranged starting from unity, and as many odds are taken after any given odd as its rank from unity: the last one reached will be a multiple of that first odd. Furthermore, the total will be a multiple of it according to its rank from the first; and all those left in the middle will be prime to it.**

¶ Let **a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, k, l, m, n, o, p, q, r, s** be odd numbers arranged in sequence from unity, and as many further as you wish. I say first that if you take **a**, which is the number three (the first odd after unity), and you take the third odd after it, which is **d** [[notes: 9]], **a** will divide it. And if you take the third after **d**, which is **g** [[notes: 15]], **a** will divide it. And if you take the third after **g**, which is **l** [[notes: 21]], **a** will divide it, and so on. Second, I say that all the intermediate numbers, such as **b, c** and **e, f** and **h, k**, are prime to **a** [[notes: "Prime" here means they share no common divisor other than 1]].

The first is clear: since the triple **a** is the first from unity, the three odds taken after it plus itself make four. Between the triple **a** and **d** (the third after it) are two intermediate odds, **b** and **c**, and three equal differences. For in a continuous series of odds, the difference is always two (the dyad). The total difference of **d** to **a** is composed of those three differences. Thus, **a** multiplied by one of those differences produces the total [[notes: 3 groups of 2 = 6; 3 + 6 = 9]]. Therefore **a** divides **d**. Similarly, it is shown that **a** divides **g**, for there are five intermediate odds and six equal differences of two, which is double the first three. Thus **a** will divide it by the twenty-third proposition of the first book. And it will divide **l** because the difference is triple, and **o** because the difference is quadruple, and so on.

The second part is also clear. First, **a** will not divide **b**, because if **a** were subtracted from **b**, by the twelfth proposition of the first book, **a** would have to divide the remaining difference, which is two. This is impossible [[notes: because 3 cannot divide 2]]. Nor will it divide **c** for the same reason. For if it were subtracted from **c**, it would have to divide the difference of **c** to **a**, which is two taken twice (four); if the difference is subtracted, it would have to divide the remainder, which is unity, and a number cannot divide unity. You can prove in the same way that **e, f**, and the rest are prime to **a**. If **a** divided **e**, I would subtract **e** from **g**; since **a** divides **g** and (by hypothesis) the subtracted number **e**, it must divide the difference, which is four. This was just shown to be impossible. By a similar argument, you can prove that **b** [[notes: 5]] divides **g, n, s** but the intermediates are prime to it, and **c** [[notes: 7]] divides **l** and **s**.

[[margin: 26]] ¶ **If odd numbers are added together starting from unity, the number produced will be a square.**

¶ Let there be unity and the sequential odd numbers **a, b, c, d, e, f**. I say that unity and **a** produce a square [[notes: 1+3=4]]. Also, unity, **a**, and **b** produce a square [[notes: 1+3+5=9]], and unity, **a, b**, and **c** [[notes: 1+3+5+7=16]], and so on for however many you aggregate. For if you divide **a** (the first odd) into two portions differing by one unit, it is certain that they are the numbers 1 and 2. When placed next to the first unity, there are two rows from unity reaching up to two, differing by one. Therefore, by the thirty-third proposition of the sixth book, unity and those two portions added together produce a square. But those two portions equal the next odd. Thus unity and the following odd **a** joined together produce a square. Likewise, if **a** is resolved into unity and two (since two is the difference between **b** and **a**), then **b** is resolved into two portions by the ninth proposition of this book, one of which is two and the other is one unit larger than two (which is three, equal to **a**). When these are placed in order with the first unity and the portions of **a**, there will be rows from unity arranged up to numbers differing by one unit...
