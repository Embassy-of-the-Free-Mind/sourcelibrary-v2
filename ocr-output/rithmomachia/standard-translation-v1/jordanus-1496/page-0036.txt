But you will perhaps grasp this last point more easily in smaller numbers found by the same method. Let **a** be six. And let the first numbers on either side be **b** and **c**. **b** being the smaller and **c** being the larger. It is certain that **b** is five, whose difference to **a** is **v**, which is certainly unity [[notes: "Unity" refers to the number 1]]. Let **e** be the difference of **v** to **b** (one to five), which is known to be four. Let **f** be the difference of **b** to **c**, which is two because it is double the difference of **b** to **a**. Thus **e** is double **f**. But **d** and **b** together are **a** by perception. And by the second [proposition] of the first [book], **b** and **c** together are double **a**. Therefore, **b** and **c** together are double **d** and **b**. Therefore, whatever the proportion of **e** to **f** (difference to difference), that same proportion is of the sum of **b** and **c** to the sum of **d** and **b**. Therefore, by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the product of **e** and the sum **d b** is equal to the product of **f** and the sum **b c**. Therefore, by the seventh [proposition] of this [book], the three squares of the numbers **d**, **b**, and **c** are such as were proposed to be found. Which is what was proposed.

### 13. To find a square number which, when added to a given square number, produces a square number.

**To find** a square number which, when added to a given square number, produces a square number. 13 [[notes: This is the classic problem of finding Pythagorean triples, $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$.]]
First, let the given square number be **a**, which is divisible into two equal parts. Let its half be **b**, and let its side [[notes: its square root]] be **c**. Let **d** be the number two (the binary). Since the product of **d** and **b** equals the product of **c** by itself, then by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], **c** is the mean proportional between two and **b**. And because **d** (two) is a prime number, and by the first [proposition] of the fourth [book] it communicates with **c**, and **c** with **b**, then **d** will also communicate with **b**. By the first [proposition] of the third [book], **d** will measure [[notes: divide evenly into]] **b**. Let **e** be the half of this **b**. Let the numbers placed around it be **f** (the larger) and **g** (the smaller), whose squares are **h** and **k**. I say that square **h** is the same as square number **l** added to the given square number **a**. For the difference of **f** and **g** is two, and their sum by the second [proposition] of the first [book] is **b**. And because from two multiplied by **b** comes **a** by the hypothesis, then by the seventh [proposition] of this [book], since **a** is formed from the difference of the sides multiplied by their sum, **a** is the difference between squares **h** and **k**. Therefore, the square **l** is found, which when added to the given square **a** produces the square number **h**. Which is what was proposed. 

But second, let the assigned square **a** be such that it cannot be divided into two equal parts [[notes: i.e., it is an odd square]]. Since two does not measure it, and two is a prime number, then by the first [proposition] of the third [book], two will be prime to it. Therefore, by the fifteenth [proposition] of the third [book], two will measure the number one less than square **a**, which let be **b**. Let its half be **c**, and let the number one greater than it be **d**. Let the square of **d** be **e** and the square of **c** be **f**. I say, therefore, that square **e** exceeds the given square **a** by exactly the amount of **f** (the square of **c**). For since **c** taken twice with unity [[notes: $2c + 1$]] produces **a**, then by the eighteenth [proposition] of the first [book], the square of **c** plus the product of **a** and the difference of the larger part **d** to **c** is equal to the square of the larger part **d**. Therefore, the square of **d** exceeds the square of **c** by the given square **a**, for the difference of **d** and **c** was unity. Which is what was proposed.

### 14. If a square is multiplied by a square, the product will be a square. If, however, it is multiplied by a non-square, a non-square will result. From which it is clear that if a square divides a square, it divides it according to a square.

First, let **a** and **b** be two squares, and let **a** be multiplied by **b** to produce **c**. I say that **c** is a square. For by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book], there is one mean proportional between **a** and **b**. Let that be **d**. Since **a**, **d**, and **b** are continuously proportional, then by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], the product of **a** and **b** is equal to the product of **d** by itself. But what is produced from **a** times **b** is **c**. Therefore, **c** is a square. Which is intended. 

Second, let **a** be a square and **b** a non-square, and let **a** be multiplied by **b** to produce **c**. I say **c** is not a square. For if **c** were a square, by the second part of the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], the side of square **c** would be a mean proportional between **a** and **b**. Wherefore, since **a** is a prime square by the tenth [proposition] of this [book], **b** would also be a square. Which is against the hypothesis. The corollary follows sufficiently from these two parts.

### 15. A square divides a non-square according to a non-square.

For if it were possible for a square to divide it according to a square, then by the first part of the preceding [proposition], the product [[notes: the original non-square]] would be a square. But a square is stated to divide a non-square; therefore, this would follow in contradiction to the hypothesis. Which is what was proposed.

### 16. If a cube is multiplied by a cube, that which is produced will be a cube.

<margin>
      c —— 216
   a —— 8  g —— 108
   e —— 4  f —— 54
   d —— 2  b —— 27
   I       a —— 8
</margin>

**Let** **a** and **b** be two cubes, and let **a** be multiplied by **b** to produce **c**. I say **c** is a cube. For I take the side of cube **a**, which is **d**, and the square of the side **d**, which is **e**. It is manifest that **d** and **e** are proportional between unity and **a**. And because as many times as unity is in **a**, so many times is **b** in **c**, since **a** multiplies **b** to produce **c** [[notes: Jordanus uses "a multiplies c according to b," meaning $a \times b = c$]]. By the fourteenth [proposition] of the fourth [book], however many mean proportionals there are between unity and **a**, just as many will there be between **b** and **c**. There are therefore likewise two mean proportionals between **b** and **c**, let them be **f** and **g**. Since **b**, **f**, **g**, and **c** are four proportional numbers, and **b** (the first) is by hypothesis a cube, then by the eleventh [proposition] of this [book], **c** (the fourth) will also be a cube. Which is what was proposed.

### 17. If a cube is multiplied by a non-cube, a non-cube will be produced. From which it is clear that if a cube divides a cube, it divides it according to a cube.

   e —— 4    b —— 20
   d —— 2

**Let** cube **a** be multiplied by number **b**, a non-cube, to produce **c**. I say **c** is not a cube. For if the product **c** were assumed to be a cube, it is manifest as in the preceding by the fourteenth [proposition] of the fourth [book] that however many mean proportionals there are between unity and **a**, just as many are between **b** and **c**. Let there be two, namely **f** and **g**. Because **c**, **g**, **f**, and **b** are continuously proportional, and **c** is assumed to be a cube, then by the eleventh [proposition] of this [book], **b** would also be a cube. But **b** was assumed by the hypothesis to be a non-cube; thus a contradiction to the hypothesis occurs. Therefore, that which is produced from them will not be a cube. Which is what was proposed. The corollary is sufficiently known from this and the preceding.
