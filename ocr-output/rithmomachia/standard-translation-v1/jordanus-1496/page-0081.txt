[[meta: This page shifts from the study of geometric medieties to the "harmonic mediety," which modern readers know as the harmonic progression. This was essential for Renaissance music theory, as it describes the relationships between musical intervals. The author continues to use Euclidean-style proofs to demonstrate the properties of these sequences, specifically focusing on the relationships between the middle term (the mean) and the extremes.]]

$\P$ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers established in a harmonic progression; let **a** be to **b** in a greater ratio than **b** is to **c**. For let **d** be the difference between **a** and **b**, and **e** the difference between **b** and **c**. By definition, **d** is to **e** as **a** is to **c**. And by the ninth [proposition] of the second [book], **a** to **c** is a greater ratio than **a** to **b**. Wherefore **a** to **b** is a lesser ratio than **d** to **e**—that is, the ratio of the whole to the whole is less than the ratio of the part taken away to the part taken away. Therefore, by the fourteenth [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of the remainder **b** to the remainder **c** is less than the ratio of the whole **a** to the whole **b**. Which is what was proposed. [[notes: The author is proving a specific technical relationship between the ratios of the terms and their differences in a harmonic sequence, such as 15, 12, 10.]]

35 $\P$ **In the same progression, the two extremes joined together produce a value greater than the middle term doubled.**
$\P$ For by the second [proposition] of the second [book], the difference of the greater terms is larger than the difference of the smaller terms. Therefore, if a number is taken smaller than the greatest extreme which exceeds the middle as much as the middle exceeds the smallest [extreme]: by the second [proposition] of the first [book], that number and the smallest will equal the middle doubled. Therefore, by common knowledge, the greatest of the extremes (which is a larger number than that one) combined with the same smallest extreme will constitute a number greater than the middle doubled. Which is what was proposed. [[notes: If we take the harmonic trio 15, 12, 10, the sum of the extremes is 25 (15+10), which is greater than the middle term doubled, 24 (12x2).]]

36 $\P$ **In the same progression, the product of the extremes is equal to the square of the middle term plus the product of the differences.**
$\P$ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in this progression as before, and their differences be **d** and **e**. I say that the product of **c** and **a** is equal to the square of **b** added to the product of **d** and **e**. For the product of **c** and **a** is equal to the product of **e** and **b** and [e and] **d** by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book]. And by the twenty-fifth of the second [book], the product of **c** and **d** is equal to the product of **e** and **a**. And the product of **e** and **a** is as much as **e** times **b** and **d**. And since the product of **e** and **c** and **b** by the tenth of the first [book] is equal to the square of **b**. Therefore, the product of **c** and **a** is equal to **b** squared and **e** times **d**. Which is what is proposed. [[notes: Using 15, 12, 10: 15 * 10 = 150. The square of 12 is 144. The differences are 3 (15-12) and 2 (12-10). 3 * 2 = 6. 144 + 6 = 150.]]

[[margin: [[image-desc: A simple line diagram with three horizontal lines labeled a (15), b (12), and c (10). A point e is marked below line c.]] ]]

37 $\P$ **In the same progression, that which is produced by the middle multiplied by the extremes is double the product of those same extremes.**
$\P$ For the product of **b** and **a** [plus b and c] is equal to the product of **c** and **a** and **e** and **a** by the tenth [proposition] of the first [book]. And because the product of **e** and **a** is as much as **d** and **c** by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], and by the tenth of the first [book] again, the product of **d** and **c** with the product of **b** and **c** is as much as **a** times **c**. Therefore, it is equal to the product of **c** and **a**. Thus, what is asserted is made known. [[notes: This refers to the property $b(a+c) = 2ac$. For 15, 12, 10: 12 * (15+10) = 12 * 25 = 300. This is double the product of the extremes: 2 * (15 * 10) = 300.]]

38 $\P$ **Between unity and any other number, or between numbers that are the smallest in their ratio, it is not possible to assign a middle term according to the harmonic progression.**
$\P$ Between a number and unity [one], a middle cannot be assigned. For since the ratio of the given number to one would be the same as the ratio of the difference between the given number and the middle to the difference between the middle and one. And since the difference of the given number to the middle would be less than the given number, it would be necessary for the harmonic mean of the given number and one to have a difference from one that is less than one. Furthermore, regarding numbers that are the smallest in their ratio, the same is immediately evident. For since the ratio of the differences would be the same as the ratio of those numbers to each other, and their differences are smaller. Therefore, those numbers would not be the smallest in their ratio. Which is against the hypothesis. [[notes: "Smallest in their ratio" refers to numbers in their lowest terms, like 3 and 2. The author is arguing that you cannot always find a harmonic mean that is a whole number (an integer).]]

39 $\P$ **To find a musical [harmonic] progression according to a given ratio.**
$\P$ Let **a** and **b** be the extremes of the given ratio, let **c** be their difference, and let **d** be the sum of them. Let **a** and **b** be multiplied by **d**, and let **e** and **f** result. Let their difference be **g**, which will be divided according to **c** by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book]. And because **d** is the same as **a** and **b**, therefore **c** multiplied by **a** and by **b** makes **g**. And from that which results from **c** multiplied by **a**, which let be **h**: when subtracted from **g**, let **k** remain, which **b** multiplied by **c** will produce. Therefore, let a middle **l** be taken between **e** and **f** whose difference to **e** is **h**; it is certain that its difference to **f** is **k**. And because by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book] **h** is to **k** as **a** is to **b**, and by the eighth of the same [book] also **e** is to **f** as **a** is to **b**, it is established by definition that the proposal has been achieved.

40 $\P$ **Having proposed the extreme terms: to find the middle between them according to the harmonic progression.**
$\P$ Let **a** and **b** be the extremes of a harmonic progression for which you wish to find the middle. I take their difference, which is **c**, and their sum, which is **d**. Then I multiply **a** by **c** and let **e** result, which is divided by **b**, and the result of the division is **f**. By the converse of the twenty-fifth of the second [book], the ratio of **d** to **a** is the same as **c** to **f**. Therefore, when **f** is subtracted from **c**, let **g** remain. The ratio of **f** to **g** will be as **a** to **b**. This I show: for as **d** is to **a**, so is **c** to **f**. Therefore, by permutation, **d** is to **c** (the whole to the whole) as **a** is to **f** (the part taken away to the part taken away). Wherefore also as **a** is to **f**, so is **b** to **g** (the remainder to the remainder). Therefore, by permutation, **a** is to **b** as **f** is to **g**. I therefore take a middle number between **a** and **b** whose difference from **a** is **f**. And because its difference from **b** is left as **g**, it is established by definition that the proposal is achieved.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A diagram showing four horizontal lines of varying lengths labeled a=24, c=18, d=16, b=12.]] ]]

41 $\P$ **If two middle terms are established between two numbers, one according to the arithmetic and the other according to the harmonic progression: the one according to the arithmetic is greater, and the ratio of the greatest to it is the same as the ratio of the other [the harmonic] to the minimum.**
$\P$ Let the extremes be **a** and **b**. Let the middle according to the arithmetic progression be **c**, and according to the harmonic be **d**. I say that **c** is greater than **d**, and the ratio of **a** to **c** is the same as **d** to **b**. That **c** is greater than **d** is immediately clear because, by definition, the ratio of the difference of **a** to **d** to the difference of **d** to **b** is as **a** to **b**. But the difference of **a** to **c** and **c** to **b** are equal; therefore the difference of **a** to **d** will be greater than **a** to **c**. And that the ratio of **a** to **c** is as **d** to **b** is clear. For by the fifth [proposition] of this [book], **c** is the half of **a** and **b** joined together, and by the thirty-seventh [proposition] of this [book], that which is produced from **d** multiplied by **a** and **b** is double the product of **a** and **b**. Therefore, that which results from **d** multiplied by **c** (which is half of **a** and **b** by the second [proposition] of the first [book]) will be equal to the product of **a** and **b**. Therefore, by the twenty-fifth of the second [book], **a**, **c**, **d**, **b** are proportional as stated. Which is what was proposed. [[notes: This identifies the famous relationship between the three means: Arithmetic Mean ($c$) times Harmonic Mean ($d$) equals the product of the extremes ($a \times b$). This also implies that the Geometric Mean lies between them.]]

42 $\P$ **If middle terms are established according to the harmonic progression between proportional numbers: the ratio of the extremes to those [middles] will be the same.**
$\P$ Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be as **c** to **d**. Let **e** be the harmonic middle between **a** and **b**, and let **f** be the middle between **c** and **d**. I say **a** is to **e** as **c** is to **f**. For let the differences be **g**, **h** and **k**, **l**. Because when the ratio of **a** to **b** is the same as **c** to **d**, and when **a** to **b** is as **g** to **h**, and **c** to **d** is as **k** to **l**, for that reason **g** will be to **h** as **k** is to **l**. Therefore, by permutation, **g** is to **k** as **h** is to **l**. Wherefore by the twenty-fifth of the second [book], **a**, **c**, **e**, **f** are proportional... as stated. Which is what was proposed.
