### ¶ When there are two composite numbers, the ratio of one to the other is composed of the ratios of their sides to the sides of the other.

¶ The "sides" [[notes: In medieval mathematics, *latera* (sides) refers to the factors of a number. For example, if 3 and 4 are multiplied to make 12, 3 and 4 are the "sides" of the number 12.]] of a proposed number are said to be the numbers which, multiplied into each other, produce the proposed number. Therefore, let there be two proposed numbers **a** and **b**. Let the sides of **a** be **c** and **d**, of which **c** is the larger and **d** the smaller. And let the sides of **b** be **e** and **f**, of which **e** is the larger and **f** the smaller. I say that the ratio of **a** to **b** is composed of the ratios of **c** to **e** and **d** to **f**. For I multiply **c** (the larger side of **a**) by **f** (the smaller side of **b**), and let **g** be produced. Because **c** times **d** makes **a**, and **c** times **f** makes **g**, therefore, by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of **d** to **f** is the same as **a** to **g**. and because **f** times **c** makes **g** and **f** times **e** makes **b**, therefore, by that same seventh [proposition], the ratio of **c** to **e** is the same as **g** to **b**. But the ratio of **a** to **b** is constituted from the ratios of **a** to **g** and **g** to **b**; therefore it is also from the ratios of **c** to **e** and **d** to **f**. This is what was proposed.

### ¶ If numbers in continuous proportion are multiplied by just as many other numbers also in continuous proportion, those which are produced therefrom will also be in continuous proportion.

¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be numbers in continuous proportion [[notes: A "continuous proportion" is a sequence like 2, 4, 8, where the ratio between each pair is the same (1:2).]], and likewise **d**, **e**, and **f** be just as many others in continuous proportion. Let **d** be multiplied by **a** to produce **g**; let **e** be multiplied by **b** to produce **h**; and let **f** be multiplied by **c** to produce **l**. I say the products **g**, **h**, and **l** are in continuous proportion. For since the sides of **g** are **d** and **a**, and the sides of **h** are **e** and **b**, and the sides of **l** are **f** and **c**: therefore, by the preceding [proposition], the ratio of **g** to **h** consists of the ratios of **d** to **e** and **a** to **b**. And the ratio of **h** to **l** [consists] of the ratios of **e** to **f** and **b** to **c**. But the ratios of **a** to **b** and **b** to **c** are the same as the ratios of **d** to **e** and **e** to **f**. Therefore, the ratio of **h** to **l** consisting of those is the same as the ratio of **g** to **h**. Therefore, the ratio of **g** to **h** is the same as **h** to **l**, which was to be demonstrated and proposed.

### ¶ To divide a number such that the ratio of the whole to the larger part is the same as the ratio of the larger to the smaller part is impossible.

¶ If any number were divided in this manner, it would be said to be divided according to the ratio having a mean and extremes [[notes: This is the classic definition of the "Golden Ratio." The author is proving that the Golden Ratio is irrational—it cannot be expressed using whole numbers (integers).]]. This [proposition] therefore proposes that no [whole] number can be divided according to the ratio having a mean and extremes. For if any [number] can be divided in this way, let it be **ab**, and let it be divided into **ad** (the smaller part) and **db** (the larger part). Let it be that as **ab** is to **db**, so **db** is to **ad**. I take away from the larger part **db** a number **cd** equal to **ad**. And because the ratio of **ab** to **db** is the same as **db** to **ad**, and because **ab** and **db** are equal [in ratio to the parts], for the ratio of the whole **ab** to the whole **db** is the same as the ratio of the part **db** to the part **cd** which was taken away. Therefore, by the fifth [proposition] of the second [book], as the ratio of **ab** is to **db**, so the ratio of **ad** is to **cb** (the remainder to the remainder). Wherefore, the same [ratio] of **db** is to **cb**. Wherefore, again, as the ratio of **db** is to **dc**, so **dc** is to **cb**. I take again **de** equal to **cb**. And because as **db** is to **dc**, so **dc** is to **cb**, and **de** equals **cb**. Therefore, just as **db** is to **dc** (whole to whole), so is **dc** to **de** (the part taken away to the part taken away). Wherefore, by the fifth [proposition] of the second [book], just as **db** is to **dc** (whole to whole), so is **cb** to **e** (the remainder to the remainder). Wherefore, again, by a similar argument, after taking away **ec** from **cb**, the remainder will be to the remainder just as the whole was to the whole. And this will go on into infinity [[notes: This is the "method of infinite descent." Since you cannot keep subtracting whole numbers forever and always have a smaller whole number remaining, the original assumption that these were whole numbers must be false.]]. Because this is impossible by the fourth petition [[notes: An axiom stating that numbers cannot be infinitely small.]], it is certain that a [whole] number cannot be so divided.

### ¶ To find a number consisting of two parts whose ratio to the larger part adds to the ratio of those same parts by less than a given ratio.

¶ Let **a** and **b** be the least terms of a given ratio, **a** being the larger and **b** the smaller. Let the difference of **a** to **b** be the number **c**. Let the ratio of **a** to **b** be larger than **b** to **c**. Let **d** be a number constituted from two, namely **a** and **b**, which I say has a smaller ratio to **a** (its larger part) than the assigned ratio of **a** to **b**. Let **e** be composed of **b** and **c**. For since the ratio of **a** to **b** (the first to the second) is greater than **b** to **c** (the third to the fourth), therefore, by the first part of the fifteenth [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of **d** to **b** is greater than **e** to **c**. And by the second part of the same, the ratio of **d** to **a** will be smaller than **e** to **b**. And since **e** is constituted from **b** and **c** (the difference of **a** and **b**), it will be equal to **a** itself. Therefore, the ratio of **d** to **a** is smaller than **a** to **b**, which is what was proposed. Secondly, let the ratio of **a** to **b** be smaller than **b** to **c**. I take any number larger than **a**, which is **d**, from which I take away **f** equal to **c**, and let the remainder be **e**. And I understand **c** taken away from **a**, and **b** remains. And because the ratio of **d** to **a** (whole to whole) is greater than **f** to **c** (the part taken away to the part taken away), therefore, by the thirteenth [proposition] of the second [book], the ratio of **e** to **b** (remainder to remainder) is greater than **d** to **a** (whole to whole). And further, the ratio of **e** to **b** is greater than **d** to **a**. Therefore, by the twelfth [proposition] of the same, the ratio of **e** to **d** is greater than **b** to **a**. Wherefore, by the eleventh [proposition] of the same, the ratio of **d** to **e** will be smaller than **a** to **b**. And since **d** is constituted [of parts], and **e** is larger than **f**, if both **e** and **f** are numbers, it is established that **d** is the number constituted of two, **e** and **f**, which we seek. But if **f** is a unit [[notes: The number 1]], I multiply some number, like **g**, into **e** and **f**. Let **h** be produced [from **e**], and into **d** let **k** be produced, and into **f** let **l** be produced. By the seventh [proposition] of the second [book], as the ratio of **e** to **d** and **d** to **f** is, so is **h** to **k** and **k** to **l**. And since by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book] that which is made from **g** into **e** and into **f** is equal to that which is made from **g** into **d**, therefore **k** is constituted from the two numbers **h** and **l**. Wherefore **k** is such a number as is sought.
