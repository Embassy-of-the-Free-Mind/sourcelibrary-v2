# VI

41 ¶ **If two plane numbers are similar: the ratio of one to the other will be as a square to a square.**
[[notes: "Plane numbers" (superficiales) are numbers that can be represented as the product of two factors, like an area. "Similar" plane numbers are those whose sides (factors) are proportional—for example, $2 \times 3$ (6) and $4 \times 6$ (24) are similar because $2/4 = 3/6$.]]
<image-desc>A diagram showing two sets of three lines representing values in proportion. Top set: a (24), b (36), c (54). These represent a continued proportion where 24:36 is the same as 36:54. Bottom set: d (4), e (6), f (9). Lines connect the terms to show their mathematical relationship.</image-desc>
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two similar plane numbers: I say the ratio of **a** to **b** is as that of a square to a square. For by the preceding proposition, there will be one mean proportional between them, which let be **c**. I therefore take the three smallest numbers in their proportion, which are **d**, **e**, and **f**, according to the first [proposition] of this [book] [[notes: This refers to finding the lowest terms of a continued proportion]]. Since **d** and **f** are squares, and since the ratio of **d** to **e** is as **a** to **c**, and the ratio of **e** to **f** is as **c** to **b**, then by equal proportionality, the ratio of **d** to **f** (square to square) is as **a** to **b**: which is what was proposed.

42 ¶ **If the ratio of two numbers is as that of a square to a square, those numbers must necessarily be similar plane numbers.**
<image-desc>A diagram with four lines: a and b are grouped together as squares, while c and d represent the numbers being compared.</image-desc>
¶ This is the converse of the preceding. Let **a** and **b** be squares, and let the ratio of **c** to **d** be as **a** to **b**: I say that the numbers **c** and **d** are similar plane numbers. For by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of this [book], there is one mean proportional between [the squares] **a** and **b**. Therefore, by the twelfth [proposition] of the fourth [book], there will also be one mean proportional between **c** and **d**. Thus, by the 40th [proposition] of this [book], **c** and **d** are similar plane numbers: which is what was proposed.

43 ¶ **If one of two similar plane numbers is multiplied by the other: a square number will be produced.**
<image-desc>A diagram with three lines a (24), b (36), c (54), and a fourth longer line below labeled d with the number 1296 (which is $36 \times 36$ or $24 \times 54$).</image-desc>
¶ Let **a** and **b** be similar plane numbers: and let **a** be multiplied by **b** to produce **c**: I say that **c** is a square. For by the thirty-ninth [proposition] of this [book], there is one mean proportional between **a** and **b**, which let be **d**. Because **a**, **d**, and **b** are in continuous proportion, by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], the product of **a** times **b** is the same as **d** times itself [[notes: In a geometric progression $a, d, b$, the property $a \times b = d^2$ always holds]]. But what is produced by **d** times itself is a square; therefore **c**, which is produced from **a** times **b**, is also a square: which is what was proposed.

44 ¶ **If the multiplication of two numbers produces a square: those two numbers must be similar plane numbers.**
<image-desc>A diagram with lines a, b, c, d, e. a, b, c are horizontal; d and e are shorter lines below serving as reference units.</image-desc>
¶ This converts the preceding. For example, if from **a** times **b** there is produced a square **c**: I say that **a** and **b** are similar plane numbers. For by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], the square root [[gloss: latus tetragonicum]] of **c** will be the mean proportional between **a** and **b**. Therefore, by the fortieth [proposition] of this [book], **a** and **b** are similar plane numbers: which is the intended result.

45 ¶ **If two plane numbers are each similar to a third: they will also be similar to each other.**
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two plane numbers, each similar to **c**: I say that **a** and **b** are similar to each other. For because **a** is similar to **c**, there is one mean proportional between **a** and **c**, which let be **d**. And because **b** is also similar to **c**, there is likewise one mean proportional between **b** and **c**, which let be **e**. Therefore, by the sixteenth [proposition] of the fourth [book], there will also be one mean proportional between **a** and **b**; wherefore, by the fortieth [proposition] of this [book], **a** and **b** will be similar plane numbers: and this is the proposal.

46 ¶ **If any number is multiplied by a plane number: the result will be a solid.**
[[notes: A "solid" number is what we now call a cubic number or the product of three factors ($x \times y \times z$), representing volume.]]
¶ For a plane number is made from the multiplication of one number by another. Therefore, that which is produced from the multiplication of [a third] number by that plane number is contained by three sides through continuous multiplication. Wherefore, by definition, it will be a solid: which was the intention.

47 ¶ **Every solid is measured by some plane number.**
¶ For every solid, by definition, has three sides, the third of which is multiplied by the product of the other two. But such a product is a plane number. Therefore, every solid is measured [[notes: "Measured" here means "divided evenly by" or "composed of"]] by some plane number, as was proposed.

48 ¶ **Given three numbers, though a solid may be created in a three-fold way: the same single result will be produced from all of them.**
[[notes: This is the Commutative Property of multiplication for three factors ($a \times b \times c = b \times c \times a = a \times c \times b$).]]
<image-desc>A numerical diagram showing: Top row 24 (the final solid); Second row 12 and 8 (intermediate plane numbers) pointing to 24; Third row 6, 12, 8; Bottom row 2, 3, 4 with labels a, b, c. Lines connect the values showing the steps of multiplication.</image-desc>
¶ Let the three sides be **a**, **b**, and **c**: and first, let **d** be made from **a** times **b**, and from **c** times **d** let the solid **e** be made. Second, let **f** be made from **b** times **c**, and from **a** times **f** let the solid **g** be made. Third, let **h** be made from **a** times **c**, and from **b** times **h** let the solid **k** be made. I say the three solids **e**, **g**, and **k**, created in a three-fold way from the three sides, are the same and equal. For since **a** times **b** makes **d**, and **c** times **b** makes **f**, therefore by the eighth [proposition] of the second [book], **d** is to **f** as **a** is to **c**. Thus, by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the result of **a** times **f** is the same as **c** times **d**. But **a** times **f** makes **g**, and **c** times **d** makes **e**; therefore the solids **g** and **e** are equal. Likewise, because **b** times **c** makes **f** and **a** times **c** makes **h**, by the same eighth [proposition], the ratio of **b** to **a** is as **f** to **h**. Therefore, by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the second [book], the result of **a** times **f** is the same as **b** times **h**. But from **a** times **f** comes the solid **g**, and from **b** times **h** comes the solid **k**. Therefore **g** and **k** are equal to each other. Since solid **e** was also equal to solid **g**, then by common knowledge [[notes: "Common notions," such as things equal to the same thing are equal to each other]], **k** and **e** are equal solids. Thus, as proposed, the three solids **e**, **g**, and **k** are equal to one another.

49 ¶ **Every number measured by three numbers is proved to be a solid.**
<image-desc>A simple diagram with three horizontal lines labeled a, b, c, representing the factors.</image-desc>
¶ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers measuring **d**: I say that **d** is a solid number. For either **a** is prime or composite. If it is composite, then by the forty-sixth [proposition] of this [book], whatever number is multiplied by it will produce a solid; thus the proposition will be established. If it is prime, since **b** measures **d** according to some number, then by the fourth [proposition] of the third [book], **a** measures **b** or that other number by which it measures. Therefore, either **b** or that other number is a plane number. It will therefore be a solid by the forty-sixth [proposition], as before.

50 ¶ **The ratio of any two solid numbers: is the ratio produced from the ratios of one set of sides to the other.**
[[notes: This means the ratio of two volumes is the compound ratio of their length, width, and height ratios.]]
<image-desc>A tabular diagram with two columns. Left: a-24, k-12, c-4, d-3, e-2. Right: b-8, l-8, f-4, g-2, h-1. These represent the dimensions and volumes of two solids.</image-desc>
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two solids: and let **c**, **d**, and **e** be the sides of **a**, and **f**, **g**, and **h** the sides of **b**. I say that the ratio of **a** to **b** is composed of the ratios of **c** to **f**, **d** to **g**, and **e** to **h**. For I multiply **c** by **d** to get **k**, and **f** by **g** to get **l**. By the ninth [proposition] of the fifth [book], the ratio of **k** to **l** is composed of the ratios of **c** to **f** and **d** to **g**. And since by the forty-eighth [proposition] of this [book], if **e** is multiplied by **k** and **h** by **l**, **a** and **b** are produced. Therefore, by the ninth [proposition] of the fifth [book], the ratio of **a** to **b** is as **k** to **l** and **e** to **h**. But the ratio of **k** to **l** was shown to be composed of **c** to **f** and **d** to **g**. Therefore, the ratio of **a** to **b** is composed of the ratios of **c** to **f**, **d** to **g**, and **e** to **h**: which is what was proposed.

51 ¶ **If two similar solids measure two others equally: it is necessary that those other solids be similar as well.**
<image-desc>A complex diagram with letters and numbers representing ratios and products: d-16, e-128, c-8, b-64, p-2, q-4, n-8, o-32, f-4, k-8, g-2, l-4, h-1, m-2.</image-desc>
¶ Let **a** and **b** be two similar solids, and let them measure **d** and **e** equally, as according to **c**. I say that **d** and **e** are likewise similar solids. For I assign **f**, **g**, and **h** as the sides of **a**, and **k**, **l**, and **m** as the sides of **b**. I multiply **f** by **g** to get **n**, and **k** by **l** to get **o**. Since **h** times **n** makes **a** by the forty-eighth [proposition], and **m** times **o** makes **b**, it follows that **n** is in **a** according to **h**, and **a** is in **d** according to **c**. Therefore, by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], **n** is in **d** according to the product of **c** times **h**, which let be **p**. Similarly, because **o** is in **b** according to **m**, and **b** is in **e** according to **c**, by the same twenty-third [proposition], **o** is in **e** according to the product of **c** times **m**, which let be **q**. Since **c** times **h** makes **p** and **c** times **m** makes **q**, the ratio of **h** to **m** is as **p** to **q** by the seventh [proposition] of the second [book]. Thus, the ratio is also as **f** to **k** and **g** to **l**. But **p** times **n** makes **d**, and **q** times **o** makes **e**, as was shown. Since **f** times **g** is **n**, and **k** times **l** is **o**, then **f**, **g**, and **p** are the sides of **d**, and **k**, **l**, and **q** are the sides of **e**. Because their sides are proportional, the solids are similar.
