## To demonstrate the relationships of each of the enharmonic modes to one another.

¶ The demonstration of this can be made clear from the preceding section. First, the Hypophrygian is a tone’s distance from the Hypodorian; the Hypolydian is a tone and a diesis [[notes: In the enharmonic genus, a *diesis* is a quarter-tone, the smallest interval]]; the Dorian is a minor third [[notes: *sesquitonum*, literally "one and a half tones"]]; the Phrygian is a fifth [[notes: *diapente*]]; the Lydian is a fifth and a diesis; the Mixolydian is a fifth and a semitone. Second, the Hypolydian is distant from the Hypophrygian by a diesis; the Dorian by a semitone; the Phrygian by a fourth [[notes: *diatessaron*]]; the Lydian by a fourth and a diesis; the Mixolydian by a fourth and a semitone. Third, the Dorian is a diesis from the Hypolydian; the Phrygian is two tones and a diesis; the Lydian is a fourth; the Mixolydian is a fourth and a diesis. Fourth, the Phrygian is two tones [[notes: *ditono*, a major third]] from the Dorian; the Lydian is two tones and a diesis; the Mixolydian is a fourth. Fifth, the Lydian is a diesis from the Phrygian, and the Mixolydian is a semitone. Sixth, the Mixolydian is distant from the Lydian by a diesis.

¶ For the authority of the ancient Pythagoreans added seven modes and no more. For just as a number grows and progresses in variety from the monad [[notes: the number one]] all the way to ten, but soon the following ten takes the place of the unit and unfolds that first unity—being the partner and rival of that same indivisible monad—extending itself again by a nine-fold progression until it reaches a hundred, finally falling back into a third unity: so too does the succession and variety of voices (from which the human concert and its modes are formed after the pattern of celestial harmony) rise up to the number eight. And there are seven continuous voices, varying among themselves, which are succeeded by the eighth fullness of voice [[notes: the octave]]. (For eight is the first numerical cube and the first solid square.) This eighth voice sounds again like the first, standing in relation to it just as ten does to the unit. And this eight-fold series proceeds so fixedly in every kind of melody that at every eighth place, one continuously strikes an eighth sound similar to it, and almost the very same sound. It is so much so that from the two, by a certain native and concordant affinity, one sound now seems to be produced rather than many; to such an extent do they mix and join themselves together in mutual friendship.

And of these seven voices, the one which is lowest and slowest is owed to **Saturn**. The first [[notes: counting up]] to **Jupiter**, the third to **Mars**, the fourth to **Phoebus** [[notes: the Sun]], the fifth to **Venus**, the sixth to **Mercury**, and the seventh—the highest and most rapid of them—to the **Moon**. The eighth either revolves back to Saturn, the ninth to Jupiter, the tenth to Mars, and in this manner those following, as wise antiquity intended. And indeed, the harmony of the whole universe is completed by the number seven; and these seven temper the celestial harmony in the heavens, and in these lower regions, the corporeal and sensible harmony.

But let these things be discussed more fully elsewhere. From this, one may understand why the "Hypermyxolydian" is hardly to be accommodated among the seven Pythagorean modes. For if in the diatonic genus the Mixolydian fifteen-string scale were sharpened by one tone more into the Hypermyxolydian, that whole mode would resonate everywhere as an octave to the Hypodorian; an eighth sound would have been born which is rendered the same as and kindred to the first by natural affinity. Wherefore it is not to be thought of as varying in every way from the first mode, the Hypodorian. And the same would happen if the Mixolydian were tightened by three semitones in the chromatic genus, or by two tones in the enharmonic; but perhaps enough has been said of these things for the present business.

The modes which the more recent authors added, such as the divine **Gregory** [[notes: Pope Gregory the Great, to whom Gregorian Chant is traditionally attributed]], depart from this antiquity of the modes, as do many other things which later musicians have drummed in and which you might easily reject. Even in our own time, musical melody and all its harmony strive to be reduced to a certain speed and headlong levity, paying very little heed to the modest, grave, full, and decorous moderation of concerts. For "modes" are so named from "moderation." Likewise, they pay little heed to the ancient honor and gravity of music, by which it used to release the insane and the possessed. It induced health, and—as once the Thracian **Orpheus** did—recalled the fierce manners of men to the gentle path of virtue. From the harmony of sensible things, it called forth captive souls—as if they were mindful of their own exile—to a longing for celestial harmony, with eyes flowing with tears.

For the disciples of Pythagoras did this by the suitable "middle-way" [[notes: *mediocritate*]] of the modes. For a man is not more modest if his every walk seems like a run, nor is he whose headlong speech mocks the understanding of those present. So too, those modes are not more modest which, by their excessive haste, escape the hearing, like those acting wantonly in a dance of Venus. For this reason, the Pythagorean school rejected the "soft" chromatic modes, and the Spartans, by a solemn decree, vehemently rebuked **Timotheus of Miletus** [[notes: A Greek musician criticized for adding strings to the lyre and making music too complex/effeminate]], approving instead diatonic concerts. If these are aptly moderated, they display a modest virtue. For just as we are filled with an empty loathing by excessive slowness, as if by a certain lethargy, so too does excessive speed display a certain dishonest softness. The middle path—neither headlong with speed nor lazy with idle slowness—is praiseworthy and a rival of virtue, which every age has approved and will approve. To this path, musical modes (as if certain fixed rules for the moderation of the soul) ought to lead us, and continually snatch our minds toward divine things. Happy will they be who seek both music and all worldly philosophy for this end; nor is celestial favor and protection usually lacking for such people. But those who do otherwise are wretched; we desire that none to whom these elements of our melodies come shall be such men. Rather, we happily wish for them the honor of an entire life of harmony, hoping they will live mindful of us.
