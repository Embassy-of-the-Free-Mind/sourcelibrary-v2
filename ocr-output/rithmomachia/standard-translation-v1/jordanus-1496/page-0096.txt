By the twenty-third [proposition] of the third book of *Arithmetic* [[notes: Referring to Boethius’s *De Arithmetica*]], since 64, 72, and 81 are two *sesquioctaves* [[notes: A 9:8 ratio; 72 is 9/8 of 64, and 81 is 9/8 of 72]] joined in their smallest numbers, they are therefore two tones joined in their smallest integers. And if you multiply nine by 64, 72, and 81, and multiply eight by 81, the numbers *d, e, f, g* arise. Through the same method, among *d, e, f, g*, three sesquioctaves are established in their smallest numbers; thus *d, e, f, g* are the smallest numbers for three continuous tones. And if you multiply nine into *d, e, f, g* and eight into *g*, and *h, i, k, l, m* arise, by the same logic as before, these are the smallest numbers for four continuous sesquioctaves, and thus for four continuous tones. And if again in this way you multiply nine into *h, i, k, l, m* and eight into *m*, and *n, o, p, q, r, s* arise, these will be the smallest numbers for five continuous tones. In this manner, you may find the minimum numbers for as many tones as you wish. However, in musical modes, it is not necessary to add so many continuously; rather, semitones are joined to tones, about which a more suitable discussion is to be expected later.

### ¶ When any space is divided into equal spaces, the ratio of the whole to the first part of the section is smaller than the ratio of that same part to the rest of the first section. From this it follows that the higher a tone is joined to another tone, the narrower the spaces it occupies.

[[image-desc: A linear scale diagram representing a string or musical interval labeled from 'a' at the left to 'b' at the right. The line is divided into nine equal segments by tick marks labeled 'c', 'd', 'e', 'f', 'g', 'h', 'i', 'k'.]]

¶ In harmonic modes, we call "space" (as has already been said) the string, the gut, the pipe, the exhaled air, the ringing brass, or anything else that produces sound. In these, the principle of analogy preserves the same power and nature. Let *a b* be the whole space divided into nine equal parts by *a c, c d, d e, e f*, and the remaining marks, as is done to obtain the interval of a tone; for however many others you might place, the result is the same. I say that the ratio of *a b* to *c b* is smaller than *c b* to *d b*. For since *a b* is set at nine equal parts divided by the middle marks *c, d, e, f* and the rest, *c b* contains only eight of those parts, while *a b* contains nine. Therefore, *a b* contains the space *c b* and its eighth part. But since *c b* contains eight parts equal to the parts of *c b* [[notes: The author is comparing the relative sizes of the segments]], *d b* contains only seven of those total parts. Therefore, *c b* contains the space *d b* and, moreover, its seventh part. But an eighth part, by definition, is smaller than a seventh part; therefore, again by definition, the *supparticular* ratio [[notes: A ratio where the numerator is the denominator plus one, like 9:8 or 8:7]] of *a b* to *c b* is smaller than *c b* to *d b*, inasmuch as it is named after a smaller part. For the latter is a *sesquiseptimal* ratio [[notes: 8:7]] from the seventh part, while the former is a *sesquioctave* [[notes: 9:8]] from the eighth part. This is demonstrated not only in supparticulars, but in any means by the first [proposition] of the tenth book of *Arithmetic*, where the ratio of the arithmetic mean is found. This was observed in the joined division of the space (as proposed) when the ratio was taken. ¶ The corollary may be recognized from this: the whole, through the fourth petition, sounds lower than its part, while the part sounds higher than its whole. Thus, if you join a tone to the tone *a b* and *c b*, the joined tone will be higher. And since the space *c b* would be divided into nine equal parts, any one of them will be smaller than the space *c d* (which is its eighth part), for any of them is a ninth and is named by a larger number. Therefore, any of those nine parts will be smaller than the space *a c*, for *a c* and *c d* are equal. In this way, it must be said for any joined higher tones. It is manifest, therefore, that the higher a tone is joined to another, the narrower the spaces it occupies. [[notes: This explains why frets on a guitar or finger positions on a violin get closer together as you move toward the bridge.]]

### ¶ When the space between the endpoints of a tone is divided into two equal parts in the middle, the tone is not divided into two equal [ratios].

[[image-desc: A diagram showing a musical scale 'a-b' divided into nine primary parts (c through k). Below this, a second row of labels 'l', 'm', 'n', 'o', 'p', 'q', 'r', 's', 't' marks the midpoints of each of those nine segments, effectively dividing the whole into eighteen parts.]]

¶ This and the following are understood such that a tone is not divided into two equal parts by a geometric ratio, nor by an arithmetic one. For here, the interval of the tone has been torn into two equal parts by an arithmetic ratio. I divide the space *a b*, therefore, as before into nine equal spaces by the marks *a, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, k, b*. It is manifest that *a b* and *c b* are the extremes of the tone and contain the tonic [interval]. I say, therefore, that since *a c* (the interval between these extremes) is torn into two equal parts by the sign *l*, the tone is not divided into two equal parts, because the sound of *a b* to *l b* is not equal to *l b* to *c b*. For I divide each of the remaining eight equal spaces in a similar way into equal parts by the marks *m, n, o, p, q, r, s, t*. It is manifest that the whole space *a b* is divided into 18 equal spaces, which are *a l, l m, m d, d n, n e, e o, o f, f p, p g, g q, q h, h r, r i, i s, s k, k t, t b*. Therefore, by the preceding [proof], the ratio of *a b* to *l b* is smaller than *l b* to *c b*. For one is a *sesquiseptidecimal* [[notes: 18:17]] and the other is a *sesquisedecimal* [[notes: 17:16]]. Therefore, the tone is not divided into two equal parts in this way. And the sounds *a b, l b* and *l b, c b* are, by definition, unequal to each other; indeed, these sounds are *ecmelic* [[notes: Unmusical or "out of tune"]] and very poorly suited to musical melody.

### ¶ When the space of a tone is divided in this way, the sound of the middle section exceeds the sound of the larger extreme of the tone in sharpness, but exceeds the smaller extreme in gravity.

[[image-desc: The scale diagram from Proposition 5 is repeated, showing the primary divisions 'a-k-b' and the bisection points starting with 'l'.]]

¶ Let *a b* be divided as has already been said in the preceding, so that *a b* to *c b* sounds a tone. I say that the sound *l b* exceeds the sound *a b* in sharpness [[notes: pitch]], and likewise the sound *l b* surpasses *c b* in gravity [[notes: depth/lowness]]. For *a b* is the whole and *l b* is its part; therefore, by the fourth petition, *a b* emits a lower sound and *l b* a thinner [higher] one. And by the same logic, since *l b* is the whole relative to *c b*, the sound *l b* is lower than the sound *c b*. Therefore, *l b* exceeds the larger extreme of the tone in sharpness and the smaller extreme in gravity. This was the proposition to be demonstrated.

### ¶ It is impossible for a tone to be divided into two equal parts by a certain, established number.

¶ For by the fifth [proposition] of the first book of this work, no *supparticular* interval is divided into several equal parts; and whatever ratio the spaces of the interval have to each other, such is also the ratio of sound to sound. But a tone is born from a supparticular interval. For it is born from an *epogdoic* or *sesquioctave* ratio [[notes: 9:8]]. Therefore, a tone is not divided or split into two equal parts. Indeed, it cannot even be divided into more than two equal parts, such as three or four. ¶ From this, it may easily be recognized that the musician Aristoxenus [[notes: A Greek theorist who argued that the ear, not math, should judge intervals]], who committed everything to the judgment of the ears, was quite mistaken. He did not think, as the Pythagoreans do, that semitones are not exactly half-tones; rather, just as they are called "semitones," he said they were also complete halves of tones. Martianus Felix [[notes: Martianus Capella, author of an influential encyclopedia on the liberal arts]] is caught in a similar slip of error...
