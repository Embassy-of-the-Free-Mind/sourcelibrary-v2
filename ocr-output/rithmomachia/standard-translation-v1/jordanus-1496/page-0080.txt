$\P$ **27** When three numbers are taken in this [geometric] mediety: it is impossible for the ratio of the greatest to the least and the ratio of the middle to either of the differences to be the same. [[notes: A "mediety" here refers to a sequence or progression. In a geometric mediety like 4, 2, 1, the "differences" are 4-2=2 and 2-1=1. This proposition states that the ratio of the extremes (4:1) cannot equal the ratio of the middle to a difference (2:2 or 2:1).]]
$\P$ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in this mediety, and let their differences be **d** and **e**. I say it is impossible for the ratio of **a** to **c** to be the same as **b** to **d** or **b** to **e**. For if **a** were to **c** as **b** is to **e**, then by permutation **a** would be to **b** as **c** is to **e**. And because by the twentieth [proposition] of this [book] **b** is to **c** as **d** is to **e**, by permutation **b** will be to **d** as **c** is to **e**. Therefore, whatever the ratio of **a** to **b**, that will be the ratio of **b** to **d**. But since **a** is greater than **b**, then by the fourth [proposition] of the second [book], **b** must be greater than **d**. Therefore, the ratio of **a** to **b** (a greater portion) would be as **b** (a greater portion) to a smaller one. Which by the eleventh [proposition] of the fifth [book] is impossible; and thus the proposition is proved.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A line diagram with numbers 8, 4, 2 at intervals. Numbers 4, 2 are written above gaps. Below, a sequence of numbers 36, 30, 6, 5, 1 are associated with points and segments on lines labeled a, b, c.]] ]]
$\P$ **28** Of three numbers of this mediety, the greater the ratio of the greatest to the least: the smaller the ratio of the middle term to either of the differences.
$\P$ Let the hypothesis be as before. This is so because as the ratio of **a** to **c** increases, the difference between **b** and **c** becomes proportionally larger, and therefore the number **e** becomes larger. Consequently, the ratio of **b** to **e** becomes smaller. Likewise, as **a** to **c** is greater, **a** to **b** is also greater, and as **a** to **b** is greater, the difference **d** is greater. However, as the difference **d** is greater, the ratio of **b** to it becomes smaller. This is what was proposed.

$\P$ **29** To continue any number of terms in a geometric mediety: such that the ratio of the extremes is not greater than a given ratio, and the smallest difference is not less than a given number.
$\P$ I divide the given ratio by the sixty-ninth [proposition] into as many equal ratios as desired. If the smallest difference is equal to or greater than the given number, the task is done. But if the smallest of the differences is less than the given number, I multiply the given number by any number of those ratios, and the result will fulfill the proposition.

$\P$ **30** If between numbers of the same [geometric] mediety, means are established according to the arithmetic mediety: those [means] will be arranged in a geometric mediety. [[notes: This observes that if you take two geometric sequences and find the arithmetic midpoints between their corresponding terms, those midpoints themselves form a geometric sequence. For example, if sequence A is (8, 4) and sequence B is (4, 2), the arithmetic means are 6 and 3, which form a geometric ratio of 2:1.]]
$\P$ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be arranged continuously in this mediety. Let **d** be the [arithmetic] mean between **a** and **b**, and **e** between **b** and **c** according to the arithmetic mediety. I say that the ratio of **a** to **b** is the same as **d** to **e**. For by the nineteenth [proposition] of this [book], as **a** is to **b**, so is **b** to **c**. Therefore, by permutation, as **a** is to **d**, so is **b** to **e**. This is what was proposed.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A line diagram with labels a, b, c on three horizontal lines. a=8, b=5, c=4. To the right, numbers 25 and 16 are listed with a difference of 9 noted between them.]] ]]
$\P$ **31** If between two numbers two means are proposed, one in the arithmetic and the other in the geometric mediety: the square of the one which is according to the arithmetic [mean] exceeds the other [the square of the geometric mean] by as much as is the square of the difference between itself and either of the extremes. [[notes: Modern notation: If $A$ is the arithmetic mean and $G$ is the geometric mean of two numbers, then $A^2 - G^2 = (A - extreme)^2$. For numbers 8 and 2, $A=5$ and $G=4$. $5^2 - 4^2 = 25 - 16 = 9$, which is the square of $(5-8)$ or $(5-2)$.]]
$\P$ Let **b** and **c** be two means between **a** and **d**, with **b** being the arithmetic mean and **c** the geometric. I say that the square of **b** exceeds the square of **c** by as much as the square of the difference between **b** and **a**, or **b** and **d** (which is the same). For by the twenty-first [proposition] of this [book], the product of **a** times **d** is equal to the square of **c**. And by the third [proposition] of this [book], the square of **b** adds to the product of **a** and **d** the square of the difference between **a** and **b**. Therefore, the square of **b** adds that same amount over the square of **c**. This is what was proposed.

$\P$ **32** To find three numbers in an arithmetic mediety: between which it is possible to assign means in pairs according to the geometric mediety. [[notes: This problem asks for three squares ($l, m, n$) that are in an arithmetic progression (having a common difference). For example: 49, 169, and 289 all differ by 120. Because they are squares ($7^2, 13^2, 17^2$), integer geometric means can be placed between them.]]
$\P$ Let **a** and **b** be two even numbers, and let **b** be either equal to or [a multiple of] **a**. Let **c** be the sum of **a** and **b**, and let **d** be its double. Let **a** be multiplied by **c** to produce **e**. Because by the first [proposition] of the seventh [book] the sum of **a** and **c** is even, and by the twelfth [proposition] of the same, **e** is even. Therefore, by the sixth [proposition] of the same, if that sum is subtracted from **c**, the remainder is even. Let the remainder be the even number **f**, and its half be **g**. Let the sum of **g** and **c** be **h**. Likewise, let **h** and **a** be summed to make **k**. Let **l**, **m**, and **n** be the squares of **k**, **h**, and **g**, which I say are the numbers we seek. For by the fifteenth [proposition] of the first [book], the square **l** (from **k**) exceeds the square **m** (from **h**) by the square of **a** and twice the product of **h** and **a**. This is equal to the square of **a** plus the product of **a** with **d** and **f**. [The proof continues demonstrating the common difference]... wherefore, as much as **l** exceeds **m**, so **m** exceeds **n**. Thus the squares **l**, **m**, and **n** are established in an arithmetic mediety. And by the corollary of the fourth [proposition] of the sixth [book], there is one proportional mean [a geometric mean] between **l** and **m**, and similarly between **m** and **n**. Thus the task is performed.

$\P$ **33** If, having taken three terms in an arithmetic mediety, means are established between them according to the geometric mediety: the square of the middle term of the original three must be in arithmetic mediety between the squares of the geometric means.
$\P$ Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be numbers in an arithmetic mediety found by the preceding [proposition], between which are **d** and **e** according to the geometric mediety. I say that the square of the middle term **b** is in arithmetic mediety between the squares of the means **d** and **e**. For because the square of **d**, by the twenty-sixth [proposition] of the second [book], is equal to the product of **b** and **a**, and by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book] the square of **d** adds to the square of **b** the product of **b** and its difference from **a**. Similarly, the square of **b** adds to the square of **e** the product of **b** and its difference from **c**. But the difference of **b** to **a** and **b** to **c** is equal [since they are in arithmetic progression]; therefore the squares of the numbers **d**, **b**, and **e** are in an arithmetic mediety. This is what was proposed.

$\P$ **34** When three terms are ordered in a harmonic mediety: the ratio of the greatest to the middle term will be greater than the ratio of the middle to the minimum. [[notes: A harmonic mediety (like 6, 4, 3) is defined by the property that the ratio of the extremes is equal to the ratio of their differences from the middle term. This proposition notes a property of their relative ratios.]]
