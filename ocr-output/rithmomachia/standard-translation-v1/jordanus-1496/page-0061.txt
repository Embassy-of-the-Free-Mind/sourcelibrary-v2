<margin>c</margin> Equality is the principle of inequality. There are five species of inequality: for either the greater of the terms contains the lesser a certain number of times so that nothing remains over, and it is called a **multiplex ratio** [[notes: Examples include 2:1 (double), 3:1 (triple), etc.]]; or it contains it only once and one part of it, and it is called **superparticular** [[notes: Examples include 3:2 (sesquialter) or 4:3 (sesquitertian).]]; or once and several of its parts, and it is called **superpartient** [[notes: For example, 5:3, which is 1 and 2/3.]]; or multiple times and one part of it, and it is called **multiplex superparticular** [[notes: For example, 5:2, which is 2 and 1/2.]]; or multiple times and several of its parts, and it is named **multiplex superpartient** [[notes: For example, 8:3, which is 2 and 2/3.]]. <insert>C</insert> The relationship of the lesser to the greater also contains just as many species, which vary among themselves just as the previous ones did according to the diversity of their denominations [[notes: These are the "sub-" versions, such as sub-multiplex (1:2) or sub-superparticular (2:3).]]. <insert>C</insert> To multiply something by a part is to take that part of the whole, as great as the part that multiplies it. <insert>C</insert> Whatever else it may multiply, we say that which is produced relates to the thing multiplied just as the multiplier relates to one. Something is said to be divided by another when that "divider" is taken which, when multiplied by the same, restores the sum of the thing divided.

<margin>1</margin> ¶ Of unequal numbers, the ratio of the greater to the lesser is the same as the ratio of the part named by the lesser to the part named by the greater.
¶ Let *a* and *b* be unequal numbers: *a* the greater and *b* the lesser. And let *d* and *e* be the parts named by them [[notes: In medieval terms, the "part named by 4" is 1/4. This proposition states that 4:2 is the same ratio as (1/2):(1/4).]], namely *d* named by *b* and *e* named by *a*. I say that the ratio of *a* to *b* is the same as *d* to *e*. For I place one [[notes: A unit, or 1.]] between *a* and *b*, which shall be *c*, and one between *d* and *e*, which shall be *f*. And because the ratio of *a* to one is the same as the ratio of one to the part named by *a*, that is the ratio of *f* to *e*. And as the ratio of one to *b* is the same as the ratio of the part named by *b* to one, that is *d* to *f*. Therefore, by the twenty-seventh [proposition] of the second [book] and by equal indirect proportionality, the ratio of *a* to *b* is the same as *d* to *e*: namely, the part named by the lesser to the part named by the greater. Which is what was proposed.

<margin>2</margin> ¶ To reduce any number of parts into equal parts [[notes: This is the process of finding a common denominator for fractions.]].
¶ Let the given parts be *a*, *b*, *c* and the numbers naming them [[notes: The denominators.]] be *d*, *e*, *f*. The goal is to reduce *a*, *b*, *c* into parts of the same denomination and equality. I take, through the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the third [book], the minimum number which *d*, *e*, *f* measure [[notes: The Least Common Multiple (LCM).]], which shall be *g*. Let them measure it according to *h*, *k*, *l*, and let *m* be the part named by *g*. For by the preceding [proposition], as *g* is to *d*, so *a* is to *m*. But *d* measures *g* according to *h*; therefore *m* will also measure *a* according to *h*. And by the same [reasoning], as *g* is to *e*, so *b* is to *m*. Since *e* measures *g* according to *k*, *m* will measure *b* according to *k*. Likewise, *m* will measure *c* according to *l* by the same reasoning. Thus those parts will be reduced to equal parts: namely *a* into the parts called by *g* according to *h*, and *b* into the same according to *k*, and *c* into the same parts called by *g* according to *l*. But if *a*, *b*, *c* are not single parts but are [composed of] multiple parts, the same will happen... [[notes: Jordanus here explains that the method works for both unit fractions (1/n) and common fractions (m/n).]]

<margin>3</margin> ¶ To reduce a number given with a part or parts into similar parts [[notes: Converting a mixed number into an improper fraction.]].
¶ Let *a* be a number and *b*, *c*, *d* be given parts; let it first be required to reduce *a* and *b* into similar parts. I multiply the number naming part *b* by *a*. And since *b* is a part of one, and one is a part of the whole *a* named by *a*: therefore by the twenty-third [proposition] of the first [book], *b* is a part of *a* named by the number which results from the multiplication of the denominators... [[notes: Jordanus is explaining the logic of multiplying the whole number by the denominator and adding the numerator.]]

<margin>4</margin> ¶ That which results from the multiplication of two parts of a unit by one another is a part named by the number which results from the multiplication of the two numbers naming those parts.
¶ Let *a* and *b* be two parts, and the numbers naming them be *c* and *d*. From *c* times *d* let *e* be made, and let the part named by *e* be *f*. I say that *f* is the part produced from *a* times *b* [[notes: This proves that 1/c * 1/d = 1/(cd).]]. For by definition, as one is to *c*, so is *d* to *e*. But also as one is to *c*, so is *a* to one. Therefore as *a* is to one, so is *d* to *e*. But by the first [proposition] of this [book], as *d* is to *e*, so is *f* to *b*. And by the definition, as *a* is to one, so the product of *a* times *b* is to *b*. Since *f* relates to *b* in this same way, *f* is by definition the product of *a* times *b*. Which is what was proposed.

<margin>5</margin> ¶ If any number of things are multiplied by something, the product is as much as if each of them were multiplied by that same thing [[notes: The distributive law: (a + b + c) * d = ad + bd + cd.]].
¶ This is similar to the tenth [proposition] of the first [book] but more general. Let there be a number *a*; let *e* be the sum of *a*, *b*, and *c* which multiplies *d* (whether *d* be a number, a part, or parts) and let *e* be the result. And from the multiplication of *a* by *d* let *f* be made; from *b* by *d* let *g* be made; and from *c* by *d* let *h* be made. I say that *f*, *g*, and *h* together are as much as *e*. For by definition, one will be to *a*, and to *b*, and to *c*, just as *d* is to *f*, and to *g*, and to *h*. Therefore by the twenty-third [proposition] of the second [book], one is to [the sum] *a*, *b*, *c* as *d* is to [the sum] *f*, *g*, *h*. But by definition, as one is to *a*, *b*, *c*, so is *d* to *e*. Therefore *f*, *g*, and *h* together are as much as *e*. Which is what was proposed.
