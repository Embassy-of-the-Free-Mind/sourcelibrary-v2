In the same manner, the following proposition is set forth. Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be any number of quantities in a continuous proportionality [[notes: A continuous proportion is a sequence where the second term of the first ratio is the first term of the next, e.g., a:b and b:c]], and let them correspond directly to as many others, **d**, **e**, and **f**. Thus, as **a** is to **b**, so **d** is to **e**; and as **b** is to **c**, so **e** is to **f**. I say that the ratio of **a** to **c** is the same as **d** to **f**. For since the ratio of **a** to **b** is that of **d** to **e**, then by permutation [[notes: "By permutation" (alternando) means swapping the inner terms of the proportion: if a:b = d:e, then a:d = b:e]] according to the third [proposition] of this book, the ratio of **a** to **d** is as **b** to **e**. And because the ratio of **b** to **c** is as **e** to **f**, therefore by that same third proposition, by permutation, the ratio of **b** to **e** is as **c** to **f**. Wherefore, **a** to **d** is also as **c** to **f**. Since, therefore, the ratio of **a** to **d** is the same as **c** to **f**, then by permutation according to the third proposition, the ratio of **a** to **c** is the same as **d** to **f**, which is what was proposed.

## If any number of quantities are proportional to as many others in an indirect order, the extremes will also be proportional in that same ratio.

Let **a** be to **b** as **e** is to **f**, and **b** to **c** as **d** is to **e**, being proportional in an inverse order [[notes: This is often called "perturbed proportion." Instead of a:b = d:e and b:c = e:f, the second set is flipped or shifted]]. I say that **a** is to **c** as **d** is to **f**. For I multiply **d** by **e** and **f**, and let **g** and **h** be produced; and I multiply **e** by **f**, and let **k** be produced. By the seventh [proposition] of this book, the ratio of **d** to **e** is as **g** to **h**; wherefore the ratio of **b** to **c** is the same as **g** to **h**. And because **d** and **e** multiply **f**, by the eighth [proposition], the ratio of **d** to **e** is as **g** to **k**. And because **g** is made from **d** times **e**, and **k** from **e** times **f**, and therefore **k** by the eighth [proposition] of the first book is made alternately from **f** times **e**, then by the eighth of this book, the ratio of **d** to **f** is as **g** to **k**. But by the preceding [demonstration], as **a** is to **b**, so is **g** to **h**; and as **b** is to **c**, so is **h** to **k**. Therefore, as **a** is to **c**, so is **g** to **k**. And since the ratio of **g** to **k** was shown to be the same as the ratio of **d** to **f**, it will therefore be that as **a** is to **c**, so is the ratio of **d** to **f**, which was to be demonstrated.

[[margin: 23]]
## If the first is to the second as the third is to the fourth, and the first is also to the fifth as the third is to the sixth: then as the first is to the sum of the second and fifth, so the third is to the sum of the fourth and sixth.

Let the first number be **a**, the second **b**, the third **c**, the fourth **d**, the fifth **e**, and the sixth **f**. Let **a** be to **b** as **c** is to **d**, and let **a** also be to **e** as **c** is to **f**. I say that as **a** is to the whole **b + e**, so is **c** to the whole **d + f**. For because **a** is to **b** as **c** is to **d**, it will be by the first [proposition] of this book that as **b** is to **a**, so is **d** to **c**. They will therefore be continuously proportional: as **b** is to **a**, so is **d** to **c**; and as **a** is to **e**, so is **c** to **f**. Wherefore, by the twenty-first [proposition], as **b** is to **e**, so is **d** to **f**. Therefore, by the sixth [proposition] of this book, they will be joined [[notes: "Joined" (conjunctim) refers to the rule of composition: if b:e = d:f, then (b+e):e = (d+f):f]]: as **b + e** is to **e**, so is **d + f** to **f**. Therefore, by the first of this book, as **e** is to **b + e**, so is **f** to **d + f**. Since, therefore, as **a** is to **e**, so is **c** to **f**, and as **e** is to **b + e**, so is **f** to **d + f**, then by the twenty-first proposition, as **a** is to **b + e**, so is **c** to **d + f**, which is what was proposed.

[[margin: 24]]
## If the first is to the second as the third is to the fourth, and the first is the greatest, the number composed of the first and fourth will be greater than the number composed of the second and third.

Let **ab** [[notes: The author uses two letters to name a single quantity, representing a line segment]] be the first number, **c** the second, **de** the third, and **f** the fourth. Let it be that as **ab** is to **c**, so is **de** to **f**, and let **ab** be the greatest of those four numbers. I say that the sum of **ab** and **f** is greater than the sum of **c** and **de**. For since **ab** is the greatest, it is greater than the second, **c**; wherefore by the second [proposition] of this book, **de** is greater than the fourth, **f**. And since as **ab** is to **c**, so is **de** to **f**, then by permutation through the third proposition, as **ab** is to **de**, so is **c** to **f**. But since **ab** is the greatest by hypothesis, it is greater than **de**. Therefore, by the second proposition, **c** is also greater than **f**. Thus, **f** is the smallest of them. Let **b** be the difference between **ab** and **c**, and **e** the difference between **de** and **f**. Because as **ab** is to **c**, it has been proved as **c** to **f** [[notes: There appears to be a slight OCR error or shorthand here; the logic follows that ratios of differences must match the ratios of the wholes]]. Thus, by the fifth proposition, as **ab** is to **de**, so is the remainder **b** to the remainder **e**. Wherefore, by the second proposition, since **ab** is greater than **de**, then **b** is also greater than **e**. Thus, **b** and **f** together are greater than **de** [[notes: This follows because ab = c + b, and the proof shows that adding the smallest and largest term results in a larger sum than the middle two]]. It is therefore that the sum of the first and fourth, **ab** and **f**, is greater than the sum of the second and third, **c** and **de**, which is what was proposed.

## If four numbers are proportional, the product contained under the extremes is equal to that which is produced from the means. If the products are equal, the numbers will be proportional.

That which is "contained under the extremes" is said to be the product of the first extreme multiplied by the last. Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be four proportional numbers, such that the ratio of **a** to **b** is the same as **c** to **d**. I say, first, that the product of **a** times **d** is equal to the product of **b** times **c**. Second, if the product of **a** times **d** is equal to **b** times **c**, those numbers are proportional. The first part is clear: let **e** be produced from the multiplication of **a** by **d**, and **f** from **b** by **c**, which I say are equal. I also multiply **a** by **b** and let **g** be produced. By the seventh [proposition] of this book, the ratio of **g** to **e** will be as **b** is to **d**. But also, because **g** comes from **a** times **b**, alternately **g** comes from **b** times **a**. And since **f** comes from **b** times **c**, then again by the seventh proposition, **g** is to **f** as **a** is to **c**. And because as **a** is to **b**, so **c** is to **d**, then by permutation through the third proposition, as **a** is to **c**, so **b** is to **d**. Wherefore, **g** is to **f** as **b** is to **d**. It was also proved that **g** is to **e** as **b** is to **d**; wherefore **e** and **f**, which have the same ratio to the same number [**g**], are equal to each other. This is the first part. Furthermore, I say if **e** and **f** are equal to each other, then **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** are proportional. For since **e** and **f** are equal, the ratio of **g** to them is one and the same. And because, as was said, **g** is to **f** as **a** is to **c**, and **g** is to **e** as **b** is to **d**, then **a** is to **c** as **b** is to **d**. Therefore, by permutation through the third proposition, **a** is to **b** as **c** is to **d**, which means the numbers **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** are proportional to each other, which is the second part.

## For three proportional numbers, the product contained under the extremes is equal to the square of the mean. If those are equal, the three numbers will be in continuous proportion.

Let **a**, **b**, and **c** be three numbers in continuous proportion. I say, first, that the product of **a** times **c** is equal to the square of **b**. Second, if the square of **b** is equal to the product of **a** times **c**, the three numbers **a**, **b**, and **c** are in continuous proportion. You prove the first part by the first part of the preceding proposition, by setting **b** twice, or any number such as **d** equal to **b**. And the second part by the second part of the preceding; there is no difficulty in the demonstration.
