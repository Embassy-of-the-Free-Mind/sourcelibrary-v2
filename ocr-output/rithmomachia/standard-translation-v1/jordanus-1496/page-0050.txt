### ¶ **According to a given even number: to take even-times-odd numbers which, when joined together, constitute an odd-times-even number.** [[margin: 47]]

¶ Let **a** be a given even number according to which it is ordered to take even-times-odd numbers [[notes: "Even-times-odd" (*pariter impares*) refers to numbers like 6, 10, 14, which are the product of 2 and an odd number]] which, when joined, constitute an odd-times-even number [[notes: "Odd-times-even" (*impariter parem*) refers to even numbers that are not powers of 2 and have an even half, such as 12, 20, or 24]]. I take **b**, **e**, **h** as continuous even-times-even numbers after the number two, according to half of the number **a**, around which let **c, d**, **f, g**, and **k, l** be the even-times-odd numbers placed around them. I say that **c, d, f, g, k, l** are even-times-odd numbers taken according to the number **a**: and being joined together they constitute an odd-times-even number. And let **m** be the double of **h**, which also by the twenty-ninth [proposition] of this [book] is an even-times-even. For because by the second [proposition] of the first [book] **c** and **d** together are as much as **e**, the double of **b**; and by the same [rule] **f** and **g** are as much as **h**; and **k** and **l** are as much as **m**. Thus **c, d, f, g, k, l** together will be as much as the three even-times-even numbers **e, h, m** taken continuously after the number two. But **e, h, m** together, by the forty-fifth [proposition] of this [book], constitute an odd-times-even number; therefore **c, d, f, g, k, l** do so as well together. Which is what was proposed.

### ¶ **To find any number of odd-times-even numbers which, when added together, make an even-times-even number.** [[margin: 48]]

¶ First, let **a** be a given even number according to which it is proposed to find odd-times-even numbers which, added together, constitute an even-times-even number. I find, by the forty-sixth [proposition] of this [book], just as many even-times-odd numbers which, added together, constitute an even-times-even number; let these be **b, c, d, e**. And I multiply any even-times-even number into each of them; let there result **f, g, h, l**. These just as many, by the thirty-eighth [proposition] of this [book], will be odd-times-even numbers. But by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book], that which is produced from that even-times-even into [the sum of] **b, c, d, e** is the same as the sum produced from the multiplication into each. But the sum of **b, c, d, e**, by the hypothesis, is an even-times-even; therefore, by the thirtieth [proposition] of this [book], that which results from an even-times-even multiplied by [the sum of] **b, c, d, e** is an even-times-even. Moreover, that is the sum of the odd-times-even numbers **f, g, h, l** found according to the given number. Therefore, what was proposed has been achieved in this regard. 
¶ Secondly, let it be that **a** is an odd number according to which it is proposed to find odd-times-even numbers that constitute an even-times-even. I take, through what was just demonstrated, odd-times-even numbers according to an even number one less than **a**, which when aggregated constitute an even-times-even number; let these be **b** and **c**. And let **d** be the even-times-even number gathered from **b** and **c**. I take **f**, the third even-times-even number from it [[notes: if **d** is $2^n$, then **f** is $2^{n+2}$]], with the middle even-times-even **e** placed between them. And because the difference of **f** to **d** consists of the difference of **d** to **e**, which is the even-times-even **d**, and the difference of **e** to **f**, which is the even-times-even **e**, since the following **f** is double the preceding. Therefore, by the forty-fifth [proposition] of this [book], the difference of **f** to **d** is an odd-times-even. Let that difference be **g**. I say, therefore, that **b, c, g** are the odd-times-even numbers that are sought. For **b** and **c** together are **d**, to which is added the odd-times-even **g**, making as much as **f**. For it was the difference of **d** to **f**. But **f** is an even-times-even number. Thus the whole proposition stands demonstrated.

### ¶ **From a given number: to investigate odd-times-even numbers from which, when joined, an odd-times-even number is produced.** [[margin: 49]]

¶ First, let the given number according to which we are ordered to investigate the odd-times-even numbers be an even number **a**. I find just as many even-times-odd numbers by the forty-sixth [proposition] of this [book] which, when joined, constitute an even-times-even number; let these be **b, c, d, e**. I take **g**, the first even-times-odd number after **e**. Because it differs from **e** only by the number four, it is manifest that **b, c, d, g** together are more than the even-times-even [sum of] **b, c, d, e** together, and less than double the [sum of] **b, c, d, e**. Therefore, by the twenty-ninth [proposition] of this [book], the aggregate of the even-times-odd numbers **b, c, d, g** is not an even-times-even number. And since by the first [proposition] of this [book] it is even, the same will be, by division, either an even-times-odd or an odd-times-even. I therefore multiply any even-times-even number **f** into **b, c, d, g**, and let there result **h, l, k, m**: as many as the units in **a**, which by the thirty-eighth [proposition] of this [book] are odd-times-even numbers. But the aggregate of **h, k, l, m** is just as much as that which is produced from **f** into **b, c, d, g** together by the ninth [proposition] of the first [book]. If **b, c, d, g** together are an even-times-odd, then by the thirty-eighth [proposition] of this [book], the aggregate of **h, k, l, m** will be an odd-times-even. And if **b, c, d, g** be an odd-times-even, the same will happen by the forty-first [proposition] of this [book]; so the proposition will always be held. 
¶ But if **a** be an odd number, I take just as many even-times-odd numbers, which when aggregated by the forty-fourth [proposition] of this [book] will be even-times-odd. These I multiply by any even-times-even number, and there will result just as many odd-times-even numbers by the thirty-eighth [proposition] of this [book]. And because these odd-times-even numbers together are just as much as what is produced from that even-times-even into the aggregate of even-times-odd numbers, which was shown to be even-times-odd, therefore by the thirty-ninth [proposition] it will be that the aggregate of the odd-times-even numbers is an odd-times-even. Which is what was proposed.

### ¶ **Any number of odd-times-even numbers and even-times-even numbers above two: joined together: will compose an even-times-even or an odd-times-even.** [[margin: 50]]

¶ For the halves of all of them are even. But they themselves together constitute the half of the composite [[notes: The author is arguing that the sum of these numbers must be divisible by 4, which excludes them from being "even-times-odd" numbers, which only have an odd half]]. Therefore, by the first [proposition] of this [book], the half of the composite is even. Therefore, the composite is not an even-times-odd number. By division, therefore, it is either an even-times-even or an odd-times-even. Which is what was intended.

### ¶ **To investigate any number of even-times-even and odd-times-even numbers: so that joined they constitute an even-times-even.** [[margin: 51]]

¶ Let **a** be a given number according to which we must aggregate even-times-even and odd-times-even numbers which constitute an even-times-even. I take just as many odd-times-even numbers which, by the forty-eighth [proposition] of this [book], being gathered together constitute an even-times-even; let these be **b** and **c**. And let the even-times-even they constitute be **d**. I take as many continuous even-times-even numbers above **d**, which let be **e** and **f**; it is certain that **e** is double **d** and **f** double **e**. I say, therefore, that the odd-times-even numbers **b, c** and just as many even-times-even numbers **d** and **e** together constitute an even-times-even. For because **b** and **c** together constitute **d**, therefore **b, c** and **d** together constitute **e**, the double of **d**. But also by the same [reasoning], **b, c, d** and **e** together will constitute **f**, the double of **e**. But **f** is an even-times-even; therefore, just as many even-times-even numbers and just as many odd-times-even numbers constitute an even-times-even. Which is what was proposed.
