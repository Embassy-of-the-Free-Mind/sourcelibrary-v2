<margin>26</margin> ### **¶ To find any number of multiples which, with a given superparticular, constitute a multiple-superparticular.**
¶ Let **a** be the given superparticular ratio [[notes: A superparticular ratio is of the form (n+1):n, like 3:2 or 4:3]], let **b** be the number naming its part [[notes: The denominator, e.g., 2 for a sesquialter (3:2)]], and let **m** be its total denomination. Let any number of multiples be assumed, whose naming numbers are **c, d, e, f**. It is manifest by the twenty-third [proposition] of this book that **b** into **c**, and into **d**, and into **e**, and into **f**, constitutes a number of a multiple and a denomination. I therefore take **g, h, k, l**, an equal number of multiples, each one greater than the denomination. And because **g** adds one over the multiple **c**: if the total denomination **m** of the superparticular ratio is multiplied by **g**, there will result a number with a part named by **b**; therefore the ratio is a multiple-superparticular. As in the present figure, there will result a septuple-sesquialter ratio [[notes: A ratio of 7 1/2 or 15:2]]. And you shall demonstrate the same for **h, k**, and **l**.

->f—10, e—8, d—6, c—4<-
->i—11, k—9, h—7, g—5<-
->3, 2 | a—2, b—1.2, m—I<-

<margin>27</margin> ### **¶ Given a multiple, to find any number of superparticulars which, with that same multiple, compose a multiple-superpartient.**
¶ Let **a** be the given multiple and **b** the number naming it. I take any number of numbers greater than it but not multiples of it, which are **g, h, k, l**, and let the parts named by them be **c, d, e, f**. So that **c** is named by **g**, and **d** by **h**, and so on for the following. I say that **b** into **c**, into **d**, into **e**, and into **f** produces not a single part, but parts [[notes: In this context, "parts" (plural) refers to a superpartient ratio, where the numerator is greater than 1, e.g., 2/3 or 3/5]]. For if **b** is multiplied by **c**, there result **m** parts named by **g**. By the corollary of the eleventh [proposition] of this book, as the ratio of **b** to **g**, so is **m** to one. But by the hypothesis **b** is "parts" of **g** [[notes: Meaning b/g is a non-reducible fraction with a numerator > 1]]; therefore **m** is parts of one. And thus if **b** is multiplied by **d, e,** and **f**, parts will result. Therefore, the superparticulars named by those parts, when combined with the given multiple, will each constitute a multiple-superpartient.

->l—9, f—I | k—7, e—I | h—5, d—I | g—3, c—I<-
->a—2, b—2 | 3, 2, I<-

<margin>28</margin> ### **¶ The sesquialter is the only superparticular which, with no multiple, produces a multiple-superpartient.**
¶ This is because the sesquialter [[notes: The ratio 3:2, or 1 1/2]] is named from the second [number, 2], which is a half of one; and a half multiplied into any number either produces a whole number or adds a half. For the number two measures every number, or else a unit remains. Therefore, the number naming the part of a sesquialter, when multiplied by the denominator of a multiple, will produce either a multiple or a multiple-superparticular [[notes: For example, 3/2 x 2 = 3 (multiple) and 3/2 x 3 = 4 1/2 (multiple-superparticular). It can never produce something like 4 2/3]]. Thus, it will not produce a multiple-superpartient with a multiple. That every other superparticular can effect a multiple-superpartient with some multiple is known from this: if you take a multiple naming-number one less than the number naming the part of the given superparticular, you will achieve the proposition by the preceding [theorem]. You can also easily know the first part from the method of demonstrating the twenty-sixth [proposition] of this book, just as you know the second part from the preceding.

**Composite**
| | | |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| f 10 | 9 | 20 / 9 |
| e 8 | 7 | 16 / 7 |
| d 6 | 5 | 12 / 5 |
| c 4 | 3 | 8 / 3 |
| a 2 | 1 | |

<margin>29</margin> ### **¶ The double is the only multiple which, with no superpartient, effects a multiple ratio.**
¶ This proposes that the double [[notes: The ratio 2:1]] constitutes a multiple with no superpartient, while every other multiple can constitute a multiple with some superpartient. The first part is clear: let **a** be a double ratio and **b** the number naming it [2]; let **c** be any superpartient ratio. I say that **a** with **c** does not constitute a multiple ratio. For I take the parts of **c** which are named by **d** and counted by **e** [[notes: i.e., the fraction e/d]], and I multiply **b** into those parts; there will result similar parts counted by the number produced from two—that is, **b** into **e**. Let the number of those parts be **f**; I say that this is not a whole number. For it will not be equal to **d**, because **e** would then measure **d** [[notes: If f/d were an integer, e/d wouldn't be a superpartient in its simplest form]]. Nor will **d** measure it. It will not measure it by one, because they would be equal. Nor by two, because then **d** and **e** would be equal. Nor by anything greater than two, because **d** is greater than **e**. Therefore, since **b** into **e** (the denominator into the parts) does not constitute a whole number, no multiple will be produced. The second part is clear: for given any other multiple such as **a**, I take the number **b** naming it, which I make the denominator of some parts; and I take a number of parts one less than **b**, which is **c**; and let **d** be the superpartient of parts **c**. I say that **a** and **d** together constitute a multiple. For because **b** is the name of **a** and the denominator of the parts of **d**, therefore **b** into **c** will constitute a whole number. Thus the composite will be named by a number; wherefore it will be a multiple. This is what was proposed.

**Composite**
->e 15 3 | a 3 I | b 3 | c 2 | d 5.3<-
->b, e, f, b | 3, 2, 4, 2 | 3, 2, I, 5 . 3<-

<margin>30</margin> ### **¶ Given a superpartient, to find any number of multiples which, each multiplied by it, produce a multiple.**
¶ Let **a** be the given superpartient, let the number of parts be **b**, and the number naming them be **c**. I first take a multiple named by **c**, which is **d**; then those named by multiples of **c** as many as I wish, which let be **e, f, g**. I say that the given superpartient **a** with any of the multiples **d, e, f, g** constitutes a multiple. For **b** into **c** will constitute a number, and into **e** also a number because it is double [c], and into **f** triple, and **g** quadruple, by taking the multiples of the denominator **c** in order. Therefore, the superpartient **a** with any of those multiples constitutes a multiple. This is what was proposed.

**Composite**
->60-3, 45-3, 30-3, 15-3<-
->f-12, e-9, d-6, c-3.I, a-5.3, b-2<-

<margin>31</margin> ### **¶ To find any number of superpartients, each of which with a proposed multiple constitutes a multiple-superparticular.**
¶ Let the proposed multiple be **a**, whose naming number is **b**. I take any number greater than **b**, which is **c**. And I multiply **b** into **c**, and let it be **d**. And I take **e**, which is one less than **d**. I also take the parts named by **e** according to the number **c**, which are **f**. Since **b** into **c** constitutes **d**, therefore **b** into **f** will constitute similar parts according to the number **d**. But those taken according to the number **d** make one and one more besides [[notes: i.e., (b * c) / (bc - 1) = d / (d-1) = 1 + 1/(d-1)]]. Let **g** be the superpartient named by the said parts from **e** and taken according to number **c**; because **b** into **f** constitutes a number and a part, that same superpartient with **a** will constitute a superparticular; and by the same reasoning, you may take as many superpartients as you wish which with **a** will constitute a superparticular. This is what was proposed.

->26, 5<-
->f-8.5, e-5, d-6, c-2.I, b-2<-
