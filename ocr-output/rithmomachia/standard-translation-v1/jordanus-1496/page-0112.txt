¶ A threefold progression <note>original: "ternaria pgressiõe"</note> is understood to be added to itself when a number is added to its first self once, then twice, then three times. I say, therefore, that any number added to itself in this manner establishes every musical consonance. For when added to itself once, it creates a double ratio [2:1] to itself and the **octave** <note>original: "diapason"</note> consonance. When added to itself twice, it establishes a **sesquialter** ratio [3:2] and a **fifth** <note>original: "diapente"</note> relative to the first addition; and relative to itself, it creates a triple ratio [3:1] and the consonance of an **octave-plus-fifth**. This is because the number is contained twice in the first addition and three times in the second. When added to itself a third time, it creates a **sesquitertial** ratio [4:3] and a **fourth** <note>original: "diatessaron"</note> relative to the second addition—for here the first number is contained four times, <gloss>indeed</gloss> whereas in the other it is contained three times. And relative to itself, it creates a quadruple ratio [4:1] and a **double octave**. Therefore, in this progression, every musical consonance is contained. And because the added number itself is the constant difference between the numbers rising from its continuous addition, the discovered consonances are contained within an **Arithmetic mean** <note>In an arithmetic mean, the quantity between terms is constant (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 4), unlike the harmonic mean where ratios are preserved.</note>. From this, it is understood why in the fourfold progression of all units—which everywhere fulfills the Pythagorean "ten-ness" <note>original: "denarũ Pythagoricũ"; the Decad (1+2+3+4=10) was the sacred number of the Pythagoreans, representing the universe and all musical ratios.</note>—all musical consonances are found, even within an arithmetic mean.

¶ To divide a whole tone and every simple consonance into two equal parts; and to show the true points of these halves on a string geometrically.

¶ The preceding seventh proposition of the second book and the twenty-third of the third book claim that the tone, the fourth, the fifth, and the octave cannot be divided into two equal parts. This text, <gloss>however</gloss>, demonstrates in what way all of them can be divided into two equal parts. Nor is this a contradiction. For the preceding authors contend that this cannot be done **Arithmetically** using certain, fixed numbers and rational proportions; <gloss>however</gloss> this text shows it can be done **geometrically** without a fixed or constant numerical ratio <note>The author is distinguishing between "rational" numbers (integers/fractions) and "irrational" geometric lengths. ¶ Therefore, let the string **a b** be given above, in which we are commanded to find a true half-tone and the true midpoints of the consonances of the fourth, fifth, and octave. I make **a b** and **c b** a tone; **a b** and **d b** a fourth; **a b** and **e b** a fifth; and **a b** and **f b** an octave, in the manner shown in the preceding sections. Then, on the lower line **a c** of indefinite length, I take **a b** equal to the upper line **a b**, and **b c** continuously equal to the upper line **b c**. From point **b** toward **c**, I take line **b d** equal to the string length **b d**, and **b e** equal to string **b e**, and **b f** to string **b f**. I understand there to be four semicircles: **a c**, **a d**, **a e**, and **a f**. From point **b**, I draw a line perpendicular to line **a c** up to the circumferences of the semicircles **a c**, **a d**, **a e**, and **a f**. Let the points where the line touches them be **g**, **h**, **i**, and **k**; to these points I draw **a g**, **c g**, **a h**, **d h**, **a i**, **e i**, **a k**, and **f k**. By the ninth proposition of the sixth book of Geometry <note>A reference to Euclid's Elements, regarding mean proportionals.</note>, **a b** is to **b g** as **b g** is to **b c**. I therefore make on the upper string **a b** a line **b g** equal to the lower line **b g**. Since the proportion of **a b** to **b g** is the same as **b g** to **c b**, as previously shown, it follows that the tone [between] **a b** and **c b** is divided into two equal parts; thus point **g** is the mark of the true half-tone <note>original: "semitonij"</note>. By the same logic, the proportion of **a b** to **b h** is the same as **b h** to **b d**. Therefore, let **h b** on the string **a b** be equal to line **b h** by the same method as before: the proportion of **a b** to **b h** will be as **b h** to **b d**. Thus the fourth [between] **a b** and **d b** is divided into two equal parts.
