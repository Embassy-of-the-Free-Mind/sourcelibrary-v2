[[notes: The pattern shown here is known as a **"stone" or "agate" marbling pattern**. Marbling is a decorative technique where paint is floated on a liquid bath of "size" (a thickened mucilage), manipulated into patterns, and then "printed" by laying a sheet of paper on the surface. This specific style—characterized by organic, cell-like droplets of color—was a staple of 18th and 19th-century bookbinding.]]

The surface of this page is covered in a dense, dark pattern consisting of organic, cell-like shapes. The primary colors are **deep black**, **burnt umber**, and **dark burgundy**. These dark regions are separated by fine, delicate veins and spots of **muted ochre** and **tan**, which define the boundaries between the larger "stones" of color. The overall tone is aged and somber, providing a protective and aesthetic finish to the manuscript's interior.

[[notes: In historical bookbinding, marbled endpapers served both a functional and an aesthetic purpose. Functionally, they helped to strengthen the hinge where the book cover meets the pages and hid the "turn-ins" (the edges of the leather or cloth folded over the boards). Aesthetically, they were a mark of quality and luxury.]]
