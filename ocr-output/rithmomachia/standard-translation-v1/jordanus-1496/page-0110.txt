## ¶ Regarding the **consonances** <note>In this context, "consonances" refers to musical intervals that sound pleasant together because of their mathematical ratios.</note> arranged in this manner: the end of a **perfect fourth** <note>original: "diatessaron"</note> sounds a **whole tone** <note>original: "tonum"</note> from the end of a **perfect fifth** <note>original: "diapente"</note>; relative to the end of an **octave** <note>original: "diapason"</note>, it sounds a **perfect fifth**; relative to the end of an **octave plus a fifth**, it is **dissonant** <note>original: "inconsonus"</note>; but relative to the end of a **double octave** <note>original: "bis diapason"</note>, it sounds an **octave plus a fifth**.

<image-desc>A horizontal line representing a monochord division with vertical strokes marking points. Above the line are the letters: a, c, d, e, f, g, b. The spacing reflects musical intervals.</image-desc>

-> | a | | c | d | | e | | f | g | | b | <-

¶ Let $a, c, d, e, f, g, b$ be arranged in the manner of the twenty consonances already mentioned. I say that point $c$ <note>This refers to the segment of the string from c to b.</note>, the end of the **perfect fourth**, sounds a **whole tone** relative to point $d$ (the end of the fifth); relative to $e$, it is a **perfect fifth**; relative to $f$, it is **dissonant**; and relative to $g$ and $b$ <note>The text likely means the segment gb.</note>, it sounds an **octave plus a fifth**. 

Following the previous proof, the ratio between the full string $ab$ and the segment $cb$ is a **perfect fourth** (4:3), and $ab$ to $db$ is a **perfect fifth** (3:2). Therefore, if we subtract the fourth ($ab$ to $cb$) from the fifth ($ab$ to $db$), by the corollary of the tenth theorem, a **whole tone** (9:8) remains. What remains is the distance between $cb$ and $db$; therefore, $cb$ to $db$ sounds a **whole tone**. 

And since by the previous proof $ab$ to $eb$ sounds an **octave**, if the **perfect fourth** $ab$ to $cb$ is subtracted from the **octave** $ab$ to $eb$, then according to the twenty-second theorem, what remains is a **perfect fifth**. Since the remainder is $cb$ and $eb$, then $cb$ relative to $eb$ sounds a **perfect fifth**. 

Furthermore, by the previous proof, $ab$ to $fb$ is an **octave plus a fifth**; if the **octave** $ab$ to $eb$ is subtracted, what remains is a **perfect fifth**. This remainder is $eb$ to $fb$. Therefore, $eb$ to $fb$ is a **perfect fifth**. But as just shown, $cb$ to $eb$ is also a **perfect fifth**; therefore, $cb$ to $fb$ is **two perfect fifths** <note>original: "bis diapente"</note>. Yet, according to the fourteenth theorem, a consonance cannot be composed of two perfect fifths <note>In this system, 3:2 squared results in 9:4, which was not classified as a primary consonance.</note>; therefore, $cb$ is **dissonant** relative to $fb$. 

Finally, as is known from the preceding, $ab$ to $gb$ sounds a **double octave**. If the **octave** $ab$ to $eb$ is subtracted, $eb$ to $gb$ remains as an **octave**. But $cb$ to $eb$ was shown in the second part of this proof to be a **perfect fifth**. Therefore, adding the **perfect fifth** $cb$ to $eb$ to the **octave** $eb$ to $gb$ creates an **octave plus a fifth**. Thus, $cb$ and $gb$ sound an **octave plus a fifth**. This completes the entire proposition.

## ¶ With the consonances placed thus: the end of the **perfect fifth** relative to the end of the **octave** measures a **perfect fourth**; and relative to the end of the **octave plus a fifth**, it measures an **octave**. Relative to the end of the **double octave**, it is **harmonious** <note>original: "euphonus"</note> but is not yet a [standard] **consonance**.

<image-desc>A line diagram identical in structure to the previous one, showing divisions labeled a, c, d, e, f, g, b.</image-desc>

¶ Following the previous hypothesis: I say that the segment $db$ (the end of the **perfect fifth**) relative to $eb$ sounds a **perfect fourth**. Relative to $fb$, it is an **octave**. And relative to $gb$, it sounds a **perfect fourth plus an octave**. 

According to the second-to-last proof, $ab$ to $eb$ is an **octave**, and $ab$ to $db$ is a **perfect fifth**. Subtracting the **perfect fifth** $ab$ to $db$ from the **octave** $ab$ to $eb$, by the twenty-second theorem, leaves a **perfect fourth**. This remainder is $db$ and $eb$; therefore $db$ relative to $eb$ sounds a **perfect fourth**. 

And since it was shown previously that $eb$ to $fb$ is a **perfect fifth**, and now that $db$ to $eb$ is a **perfect fourth**, then by the twentieth theorem, the consonance $db$ to $fb$ (forged and joined from those two) will sound an **octave**. 

Again, since $eb$ to $gb$ was shown to be an **octave**, and $eb$ to $fb$ a **perfect fifth**, by the twenty-second theorem $fb$ to $gb$ is a **perfect fourth**. Since $db$ to $fb$ was just shown to be an **octave**, $db$ to $gb$ is an **octave plus a perfect fourth**. While this sound is **harmonious** <note>original: "euphona"</note>, the twenty-fourth theorem has shown it is not a technical **consonance**; thus the whole proposition is clearly demonstrated. From this, it is likewise known that the end of the **octave** relative to the end of the **octave plus a fifth** measures a **perfect fifth** (as $eb$ to $fb$), and relative to the end of the **double octave**, it sounds an **octave**.

## ¶ With the consonances thus arranged: to designate the numbers for the whole string and each of its sections.

| 24 | | 18 | | 16 | | 12 | | 8 | | 6 | |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| **a** | | **c** | | **d** | | **e** | | **f** | | **g** | | **b** |

¶ I multiply two, three, and four by each other <note>2 x 3 x 4 = 24</note>. The resulting product, which will have a half, a third, and a fourth part, I set as the number for the whole line, which I call the number $ab$. From this, I remove a fourth part <note>24 - 6 = 18</note>, and the remainder is $cb$. The first number (24) will be in a **sesquitertian** <term>Sesquitertian: A ratio of 4:3, where the larger number contains the smaller plus one-third of it.</term> ratio to this. Therefore, it is a **perfect fourth**. 

From the same number (24), I remove a third part <note>24 - 8 = 16</note>, and the remainder is $db$; thus $ab$ to $db$ will be **sesquialter** <term>Sesquialter: A ratio of 3:2, where the larger number contains the smaller plus one-half of it.</term>; therefore, it is a **perfect fifth**. 

Again, from $ab$ I take away half, and let the remainder be $eb$ <note>24 / 2 = 12</note>; $ab$ to $eb$ will be **double** <note>original: "duplus"</note>, resulting in an **octave**. 

I take only a third of the number $ab$ to be $fb$ <note>24 / 3 = 8</note>; therefore the relationship of $ab$ to $fb$ is **triple** <note>original: "tripla"</note>. Thus $ab$ and $fb$ will contain an **octave plus a fifth**. 

Finally, let $gb$ be only a fourth of $ab$ <note>24 / 4 = 6</note>; $ab$ to $gb$ will be **quadruple** <note>original: "quadruplus"</note>. Therefore $ab$ and $gb$ are the numbers for a **double octave**. These are the numbers designated for the whole string $ab$ and each of its sections according to the assigned consonances. This was to be demonstrated.
