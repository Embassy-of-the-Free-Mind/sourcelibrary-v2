¶ Let **a, b** be the smallest numbers of the minor semitone [[notes: 256 and 243]]. I multiply **a** by itself and by **b**, and **b** by itself, and there arise **c, d, e**: and **c, d** and **d, e** will be two joined minor semitones. Furthermore, let **f** be the number eight, which from the eighteenth [proposition] of this book is the first [ratio] to **b**. Wherefore, by the eleventh [proposition] of the third [book] of *Arithmetics*, it is also prime to **e**. Therefore, **e** does not have an eighth part. I therefore multiply **f** (the eight) by **c, d, e**: and there result **g, h, i**: so that **i**, namely, arises from **f** times **e**. It is also certain that between **g, h** and **h, i** there are two minor semitones. And because **e** is the eighth part of **i**, I add **e** and **i** together, and let their sum be **lz** [[notes: 531441]]. There is no doubt that the ratio of **lz** to **i** is a *sesquioctava* [[notes: 9:8]]: and **lz** to **g** is the proportion by which the *sesquioctava* is greater than the two minor semitones **g, h** and **h, i**. Therefore, **lz** and **g** are the numbers of the comma. But that they are the smallest is known from the fifteenth [proposition] of the third [book] of *Arithmetics*. For by subtracting—as that very rule teaches—**g** from **lz**, and subtracting that which is left from **g** as much as possible, and so on in turn, finally, as appears in the addition at the side, a remainder of one is left [[notes: This refers to the Euclidean algorithm shown in the margin; a remainder of 1 proves the numbers are "prime to each other" and the ratio cannot be simplified]]. And just as the comma is placed before two *dieses* [[notes: Here meaning small intervals or semitones]], so also it is very easy to place a comma after two *dieses*, or to insert it between two.

¶ For let **a, b** be the smallest numbers of the comma found through the preceding [demonstration]. I extract the seventy-fourth part of **b** through the tenth [proposition] of this book, which shall be **c**. I then add **b** and **c** together, and let them combine into the sum **d**. If this **d** is compared to **b**, it is found to be a *sesquiseptuagesimaquarta* [[notes: The ratio 75:74]]. Yet this same **d** is found to be less than **a**. Therefore, by the eleventh [proposition] of this book, the ratio of the comma exceeds the seventy-fourth proportion.

### ¶ The ratio of the Comma: is less than the *sesquiseptuagesimatertia* proportion. Whence it happens that the ratio of the comma is found to be situated between the seventy-fourth and seventy-third proportions.

¶ Let **a, b** be the smallest numbers of the comma as before. I take the seventy-third part of **b**, which shall be **c**. I join **b** and **c** together, and let them be summed as **d**. Thus **d** to **b** will be a *sesquiseptuagesimatertia* [[notes: The ratio 74:73]]. And **d** is seen to be greater than **a**. Therefore, the ratio of the comma is less than the seventy-third proportion: which was to be demonstrated. The corollary is known as elsewhere.

<margin>31</margin>
### ¶ The ratio of the Comma: consists in a *superpartient* ratio.

¶ For it does not consist in a *superparticular* [[notes: A ratio of the form (n+1)/n, like 3:2 or 16:15]] ratio, since two nearest superparticulars—the seventy-fourth and the seventy-third—exclude every other superparticular in between them. So much the less can it consist in the *multiple* genus [[notes: Ratios like 2:1 or 3:1]], as it has seventy-two superparticulars larger than itself. It remains, therefore, that it can consist in the *superpartient* genus [[notes: A ratio where the numerator is larger than the denominator by more than 1, e.g., 5:3]]. For that is the relationship of the greater to the lesser.
