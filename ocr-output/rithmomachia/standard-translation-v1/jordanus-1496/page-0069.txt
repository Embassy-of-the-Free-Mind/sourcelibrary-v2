### 45 ¶ If two different superparticulars are joined together, the compound will be either double, superparticular, or superpartient.
Let **a**, **b**, **c**, **d**, **e**, **f** be any number of superparticulars in order: I say that if any two are joined together [[notes: "joining" ratios in this context means multiplying them, such as 3/2 × 4/3]], the resulting compound is either double, superparticular, or superpartient. For if you join **a** and **b**—a sesquialter [[notes: 3:2]] and a sesquitertial [[notes: 4:3]]—the compound will be double [[notes: 3/2 × 4/3 = 12/6 = 2/1]]. Once the double is established, you find by the first [proposition] of the fifth [book] the sesquitertial ratio by which the double exceeds the sesquialter. Thus, the first two constitute a double. But if you join **a** with **c**, **d**, **e**, or **f**: because by the thirty-seventh [proposition] of this book any of these is smaller than **b**, the composite ratio will be less than double and therefore not a multiple. For by that same thirty-seventh proposition, the double is the smallest of the multiples. Therefore, the composite ratio will be either superparticular or superpartient: which is what was proposed.

### 46 ¶ To find a superparticular ratio such that when combined with any number of other superparticulars, it forms a superparticular.
Let **a**, **b**, **c** be any number of proportional numbers starting from unity. Let **d** be prime to **a**, which by the twenty-seventh of the fourth will also be prime to **b** and **c**. Let **d** be joined to any of them to produce the compounds **e**, **f**, **g**. By the ninth of the third, each of these will be prime to every other in that proportional series. I then take the next proportional following the first, which is **h**, and by the twenty-fifth of the third, I find the least number that **d**, **e**, **f**, **g** measure, which is **k**; by the twenty-seventh of the third, this will be prime to **h**. Then, by the last of the third, let **l** be a multiple of **h** and **m** a multiple of **k**; and let **l** exceed **m** by the amount of **d**. Then I add the numbers **a**, **b**, **c** individually to **l** to produce the compounds **n**, **p**, **q**. It is manifest that **l** is superparticular to **m**, for it adds to it its own part, namely **d**. But **n** also adds to it **d** and **a** (which is **e**); **p** adds **d** and **b** (which is **f**); and **q** adds **d** and **c** (which is **g**). By the twenty-third of the first, each of **e**, **f**, **g** is a part of **m**, because each is a part of **k**. Therefore, **n**, **p**, **q**, and likewise **l**, are superparticulars to **m**. Thus the proposition is established.

### 47 ¶ Given a superparticular: to find any number of others which, when combined with it, produce a superpartient ratio.
Let **a** be the denominator of the part of the given superparticular. I take proportional numbers starting from unity, of which **a** is one: let these be **a**, **b**, **c**. I say that the superparticular ratio designated by **a**, when joined with the superparticular whose part is designated by **b** or **c**, constitutes a superpartient ratio, as the present finding proposes. For the composite of the superparticular ratio designated by **a** and that designated by **b** has in its denomination a unit and the parts called by **a** and **b**, per the eighth [proposition] of this book. These parts, per the fourth of this book, are denominated by the product of **a** and **b**, which is **d**. But by the thirteenth of this book, those parts do not constitute a single part [[notes: they don't simplify into a 1/n fraction]]. Therefore, since the composite ratio (by the demonstration of the penultimate [proposition]) is neither multiple nor superparticular, because the composite parts do not constitute one single part, it remains that the composite is superpartient: and so for any other.

### 48 ¶ Every superparticular combined with any superpartient constitutes a ratio less than triple.
For a double combined with a sesquialter [[notes: 3:2]]—which by the thirty-seventh [proposition] of this book is the largest of the superparticulars—produces a triple ratio according to the corollary of the twenty-third of this book. But every superpartient ratio is less than a double ratio [[notes: because a superpartient is between 1 and 2]]; therefore, any superpartient ratio joined with the largest superparticular (or a smaller one) will, by the fifth [proposition] of the fifth, constitute a ratio less than triple: which is what was proposed.

### 49 ¶ Given a ratio that is less than triple: to divide it into a superparticular and a superpartient.
First, let any superparticular be given which is sought to be divided in this way. I take **a** and **b** as the least terms [[notes: the simplest form of the ratio]], **a** being the smaller and **b** the larger between which it exists. By the thirty-seventh of this book, these will be numbers taken in a continuous series, differing only by unity. Let **c** be composed of them, to which **a** is added to produce **d**. Let **e** be one greater than this, and let **a** and **b** be multiplied by **d** to produce **f** and **g**. By the eighth of the second, the ratio of **g** to **f** is the same as **b** to **a**. Next, I multiply **a** by **e** to produce **h**. Then **h** exceeds **f** by the ninth of the first by the amount of **a**, and **a** is a part of **f**. Since **a** is set to measure **f** according to **d**, the ratio of **h** to **f** is superparticular. Also, because **b** is one greater than **a**, by the tenth of the first, **g** adds over **f** the amount of **d**. Therefore **g** adds over **h** the amount of **d** minus **a**; but subtracting **a** from **d** leaves **c**. Thus **g** adds over **h** the amount of **c**, and by the ninth and tenth of the third, **c** is prime to **g**. The ratio of **g** to **h** will therefore be a superpartient ratio. Thus the assigned superparticular ratio, which is **g** to **f**, is divided into two: namely **h** to **f** (superparticular) and **g** to **h** (superpartient). This completes the first part of the proposal.
