[[meta: This page continues the ninth book of Jordanus de Nemore's Arithmetica. Having established the basic rules for multiplying fractions on the previous page, Jordanus now explores how the ratios of whole numbers relate to the ratios of their sums and parts. He uses the classical Boethian terminology for ratios: multiple, superparticular, and superpartient.]]

**VIII**

¶ Let *a*, *b*, *c*, and *d* be numbers in continuous proportion starting from unity [[notes: This means a geometric progression like 1, 2, 4, 8 or 1, 3, 9, 27]]. Let *e* and *f* be the parts denominated by any of them, such as *c* and *d*—that is, *e* from *c* and *f* from *d*. I say that *e* and *f* do not constitute a single part [[notes: A "single part" refers to a unit fraction (1/n). Jordanus is proving that the sum of these specific fractions will not result in another unit fraction]]. And let *g* be the sum of *a* and unity. Since *c* divides *d*, it is clear by the twenty-fifth [proposition] of the third [book] that *d* is the least number which *c* and *d* both divide. But by the twenty-fifth of the second, *c* divides *d* according to *a*, and *d* divides itself according to unity. And by the second [proposition] of this [book], *e* represents the reduced parts denominated by *d* and taken according to *a*. And *f* is the part denominated by *d* taken according to unity. Therefore, *e* and *f* together are the parts denominated by *d* taken according to *g*. Wherefore, by the penultimate corollary of this [book], *e* and *f* together are to one as *g* is to *d*. But *g* is not a part of *d*. For if *g* were a prime number and divided *d*, by the twenty-seventh of the fourth it would also divide the whole of *a* (that is, its own part), which is impossible. And if *g* is a composite number, then by the second of the third it will be divided by some prime which, by the twenty-third of the first, will divide *d*. Wherefore, by the twenty-seventh of the fourth, it will divide *a*; and it also divides the whole *g*. Therefore, by the twelfth of the first, it will divide the remainder: namely, the number unity, which is impossible. Therefore, *g* is not a part of *d*; wherefore neither is the sum *e* and *f* a part of one. Thus *e* and *f* do not constitute a single part. This is what was proposed.

[[margin: 14]] ¶ **Parts named by two numbers relate to the part named by the number composed of them, just as the square of the sum relates to the product of the two numbers.** [[notes: This proposition establishes a sophisticated relationship between fractions: $(1/a + 1/b) \div 1/(a+b) = (a+b)^2 / ab$.]]
¶ Let *a* and *b* be two numbers, and *c* be the sum of them. Let *d* be the square of *c*. Let *a* be multiplied by *b* to produce *e*. Let *f* be the part named by *c*, and let *g* be the part named by *a*, and *h* the part named by *b*. I say that as *d* is to *e*, so are *g* and *h* together to *f*. For I multiply *c* by *a* to produce *k*, and *c* by *b* to produce *l*. By the ninth of the first, *k* and *l* together will be equal to *d*. I then consider six numbers: *e*, *k*, *f*, *h*, *l*, *g*. I say that *e* is to *k* (the first to the second) as *f* is to *h* (the third to the fourth). Likewise, *e* is to *l* (the first to the fifth) as *f* is to *g* (the third to the sixth). For by the seventh of the second, *e* is to *k* as *b* is to *c*. But by the first of this [book], *b* is to *c* as *f* is to *h*. Therefore, *e* is to *k* as *f* is to *h*. Likewise, by the same seventh of the second, *e* is to *l* as *a* is to *c*. But by the first of this [book], *a* is to *c* as *f* is to *g*. Therefore, *e* is to *l* as *f* is to *g*. Therefore, by the twenty-third of the second, *e* (the first) is to *k* and *l* together (the second and fifth) as *f* (the third) is to *g* and *h* together (the fourth and sixth). But *k* and *l* together are *d*. Therefore, *d* is to *e* as *g* and *h* together are to *f*. This is what was proposed.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A semi-circle diagram for Proposition 15, showing a peak of 12 with a base divided into 3 and 9, labeled 'a' and 'b'.]]]]
[[margin: 15]] ¶ **If two numbers are added together and the sum has a "multiple" ratio to one of them, the other number will be equal to it or a multiple of it.** [[notes: A "multiple" ratio is an integer ratio like 2:1, 3:1, etc. If $(a+b)/a = n$, then $b/a = n-1$.]]
¶ Let number *ab* be composed of two parts, *a* and *b*. Let the sum *ab* be a multiple of *a*. I say *b* is either equal to *a* or a multiple of it. For since *ab* is a multiple of *a*, *a* divides the whole *ab*; and it also divides itself when subtracted. Therefore, by the twelfth of the first, it divides the remainder *b*. If *a* divides *b* once, *a* will be equal to *b*. But if *a* divides *b* many times, then by definition *b* is a multiple of *a*. This is what was proposed.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A semi-circle for Propositions 16 and 17, with 10 at the peak and a base divided into 2 and 8, labeled 'a' and 'b'.]]]]
[[margin: 16]] ¶ **If the ratio of the whole to one part is "superparticular," the ratio of that part to the other will be a "multiple" ratio of the same denomination.** [[notes: A "superparticular" ratio is $(n+1):n$. For example, $3:2$ (sesquialter). This proposition says if $(a+b):b = 3:2$, then $b:a = 2:1$ (a multiple).]]
¶ A ratio is called "entirely superparticular" or "entirely multiple" according to its rank. For example, the first superparticular is *sesquialter* (3:2), followed in order by *sesquitertius* (4:3), *sesquiquartus* (5:4), and so on according to the series of numbers. And the first multiple is *double* (2:1), followed by *triple* (3:1), *quadruple* (4:1), *quintuple* (5:1), and so on. Let *ab* be the whole number composed of *a* and *b*, and let *ab* be superparticular to *b*. I say *b* is a multiple of the same denomination to *a*. For since the sum *ab* is superparticular to *b*, it adds the number *a* to *b*. Therefore, by definition, *a* is some [unit] part of *b*. Now, however many times *a* is a part of *b*, the whole *ab* is that same degree of superparticular to *b*. And however many times *a* is a part of *b*, the whole *b* will be that multiple of *a*. Therefore, by the definitions, whatever the superparticular ratio of *ab* to *b* is, the ratio of *b* to *a* will be a multiple of the same denomination. This is what was proposed.

[[margin: 17]] ¶ **If the ratio of one part to the other is a "multiple," the ratio of the whole to that part will be "superparticular."**
¶ This is the converse of the preceding. Let the ratio of *b* to *a* be a multiple. I say the ratio of the sum *ab* to *b* is entirely superparticular. For since *b* is a multiple of *a*, then *a* will be, by definition, some part of *b*. But *ab* adds exactly that part to *b*, for it adds *a*. Therefore, whatever the multiple ratio of *b* to *a* is, the ratio of *ab* to *b* will be superparticular of the same denomination. This is intended.

[[margin: [[image-desc: A diagram for Proposition 18 showing a horizontal line of 18, a semi-circle labeled 10 above a base of 2 and 8 (a and b), and a segment 'd' with a value of 8.]]]]
[[margin: 18]] ¶ **If the ratio of a greater to a lesser is "superparticular" or "multiple superparticular," the ratio of their sum to the lesser will be "multiple superparticular," and to the greater it will be "superpartient."** [[notes: "Multiple superparticular" means $n + 1/m$ (e.g., $2 \frac{1}{2}$). "Superpartient" means $1 + m/n$ where $m > 1$ (e.g., $1 \frac{2}{3}$).]]
¶ Let *a* be the greater number and *b* the lesser. Let the ratio of *a* to *b* be superparticular. Let *c* be the sum of *a* and *b*. I say the ratio of *c* to *b* is multiple superparticular, and the ratio of *c* to *a* is superpartient. Taking from the greater number *a* a part equal to *b* (let it be *d*), let the remainder be *e*. Since *a* is superparticular to *b*, it is clear that *a* exceeds *b* by the amount *e*, and *e* is a part of *b*. Since *c* is composed of *e*, *d*, and *b*, and *d* and *b* together are double *b*, *c* exceeds double *b* by the amount *e*. Therefore, *c* contains *b* twice plus that whole part which is *e*. It is therefore, by definition, a multiple superparticular ratio of *c* to *b*. This is the first part. But it is also shown that *c* to *a* is superpartient. Since *b* is less than *a* and is not a [unit] part of *a* (otherwise the ratio of *a* to *b* would be multiple, which contradicts the hypothesis), then by the first [proposition] of the first [book], *b* is "parts" of *a* [[notes: Meaning a non-unit fraction, $m/n$]]. But *c* exceeds *a* by the amount *b*. Therefore, *c* contains *a* plus those parts; thus the ratio of *c* to *a* is superpartient. This is what was proposed. And if the ratio of the greater to the lesser was multiple superparticular, the same demonstration holds for the sum to the lesser being multiple superparticular and to the greater being superpartient.

[[margin: 19]] ¶ **If the ratio of the greater to the lesser is "superpartient" or "multiple superpartient," the ratio of the sum to the greater will be "superpartient," and to the lesser "multiple superpartient."**

[[summary: This page explores how adding two numbers affects their mathematical ratios. Jordanus demonstrates that if two numbers have a simple ratio (like a multiple or a unit fraction), their sum will have a predictable, slightly more complex ratio (like a superparticular or superpartient ratio) relative to the original parts.]]
