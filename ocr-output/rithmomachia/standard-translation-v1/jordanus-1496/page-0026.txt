[[meta: This page continues the discussion of Book IV of Jordanus de Nemore's Arithmetica, focusing on Proposition 7 and moving through to Proposition 12. The text explores the relationship between "continuous proportions" (geometric progressions) and the unit (the number 1). It relies heavily on the concept of numbers being "least in their proportion," which we would call a ratio in its simplest form.]]

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be numbers in continuous proportion [[notes: A "continuous proportion" is a geometric progression, such as 8, 12, 18, 27, where each term is related to the next by the same ratio (here, 2:3)]] and let them be the least in their proportion: I say that if there were mean proportionals between **a** and **d**, there would be just as many mean proportionals between **a** and unity, and just as many between **d** and unity. For because, by the first [proposition] of this book, any two adjacent numbers will be commensurable, I take the greater ones that measure them two by two, which shall be **e**, **f**, and **g**; these, by the second corollary of the second [proposition] of this book, will be in continuous proportion. And I take the greater of those that measure those two by two, which shall be **h** and **l**. These **h** and **l** will be as **a** to **b** by the 23rd [proposition] of the third [book], measuring **e**, **f** and **f**, **g**, and **k**, **m** [as] the least in that proportion. But since **e** measures **a**, and **g** measures **d**, and **a** and **d** are prime to each other by the fifth [proposition] of this book, therefore **e** and **g** are prime to each other by the eighth of the third. By the same reasoning, **h** and **l** are prime to each other, and therefore are the least in their proportion. Therefore, by the corollary of the 23rd of the third, **e** measures **a** according to **h**, and **h** measures **e** according to **h**. Likewise, **g** measures **d** according to **k** and **l** [[notes: The manuscript here uses "k" and "l" to denote the factors of the higher powers]]. Therefore, as many times as unity is in **h**, so many times is **h** in **e**, and **e** in **a**. Similarly, as many times as unity is in **l**, so many times is **l** in **g**, and **g** in **d**. Therefore, unity, **h**, **e**, and **a** are in continuous proportion; likewise, unity, **l**, **g**, and **d** are in continuous proportion. Wherefore, just as between the extremes **a** and **d** there are contained two mean proportionals **b** and **c**, so between **a** and unity there are contained two [means] **e** and **h**, and between **d** and unity likewise two means **g** and **l**: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If between each of two numbers and unity an equal number of proportional numbers fall: it is necessary that just as many fall between [the two numbers] themselves. [[margin: 8]]

¶ This is the converse of the preceding [proposition]. As if between unity and **a** there fall **h** and **e**, two numbers in continuous proportion, and between unity and **d** there fall **l** and **g**: I say that just as many mean proportionals fall between **a** and **d**. For since as many times as unity is in **h**, so many times is **h** in **e**, therefore **h** multiplied by itself will produce **e**. By the same [reasoning], **l** multiplied by itself will produce **g**, and **h** and **l** multiplied into **e** and **g** produce **a** and **d**. I multiply **h** into **l** [multiplied by itself] and let it become **f**; it will be manifest by the seventh and eighth [propositions] of the second [book] that **e**, **f**, and **g** are in continuous proportion as **h** is to **l**. Then I multiply **h** into **f** and let it become **b**, and **l** into **f** and let it become **c**. It will be established by the seventh of the second that as **a** is to **b**, so is **b** to **c**, and **c** to **d**. Thus two numbers, **b** and **c**, will fall as mean proportionals between **a** and **d**, just as they fell between unity and **a**, and between unity and **d**: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If there are any number of numbers in continuous proportion: the least [numbers] which measure them two by two will be proportional in that same proportion. [[margin: 9]]

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be numbers in continuous proportion, and let the least numbers which measure them two by two be **e**, **f**, and **g**. I say that **e**, **f**, and **g** are in continuous proportion. For I take **h** and **l** as the least in that proportion; by the 24th of the third, **a**, **b**, and **c** measure **e**, **f**, **g**, **k**, **m**, and **l**. And **b**, **c**, and **d** measure the same according to **h**. Therefore, by the seventh of the first, **e**, **f**, and **g** are in continuous proportion: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ To take numbers of different proportions, from which the least numbers measured two by two are in continuous proportion. [[margin: 10]]

¶ I wish to take numbers of different proportions from which the least numbers measured two by two are proportional. I take numbers **a** and **b** that are prime to each other, with **a** being the greater and **b** the smaller, whose sum is **c**, which is prime to both by the ninth of the third. I multiply **b** by itself to produce **d**, and **b** by **a** to produce **e**, and **a** by **c** to produce **f**, and **c** by itself to produce **g**. By the seventh of the first, because the proportion of **a** to **b** is that of **e** to **f**, and since **c** is greater than **a**, therefore by the ninth of the second, the proportion of **f** to **e** is greater than **e** to **d**, and **g** to **f** is as **c** to **a**, which is not as **b** to **a**. For [otherwise] **c** and **b** would be equal, nor is it as **a** to **b**. For by the first [proposition] of this book, if there were a continuous proportionality of **c** to **a** and **a** to **b**, then **c** and **a** would share a common factor [[notes: "Communicarent" means they would not be coprime]]. Thus the proportion of **g** to **f** is different from the proportion of **f** to **e** and **e** to **d**. I take, therefore, the least [multiple] which **d** and **e** measure, which is **h**, and the least which **e** and **f** measure, which is **l2** [[notes: The manuscript uses "l" with a superscript or variation to distinguish terms]], and the least which **f** and **g** measure, which is **l**. I say that **h**, **l2**, and **l** are in continuous proportion. For by the 24th of the third, **b** in **e** makes **h**. And **f** is to **e** as **c** is to **b**, and **c** and **b** are the least in their proportion; therefore by the same 24th, **b** in **f** produces **l2**. Therefore **l2** is to **h** as **f** is to **e**, which is as **c** to **b**. And because by the same [reasoning] **c** leads into **f** to produce **l**. For **f** is smaller and **g** is larger; therefore by the eighth of the second, **l** will be to **l2** as **g** is to **b**. For **b** in **f** makes **l2** and **c** in **f** makes **l**. Therefore **l**, **l2**, and **h** are the least continuous proportionals measured from two and two numbers of different proportions: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If between two numbers any [number of terms] are taken in continuous proportionality: between each of them and the least number which measures all of them, it is necessary to assign just as many. [[margin: 11]]

¶ Let **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** be numbers in continuous proportion, of which by the 25th of the third, **e** is found to be the least [multiple] they all measure [[notes: The Least Common Multiple or LCM]]. I say that just as many numbers in continuous proportion fall between **a** and **e**, and between **d** and **e**. Let **f**, **g**, and **h** be the least numbers which **a**, **b**; **b**, **c**; and **c**, **d** measure two by two. These **f**, **g**, and **h**, by the ninth of this book, will be in the same continuous proportion as **a** to **b**. Likewise, let the least numbers which **f**, **g** and **g**, **h** measure two by two be **l2** and **l**. I say that **l2** is the least number which **a**, **b**, and **c** measure. For if there were a smaller one, by the corollary of the 23rd of the third, **f** and **g** would measure it, wherefore by the same corollary **l2** would be the least measured by them: the greater by the smaller, which is impossible. And by the same argument, **l** is the least that **b**, **c**, and **d** measure, and from the same it is shown that the least which **l2** and **l** measure is the least which **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** measure. But the least which **a**, **b**, **c**, and **d** measure was set as **e**. Therefore **e** is the least which **l2** and **l** measure. Furthermore, let **m** and **n** be the least terms of the proportion **a** to **b**; therefore by the 24th of the third, **a** measures **f** and **l2**, and **l2**, **e**, **f**, **m**, and **n**. And by the same, **d** measures **h**, **l**, and **l**, **e** according to **m**. Wherefore by the eighth of the second, **a**, **f**, **l2**, and **e** are in continuous proportion. Likewise, **d**, **h**, **l**, and **e**. And just as between **a** and **e** there are **b** and **c**, two mean proportionals; so between **a** and **e** there are two, **f** and **l2**, and between **d** and **e** two, **h** and **l**, likewise mean proportionals: which is what was proposed.

### ¶ If the first is to the second as the third is to the fourth: however many [means] consist between the first and second, just as many must consist proportionally as means between the third and fourth. [[margin: 12]]

¶ Let **a** be to **d** as **e** is to **h**; and between **a** and **d** let there be two mean proportionals **b** and **c**. I say that also between **e** and **h** there are two mean proportionals. I take four least [terms] of that proportion, which are **k**, **l**, **m**, and **n**; by the 21st of the second and equal proportionality, **k** is to **n** as **a** is to **d**, wherefore also as **e** is to **h**. And because by the fifth of the present [book], **k** and **n** are prime to each other, they will be, by the 20th of the third, the least in their proportion; and by the 19th of the same, the least **k** and **n** will measure **e** and **h** equally. Therefore, let them measure the numbers **f** and **g** according to the same number. And by the seventh of the second, **e**, **f**, **g**, and **h** will be in continuous proportion. There are therefore likewise two mean proportionals **f** and **g** between **e** and **h**, just as there were two **b** and **c** between **a** and **d**: which is what was proposed.

[[summary: This page establishes the relationship between geometric progressions and their least common multiples, proving that the number of geometric means between two terms is preserved when comparing them to their common multiples or between equivalent ratios.]]
