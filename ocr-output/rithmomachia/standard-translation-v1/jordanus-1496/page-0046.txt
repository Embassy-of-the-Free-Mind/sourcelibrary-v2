## ¶ 27. If even numbers are summed together starting from unity, the total will be an oblong number.

¶ I arrange the even numbers starting from unity: **a, b, c, d, e, f**, and as many as I wish. I say that **a** and **b** together are **oblong** [[notes: Latin: *altera parte longior*, meaning 'longer by the other part.' In Greek and Latin mathematics, this refers to a number of the form $n(n+1)$, which can be represented as a rectangle where one side is one unit longer than the other]], and likewise **a, b,** and **c**; and **a, b, c,** and **d**, and so on. For I divide them into equals: one set of halves I arrange here, and the others there. And because by the eighteenth [proposition] of this [book] these were doubles of the numbers proceeding in order from unity, those ranks proceed from unity according to the natural series of numbers. Therefore, by the thirty-fourth [proposition] of the third [book], the first two halves and the second two joined together constitute an oblong number. But these equal **a** and **b** together. And by the same thirty-fourth [proposition], the third two halves with the previously placed halves constitute an oblong number, and these equal **a, b,** and **c**; and so on. Thus the proposition stands.

## ¶ 28. If odd numbers are arranged from unity, and the first two after unity are joined, and after those three, then four, and so on according to the natural increase of numbers: those produced will be cubes. The side of each cube will be the count of the joined odd numbers.

¶ Let the odd numbers be arranged from unity: first, unity itself, which is a cube, and unity is its side. Then the first two odd numbers as **a** [[notes: 3 + 5 = 8, which is 2 cubed]]. After which the three following as **b** [[notes: 7 + 9 + 11 = 27, which is 3 cubed]]. After which four as **c** [[notes: 13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = 64, which is 4 cubed]]. Then five, then six, and so on according to the natural increase of numbers. I say that the first two aggregated make a cube whose side is the number of odd numbers (that is, two). Likewise, the three others aggregated constitute a cube whose side is the number of those joined odd numbers (that is, three), and so on. I take first some number according to which the odd numbers are taken to constitute the cube; let it first be an even number like **c**, whose square is **g**. Since by perception **c** as a whole is as many units from unity as there are units in itself, it will be that it and unity and every number below it, like **a** and **b**, are as many numbers as there are units in it. And since the numbers are taken according to the natural increase of numbers, that whole will have as many even numbers as there are odd numbers below it (for I call unity an odd number). Therefore, there are as many odd numbers below it as there are units in its half. 

Let two ranks be arranged from unity, one ending in **c** and the other in **b**, where **b** is less than **c** by unity. Because by the thirty-third [proposition] of the third [book] those aggregated together constitute a square whose side is **c** (which square is set as **g**), and since **c** is even, then by the tenth [proposition] of this [book], **g** (its square) is also even. From this it also happens that half of **c** with **b** and those below **b** are as half of the square **g**. Thus, there will be as many odd numbers up to **g** as there are units in **b** and its inferiors and the half. I likewise take as many odd numbers beyond **g** as there are units in the remaining half of **c**; let the last be **h**. Therefore, **h** will be the total of odd numbers from unity as there are units in **c** and those below it. But it was manifest that the last of the previously taken odd numbers, like **k**, was the total from unity. Therefore, **k** is the same as **h**. And since half of the odd numbers taken for themselves are taken above **g**, the other half will be located below **g**. And because **g**, by the second [proposition] of the first [book], is the mean of the two first surrounding numbers (as in the figure of **l** and **m**), and by the same, the mean of the two others (as **h** and **n**), and so on for however many there are. Therefore, with the help of the eleventh [proposition] of the first [book], it will happen that the number aggregated from those odd numbers is as the number produced from the count of **c** odd numbers into **g**. Therefore, the product of those odd numbers will be a cube, and the count of the odd numbers will be its side. This is the proposition as far as this point.

Secondly, let **b** be an odd number of some odd numbers from which a cube is to be constituted, and let its square be **f**. Because **b** is odd, therefore by the eleventh [proposition] of this [book], its square **f** is odd. I divide **f** into two unequal portions differing by unity by the nineteenth [proposition] of this [book], of which the greater is **p** and the smaller **q**. Because the odd number **f** overflows the series of numbers without an even one, it follows that as many times as unity is in **p**, that is the total of odd numbers for **f** from unity; and as many times as unity is in **q**, there are that many even numbers below it. I thus establish two ranks: the first from unity to **b**, and the second from unity to **a**, where **b** is greater than **a** by unity. And I understand **b** to be divided into two parts differing by unity, since it is odd, of which the greater is **r** and the smaller **s**. By the thirty-third [proposition] of the third [book], those aggregated constitute a square whose side is **b**. But that square is set as **f**. Therefore, with the help of the same, **a** with its inferiors and **r** (the larger portion of **b**) contain as many units as **r** is contained in **p**. There are therefore as many odd numbers above **f** as there are units in **s**. Let the last be **t**. For because **f** was the total of odd numbers from unity as there are units in **a**, its inferiors, and in **r**, therefore **t** is the total of odd numbers from unity as there are units in **b** and all its inferiors. But the last of the taken odd numbers was also the total from unity. Therefore **t** is the last of them. Of the number **b**, there are as many assigned odd numbers above **f** as below; thus **f** will be their middle. Therefore, all of them (as before with **p**) being numbered, they will produce as much as the count of them all—that is, multiplied by **b**. And since **b** produced into itself is **f**, it is manifest that **b** produced into **f** is a cube, whose side is that number **b** of assigned odd numbers. Thus the whole proposition stands.
