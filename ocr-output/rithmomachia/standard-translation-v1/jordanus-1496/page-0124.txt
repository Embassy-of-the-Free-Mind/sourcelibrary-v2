¶ In the **Diatonic tetrachords**, from the **hypate hypaton** (the first boundary of the consonances) up to the fourth boundary, three **diatessaron** [[notes: The interval of a perfect fourth]] are contained, but only once among the stable and immobile notes. And up to the fifth boundary, likewise three **diapente** [[notes: The interval of a perfect fifth]]: once among the mobile, once among the partly variable, and once among those entirely invariable and firm. Up to the eighth boundary, the **diapason** [[notes: The interval of an octave]] will sound seven times: three times among the immobile notes and four times among the mobile.

¶ When it is said "from the first boundary to the fourth, fifth, and eighth boundary," we understand that the first boundary is not excluded, but the fourth, fifth, and eighth are. Let **b c d e f** up to **p** be the four tetrachords, with **b** being the **hypate hypaton** and the others following in order. I say, first, that from **b** to **e**, a **diatessaron** is contained three times, but only once among the immobile notes. For by the fourth rule of this book, **b** to **c** contains a minor semitone, **c** to **d** a tone, and **d** to **e** a tone. Therefore, **b** to **e** sounds a **diatessaron**; and this will be found to be the first **diatessaron** consonance. And since it was shown that **c** to **d** and **d** to **e** are two tones, and by the fifth rule **e** to **f** is a minor semitone, then **c** to **f** sounds a **diatessaron**. This will be the second **diatessaron**. Likewise, it was shown that **d** to **e** is a tone and **e** to **f** a minor semitone; and by the fifth rule **f** to **g** is a tone. Therefore **d** to **g** is a **diatessaron**, and this will be the third. But since the first of these three, which is **b** to **e**, is from the **hypate hypaton** to the **hypate meson**—which the preceding sections showed to be stable and immobile—and the second is from **c** to **f** (**parhypate hypaton** to **parhypate meson**), and the third is from **d** to **g** (**lichanos hypaton** to **lichanon meson**). [I say] that the **diapente** is only contained three times. For by the thirteenth rule, a **diapente** consists of three tones and a minor semitone. But by the fourth and fifth rules, **b** to **f** contains only two tones and two minor semitones; these two minor semitones, according to rule 24, are less than one tone by a **comma**. Thus, for **b** to **f** to complete the **diapente** consonance, a full **apotome** [[notes: An apotome is the "major semitone," the difference between a whole tone and a minor semitone]] is lacking. Therefore, **b** to **f** does not constitute a **diapente**. Furthermore, **c** to **g** by the fourth and fifth rules contains three tones—which are **c** to **d**, **d** to **e**, and **f** to **g**—and one minor semitone, which is **e** to **f**. Therefore, by the thirteenth rule, **c** to **g** sounds a **diapente**. This will be the first **diapente**. And by the same rules, **d** to **h** contains three tones—which are **d** to **e**, **f** to **g**, and **g** to **h**—and the minor semitone **e** to **f**. Therefore, **d** to **h** sounds a **diapente**; this will be the second **diapente**. And from **e** to **i**, by the fourth and sixth rules, three tones and a minor semitone are similarly contained. Therefore, **e** to **i** will be the third **diapente**. Thus, from **b** to **f**, the **diapente** is contained three times. But since the first is from **c** (**parhypate hypaton**) to **g** (**lichanos meson**), and the nineteenth rule showed the **parhypates** and **lichanas** to be mobile, the first **diapente** from **c** to **g** is established among the mobile notes. And since the second is from **d** (**lichanos principalis** [[notes: Another name for the lichanos hypaton]]) to **h** (**mese**), and the **lichanos** is mobile while the **mese** is immobile, the second **diapente** exists as partly variable. And since the third is from **e** (**hypate meson**) to **i** (**paramese**), which were shown to be stable by that same nineteenth rule, the third **diapente** will be entirely stable and firm.

¶ Thirdly, I say that from **b** (**hypate hypaton**) to **i** (**paramese**), the **diapason** is contained seven times: but only three times among the immobile notes, and four times among the mobile. For **b** to **i**, by the fourth, fifth, and sixth rules, contains five tones and two minor semitones. Therefore, by the twenty-first rule, **b** to **i** sounds a **diapason**, and it will be the first **diapason**. And by the same logic, **c** to **k**, **d** to **l**, and **e** to **m** each intercept five tones and two minor semitones; therefore **c** to **k** will be the second **diapason**, **d** to **l** the third, and **e** to **m** the fourth. But by the fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh rules together, **f** to **n** will similarly contain five tones and two minor semitones. Likewise **g** to **o** and **h** to **p**. Thus **f** to **n** will be the fifth **diapason**, **g** to **o** the sixth, and **h** to **p** the seventh. Therefore, from **b** to **i** and beyond, the **diapason** is contained seven times. However, the first is provided by the **hypate hypaton** and **paramese diezeugmenon**; the fourth by the **hypate meson** and **nete diezeugmenon**; and the seventh by the **mese** and **nete hyperboleon**. Now, the eighteenth rule showed the **hypates**, **meses**, **parameses**, and **netas** to be immobile and stable. Therefore, among those seven **diapason** consonances, the **diapason** is found three times among the immobile notes. But the second is provided by the **parhypate hypaton** and **trite diezeugmenon**; the third by the **lichanos hypaton** and **paranete diezeugmenon**; the fifth by the **parhypate meson** and **trite hyperboleon**; and the sixth by the **lichanos meson** and **paranete hyperboleon**. But the nineteenth rule showed the **parhypates**, **tritas**, **lichanas**, and **paranetas** to be mobile. Therefore, among those seven instances of the **diapason** consonance, it is found to occur four times among the mobile and changeable notes; which was the whole point of the proposition. 

¶ As for the fact that the divine **Severinus** [[notes: Referencing Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 480–524 AD), the most influential musical authority for medieval and Renaissance scholars]]—the first among those followers of the disciplines of the Latin wise men, whom we imitate in this work as much as we are able, being not a little aided by his teachings—makes the species of the **diapente** four in number in the thirteenth chapter of the fourth book of his *Music*: let that be considered to have been done for introductory purposes, where he did not concern himself with the precise truth, but followed common estimation there. This is easily recognized from his own earlier determination. Here, however, is a place not for an introduction, but for exact determination.
