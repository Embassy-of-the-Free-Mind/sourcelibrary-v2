[[margin: 11]]
### If two unequal numbers are compared to the same smaller number: the ratio of the larger to it is greater, and the ratio of the smaller to it is smaller.

| 9 | 8 | 7 |
|---|---|---|
| a | b | c |

Let **a** and **b** be two unequal numbers of which **a** is greater and **b** is smaller; let them be compared to the same smaller number **c**. I say that the ratio of **a** to **c** is greater than **b** to **c**. For since, by the eighth postulate of extremes, the ratio of **a** to **c** is composed of the ratios of **a** to **b** and **b** to **c** as its parts [[notes: In early music theory, ratios were "added" by multiplying them: $(a/b) \times (b/c) = a/c$]]: therefore the ratio **b** to **c** is a part of the ratio **a** to **c**, and the ratio **a** to **c** is the whole. And since, by the last common axiom, *every whole is greater than its part*: therefore the ratio **a** to **c** is greater than the ratio **b** to **c**. And so it is to be done concerning any others by a similar argument.

### The sesquisextadecima ratio exceeds a whole half-tone.

Because the middle of this has shown the space of the extremes of a tone divided into two equal parts: to divide a tone minimally into two equal parts geometrically, and to retain the **sesquiseptimadecima** [[notes: 18:17 ratio]] and **sesquisextadecima** [[notes: 17:16 ratio]] sections on this side and that on the other. This indeed shows that the **sesquisextadecima** ratio, which is left there on the side of the tension and sharpness [[notes: The higher-pitched side of the interval]], is greater than a hemitone and a whole half-tone. And the following will show that the **sesquiseptimadecima**, taken from the heavier [[notes: lower-pitched]] part, is smaller than the same half-tone. Let it be, therefore, as in the fifth [proposition] of this, the space **a b** divided into eighteen equal parts through **a c d e f** and the remaining marks. So that **a b** contains eighteen of those parts, and **c b** sixteen, and **l b** seventeen. It is manifest that **a b** and **c b**, as seen before, are a tone [[notes: 18:16 simplifies to the 9:8 ratio of a whole tone]]; and **l b** (17) to **c b** (16) is a **sesquisextadecima**, which relationship I say is greater than a whole semitone. For since by the tenth [proposition] of this, a unit and a sixteenth of one is a sixteenth part of the sum seventeen. Which, if added to the same sum, becomes eighteen and a sixteenth of one. But eighteen and a sixteenth of one to seventeen, by definition, is **sesquisextadecima**. There are, therefore, eighteen and a sixteenth of one to seventeen, and seventeen to sixteen, two joined **sesquisextadecimas**. But eighteen and a sixteenth of one, by the preceding, is greater than a **sesquioctaua** [[notes: 9:8 ratio]] to sixteen. For eighteen to sixteen, as seen before, is a **sesquioctaua**. Therefore, the **sesquisextadecima** increased twice transcends a tone and the interval of a tone. Wherefore, by the ninth axiom, the **sesquisextadecima** ratio exceeds a whole half-tone. For whatever, being increased twice, transcends something, it is necessary that it be beyond its half. From which it is known by stronger right that the **sesquiquintadecima** [[notes: 16:15 ratio]] and every ratio greater than **sesquisextadecima** exceeds a whole half-tone.

### The sesquiseptimadecima: is smaller than a whole half-tone.
[[notes: This refers to the 18:17 ratio.]]

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| d | 8 | c | 17 | 8 | b | | **A** to **b** contains a **sesquiseptimadecima** ratio: |
| | | | | | | | which is narrower than a whole half-tone. |

Let **a** be eighteen parts of the interval and **b** seventeen, **c** nineteen and one seventeenth, and **d** indeed nineteen and one eighth. By the tenth [proposition] of this, **a** to **b** is a **sesquiseptimadecima**. And **c** to **a** is similarly a **sesquiseptimadecima**. There are, therefore, **c** to **b** two **sesquiseptimadecimas** joined to each other. But also by the same, **d** to **b** is a **sesquioctaua** and a tone [[notes: 9:8 ratio]]. For **d** contains **b** and its eighth part. But the ratio of **d** to **b** is greater than the ratio of **c** to **b**. For an eighth part of one is greater than one seventeenth. Therefore, the **sesquiseptimadecima** ratio, by the tenth axiom, is smaller than a whole semitone. For that which, being doubled, does not fill the whole, does not contain the half either. It is therefore known that the **sesquiseptimadecima** ratio is smaller than a whole half-tone.

[[margin: 14]]
### It is necessary that the whole half of a tone falls between the sesquisextadecima and sesquiseptimadecima ratio.

For by the twelfth [proposition], the **sesquisextadecima** is greater than the half of a tone: and by the thirteenth, it is smaller than the same half of a tone. But by common knowledge, it is necessary that the half itself consists between the greater and the lesser than the half. Therefore it is necessary that the whole half-tone falls between the **sesquisextadecima** and the **sesquiseptimadecima**. But that can happen in no way in a known and whole number, as the seventh [proposition] of this has shown: just as in geometry the diagonal of a square cannot be measured by a certain and established number of its side [[notes: This is a reference to irrational numbers, like the square root of 2, which cannot be expressed as a simple fraction]]. But this last [point] must be sought from another place.
