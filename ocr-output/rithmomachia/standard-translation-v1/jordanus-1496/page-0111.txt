and it will be known that **b** to **c** is a **sesquitertial ratio** <note>original: "sesquitertiū"; a ratio of 4:3, which produces a perfect fourth.</note>: and **b** contains four parts of **e**, and **e** is the difference between **b** and **c**. Likewise, because **b** contains four of **e** and **a** contains six of **e**: **a** will be **sesquialter** <note>original: "sesqualter"; a ratio of 3:2, which produces a perfect fifth.</note> to **b**. And because **b** equals four of **e**, and **a** equals six: therefore the difference between **a** and **b** equals two of **e**, which we shall call **d**. Since **d** also equals two of **e**, therefore **d** is double to **e**. I say, therefore, that **a**, **b**, and **c** are given as a **harmonic mean** <note>original: "harmonicam medietatem"; a type of mathematical proportion where the ratio of the extremes is equal to the ratio of their differences from the middle term.</note>; between these terms **a**, **b**, and **c**, and their differences **d** and **e**, all musical consonances may be found. For the ratio of **a** to **b**, the greatest to the least, is shown to be a double ratio [2:1], and similarly the relationship of **d** to **e** (that is, the ratio of the larger difference to the smaller difference) is also shown to be double. Therefore, by definition, the terms **a**, **b**, and **c** are established in a harmonic mean. 

But if you compare **b** to **c**, it is shown to be **sesquitertial** [4:3]; therefore, it is the interval of the **perfect fourth** <note>original: "diatessaron"</note>. And if **a** to **b**: it is shown to be **sesquialter** (also called **hemiolus**) [3:2] and the interval of the **perfect fifth** <note>original: "diapente"</note>. And if **a** to **c**: you have the double ratio [2:1] and the consonance of the **octave** <note>original: "diapason"</note>. If you then compare **c** to **e**: you have a triple ratio [3:1]; and according to the twenty-fifth theorem of the third book, this is the composite consonance of an **octave plus a fifth** <note>original: "diapason ac diapente"</note>. And if **b** to **e**: a quadruple ratio [4:1]; and according to the twenty-seventh theorem of the third book, it is a **double octave** <note>original: "bis diapason"</note>. 

And since the others are shown to be these consonances, it is established that the **fourth**, the **fifth**, the **octave**, the **octave-plus-fifth**, and the **double octave**—all the consonances in which musicians are accustomed to practice their discipline—have been found between the terms of the harmonic mean and their differences. And if you multiply these by two, or as many times as you wish, it is easy to know from the seventh theorem of the second book of *Arithmetics* that a similar harmonic mean is established among its terms and differences, preserving the musical consonances. The same result occurs if you multiply those terms by any other number. It pleased the **divine Severinus** <note>Referring to Boethius (Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, c. 477–524 AD), whose works were the standard textbooks for music and math in the Middle Ages and Renaissance.</note> in the forty-eighth chapter of the second book of his *Arithmetics* to arrange two harmonic means to embrace all musical consonances: one in a double ratio and the other in a triple ratio. But we judge it has now been sufficiently shown that this same thing can be done with only one mean established.

¶ They call it the **"Greatest Harmony"** when, among four **solid terms** <note>term: "solid terms" (solidi termini). In ancient math, these are numbers representing three dimensions, but here they refer to the four-number sequence (12, 9, 8, 6) that creates a geometric framework.</note> established in a **geometric mean**, an **arithmetic mean** is contained between the greatest term, one of the middle terms, and the smallest; and again, a **harmonic mean** is contained between the greatest, the other middle term, and the smallest. 

A **geometric mean** is when the ratio of the terms is the same. An **arithmetic mean**, on the other hand, is when the differences between the terms are equal. What a **harmonic mean** is has already been said. Numbers are called "solid terms" which are produced from the multiplication of three sides into themselves; but all these things are very well known from works on Arithmetic. We call the **whole tone** <note>original: "tonum"</note> the starting point of consonances. 

¶ I take any number **v** which has a half and a third; let its half be **f** and its third be **h**. Double it to get **d**, and let **a** be doubled again; whatever **a** is, it will be double to **d**. To **b** and to **b** to **v** let the composite be **c**. It will be **sesquitertial** [4:3] to **d**. And since **a** is shown to be double to **d**, then by the eleventh theorem of the first book, **a** to **c** is **sesquialter** [3:2]. Furthermore, I add **f** (the second part of **d**) to **d** itself, and let the result be **b**: it is certain that **b** to **d** is **sesquialter**. By the same eleventh theorem, **a** to **b** is **sesquitertial**. From **a** and **c**, which were shown to be **sesquialter**, if the **sesquitertial** ratio of **a** to **b** is subtracted, then by the fifteenth theorem of the first book, there remains the ratio of **b** to **c** as **sesquioctave** <note>original: "sesquioctuauus"; a 9:8 ratio, the mathematical definition of a musical whole tone.</note>. 

Furthermore, let **g** be the difference between **a** and **c**. Since **c** contains four parts and **a** contains six, the difference **a** to **c** contains two parts of **h**. Thus **g** is double to **h**. And because **f** is half of **d**, and **b** is **sesquialter** to **d**, then **b** contains three of **f**. Thus **f** is a third part of **b**. But since **a** is **sesquitertial** to **b**, it adds a third part of **b** above it; therefore, the difference of **a** to **b**, which we call **e**, equals **f**. 

I say, therefore, that **a/b/c/d** constitute the **Greatest Harmony**: which encompasses the **whole tone** (the element of consonances) and every other consonance. For **a** to **b** is shown to be **sesquitertial**, and similarly **c** to **d** is **sesquitertial**. They contain, therefore, **a** to **b** and **c** to **d** by definition: a **geometric mean**. And the difference **e** (between the greatest term **a** and the middle term **b**) and the difference **f** (between the middle term **b** and the smallest term **d**) are shown to be equal. Therefore, by definition, **a**, **b**, and **d** are established in an **arithmetic mean**. 

Furthermore, **a** to **d** (greatest to smallest) is shown to be double: and similarly **g** (the difference between the greatest **a** and the other middle term **c**) is demonstrated to be double the difference between **c** and the smallest term **d**. Therefore, by definition, **a**, **c**, and **d** constitute a **harmonic mean**. 

It is established, then, that **a/b/c/d** (if they are solid numbers) constitute the **Greatest Harmony**. If they are not solid numbers, multiply any number into them and solid numbers will result in the same proportions. But it has already been shown that **b** to **c** is **sesquioctave** [9:8]; therefore, by definition, **b** to **c** contains the **whole tone**, the starting point of consonances. And **a** to **b** is **sesquitertial**, containing the **perfect fourth**. And **a** to **c** is **sesquialter**, containing the **perfect fifth**. And **a** to **d** is double, containing the **octave**. And **a** to **h** <note>likely referring to a specific difference in the table</note> is triple, containing the **octave plus a fifth**. And **c** to **h** is shown to be quadruple, containing the **double octave**. And as often as you multiply any number into **a/b/c/d**, you will constitute the **Greatest Harmony**, containing the tone and all consonances. Thus, what was proposed has been achieved. And from this you can understand that what the divine **Severinus** [Boethius] brings forth concerning the "harmony of the cube" in chapter 49, and concerning the "greatest harmony" in chapter 54 of the second book of his *Arithmetics*, has been demonstrated.
