### ¶ If the first term of an interval compared to the second holds the same ratio as a third to a fourth: then as many proportional means as fall between the first and second must also fall between the third and fourth.

->E is the proportional mean between the third and fourth (d and f), just as b is between the first and second (a and c).<-

¶ The twelfth proposition of the fourth book [[notes: Refers to Lefèvre's own *Arithmetica*]] demonstrates this; however, because it is of great momentum for what follows, the demonstration is repeated here so that the knowledge may be more readily grasped.
¶ Let **a** be the first term of any interval and **c** the second, just as **d** is the third and **f** the fourth; and let **b** be the proportional mean established between **a** and **c**. I say that one proportional mean also falls between **d** and **f**. For I take, by the sixth proposition of the fourth book, **g**, **h**, and **k** as the three minimum terms [[notes: The lowest whole-number representation of a ratio, such as 4:6:9 for the ratio 2:3]] according to the proportion of **a** to **b**. I argue from the twenty-first of the second book that by equal proportionality, **g** is to **h** as **a** is to **b**, and **h** is to **k** as **b** is to **c**. Therefore, **g** is to **k** as **a** is to **c**, and consequently as **d** is to **f**. Now, if **d** and **f** are the same terms as **g** and **k**, it is already manifest that one proportional mean falls between **d** and **f**. If they are not the same, then by the twenty-third proposition of the *Arithmetica*, **g** and **k** will measure [[notes: "Measure" in this context means "to be a factor of" or "to divide evenly into"]] **d** and **f** equally; let this be according to the number **l**. I therefore multiply **l** by **g**, **h**, and **k**, and let there result **d**, **e**, and **f**. By the seventh of the second book, **d** is to **e** as **g** is to **h**, and **e** is to **f** as **h** is to **k**. Therefore, **e** falls as a proportional mean between the terms **d** and **f**, which was to be demonstrated.

### ¶ In a superparticular interval, no mean number—neither one nor many—can intervene proportionally.

<margin>
[[gloss: a] A superparticular ratio: cannot be divided into two equal parts.]]
</margin>

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **a** | **b** | **c** | | **d** | **e** | **g** | **f** | **D e f are the three minimums of the ratio a to b.** |

¶ We understand by this that no superparticular interval [[notes: A "superparticular" ratio is one where the leading number is exactly one unit larger than the following number, such as 3:2 (the musical fifth) or 9:8 (the whole tone).]] is divisible into two or more equal interval relationships or proportions. This will be made very clear by the demonstration immediately following.
¶ Let **a c** be a superparticular interval which is divided into two spaces of equal relationship, **a b** and **b c**, by certain established numbers, if it is possible. Therefore, let **a** be to **b** as **b** is to **c**. I take, by the sixth of the fourth book of the *Arithmetica*, **d**, **e**, and **f** as the three minimum continued proportionals in the ratio of **a** to **b**. And because **d** is to **e** as **a** is to **b**, and **e** is to **f** as **b** is to **c**, then by equal proportionality **d** is to **f** as **a** is to **c**. But **a** to **c** was posited as a superparticular interval; therefore, the ratio **d** to **f** is superparticular. But since **d**, **e**, and **f** are the minimum terms of that relationship, then by the first of the fourth book, the first and last—**d** and **f**—are prime to each other [[notes: "Prime to each other" or "coprime" means they share no common factors other than 1]]. Yet since **d** has been shown to be superparticular to **f**, by definition **d** contains **f** plus a part of it, which by definition must measure **f**. If that part were a number, then by a common concept it would also measure **d**, for it would measure itself (the part taken away) and the remainder (which is equal to **f**). Thus **d** and **f** would not be prime to each other, the opposite of which has already been shown and is impossible. Therefore, that part must be unity (the number 1). In that case, I add unity to **f** and let it become **g**, so that **g** and **f** differ only by the distance of a single unit; then I argue that **d** is to **f** as **g** is to **f**. But it was posited that one mean falls between **d** and **f**; therefore, by the preceding proposition, some mean number falls and is intercepted between **g** and **f**, numbers which differ by only a single unit—which is impossible according to the second petition [[notes: In the arithmetic of integers, there is no whole number between two consecutive numbers like 8 and 9]]. Therefore, not one mean number falls within a superparticular interval. Nor, by the same argument, can many fall. 

This is also demonstrated by the sixty-first proposition of the ninth book [[notes: Refers to Euclid’s *Elements*]]. From this, one also recognizes the critique of the demonstration by Archytas [[notes: Archytas of Tarentum, a Pythagorean philosopher/mathematician]]—that no superparticular can be divided into two equal parts—which the Divine Severinus [[notes: Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 480–524 AD), whose *De institutione musica* was the standard textbook for a millennium]] appears to bring forward in the eleventh chapter of the third book of the *Institution of Music*. This proof belongs not so much to Divine Severinus as to Nicomachus [[notes: Nicomachus of Gerasa, a Greek mathematician whose work Boethius translated/adapted]] or others whom he interprets most elegantly, and who is found to have done similarly in many places in both the *Arithmetical* and *Musical Institutions*. 

And yet some (if I may speak with the peace of all) are not afraid to scour his books, not so they may know, but so they may pluck at them; and in the manner of a dog, if anything appears gaping or needing support, they fix their teeth in deeply, shaking and critiquing it. They tear at the holy and venerable name of Philosophy with their insults. What is the fault of others, they proclaim to be the error of Divine Severinus, making him guilty of another’s crime. While they know nothing, they wish to seem to know everything by hounding, carping at, and mangling the leaders of literature. They extol themselves boastfully above the peaks of the Philosophers. They do what is said in the Comedy: "by understanding, they understand nothing" [[notes: A quote from Terence, *Andria* 17: *faciunt nae intelligendo ut nihil intelligant*]]. These people should be warned immediately to stop their slandering, lest they become acquainted with their own misdeeds. But these matters are beyond the duty of the present business. For those who are rightly inclined by nature toward Philosophy will always think rightly, even without being warned; those who do otherwise segregate themselves from that fellowship, and it is necessary that they be recognized as no longer being the Philosophers they wish to seem.
