# The book's verdict <note>original: "verdicte"</note>.

Missing <note>original: "Wanting"</note> I have been truly long,
In the English language for many a day;
Behold, yet at last now here am I,
To delay your great labors,
And to show you a pleasant pastime,
Intending <note>original: "Mynding"</note> to move your wits, I believe <note>original: "trow"</note>.

For though I provoke you to mirth,
To wisdom yet I move you more:
Laying on them a pleasant yoke,
Wisdom, I mean, which is the door
Of all good and commendable things;
I think no man is able to doubt this:

->CATO.<-
> *Intermingle your cares with joy from time to time:* <note>original: "Interpone tuis interdum gaudia curis"</note>
> *So that you may be able to endure any labor with spirit.* <note>original: "Ut possis animo quemvis sufferre laborem"</note>

<summary>The book speaks directly to the reader, explaining that it has been absent from the English language for a long time but has finally arrived to provide a pleasant diversion from hard work. The author explains that while the game provides entertainment and mirth, its true purpose is to lead the player toward wisdom, which is the source of all good things. It concludes with a quote from Cato advising the reader to balance work with leisure to better endure life's challenges.</summary>
