<header>THE EPISTLE</header>

But now, instead of pleasant mirth,
great passions arise:
Instead of recreation now,
we practice revenges.

Instead of love and friendship <note>original: "amitie"</note>,
long discords appear:
Instead of truth and quietness,
we hear great oaths and lies.

Instead of friendship, now falsehood,
mixed with cruel hate:
We find within plays and games,
which daily cause debate.

Pythagoras <note>original: "Pithagoras"</note>, therefore, I say,
to make redress in this:
First invented this godly game,
thereby to flee <note>original: "flye"</note> from sin.

Since which time it has continued,
in French and Latin also <note>original: "eke"</note>:
Still practiced <note>original: "exercisde"</note> by learned men,
seeking their comforts in this way.

<summary>In this section of the verse dedication, the author contrasts the negative state of contemporary gaming—which he claims is filled with discord, lies, and hatred—with the original intent of "The Philosophers' Game." He attributes the invention of this game to the ancient philosopher Pythagoras, who supposedly created it as a virtuous pursuit to help players avoid sin. The author notes that the game has a long tradition of study by learned men in both French and Latin.</summary>
