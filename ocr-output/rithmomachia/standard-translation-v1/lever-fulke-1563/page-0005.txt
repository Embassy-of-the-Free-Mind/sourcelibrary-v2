Yes, thereby he would well refresh
his studious, weary brain;
And still wade further in knowledge
and apply it to his gain.

Accounting <note>original: "Accompting"</note> that a wicked play,
wherein a man wickedly <note>original: "leudely"</note>
misspends his time and wit also,
and gets no good from it.

But grievously offends the Lord,
and so instead of rest:
Is pressed <note>original: "preſt"</note> on every side
with great trouble and vexation.

Most games and plays are abused,
and few now remain
In good and godly order as
they certainly <note>original: "certaine"</note> ought to be.

For why? All games should refresh <note>original: "recreate"</note>
the heavy mind of man,
And also <note>original: "eke"</note> the body then <note>original: "than"</note>
overburdened with cares and troubles.

<summary>In this continuation of the dedication to Lord Robert Dudley, the author explains the purpose of "The Philosophers' Game." He argues that intellectual games should be used to refresh a "weary brain" and increase knowledge, rather than being used for "wicked" or "lewd" pastimes that waste time and offend God. He concludes that the true purpose of any game is to provide recreation for the mind and relief for a body burdened by the cares of the world.</summary>
