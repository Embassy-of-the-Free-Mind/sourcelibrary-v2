### THE EPISTLE

I thought therefore your honor should
not lack some godly game:
By which you might at vacant times
adapt <note>original: "frame"</note> yourself to pastime.

By which, I say, you might release
such labors <note>original: "trauailes"</note> from your mind:
And in the meanwhile honest mirth
and prudent pastime find.

Remembering then this ancient play,
where wisdom does <note>original: "doth"</note> abound:
Called the Philosophers' Game,
I think I have found one <note>original: "me thinkth I haue one found"</note>.

Which may your honor refresh <note>original: "recreate"</note>,
to read and practice <note>original: "exercise"</note>:
And which to you I here submit,
in a simple <note>original: "rude"</note> and humble <note>original: "homly"</note> manner <note>original: "vviſe"</note>.

Pythagoras did first invent
this play, as it is thought:
And thereby after great studies,
sought his recreation.

<summary>This portion of the dedication continues James Rowbothum's address to Lord Robert Dudley. The author presents "The Philosophers' Game" (Rythmomachia) as a mentally stimulating and "godly" pastime intended to provide Dudley with a productive way to relax from his heavy administrative burdens. Rowbothum attributes the invention of the game to the ancient philosopher Pythagoras, suggesting it serves as a form of intellectual refreshment after periods of intense study.</summary>
