# The Second Book / Chapter 1:

...and (3.) why they are used / and have come about / to learn to recognize.

Some claim that the little words **Schach** [[notes: Check]] and **Mat** [[notes: Mate]] are Hebrew. But it is called *Scacha* (as Matth. Flacius Illyr. [[notes: Matthias Flacius Illyricus (1520–1575), a Lutheran reformer and scholar who wrote the *Clavis Scripturae Sacrae*]] will have it in *Clav: Script: verb: Adorare*) in Hebrew, meaning as much as *adorare*, to worship someone / and to show him honor with a bowed or bent body; or / (as others will have it) to be surrounded by a wall / or fenced in: **Mat** or *Mot*, however / is said among them to mean to have died.

In the Chaldean language [[notes: A term historically used to refer to Aramaic or ancient Babylonian languages]], **Schach** is said to serve as a warning / to take good care / of one's affairs: Therefore / the Oriental peoples / (as found in Joseph Scaligero [[notes: Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609), a famous French religious scholar and father of modern chronology]], *lib: de Emendat: Temporum*, page 78) perhaps named the time of the twelve warning-years *Schaichun*.

Some wish / that this little word **Schach** / though it be somewhat altered / takes its origin / from the Arabs / and in their language / signifies as much as an expulsion / a chasing away: just as then / by **Mat** / such a thing is understood / which pertains to death. Therefore the Arabs / call the place of the other life / after this death / *Mahad*.

Others seek / the origin of this little word / from the modern Persians: (whose language is in use throughout the entire Orient / as the Latin is among us / in the Occident [[notes: The West]]: Joseph Scalig: *d. lib: de Emend: Temp: page 338.*) because namely these / and with them the Mauritanians [[notes: People of Northwest Africa, often called Moors in this period]] and others / call their Kings / (as we have previously recounted / from Garc. ab Orta [[notes: Garcia de Orta, a 16th-century physician and pioneer of tropical medicine]], Bizaro [[notes: Pietro Bizzari]], and Minadoo [[notes: Giovanni Tommaso Minadoi, an Italian historian of the Persian-Turkish wars]]) *Xa*, *Sah*, *Schah*, or **Schach**.

We have otherwise / in the 2nd Chapter of the 1st Book / above / already surmised with Vito Amerbachio [[notes: Veit Amerbach (1503–1557), a German humanist scholar]], that these were / good German words / and that **Schachen** [[notes: to check]] signifies as much / as being hunted / or to flee. What **Mat** is / is quite well known / meaning as much / as to lose one's strength / to languish / to grow weary / to be unable to move a foot from the spot / nor go further / but / to be forced to stand still. From this German *matten* [[notes: to tire out or exhaust]] / *ermatten* / and *Schach-Mat geben* / the *Welschen* [[notes: A period term for speakers of Romance languages, particularly Italians and French]] / have made and taken their *Mattare*, and *Scacco matto dare*.

To what end now / these words are used / is to be repeated here from the previous description of **Schachen** and **Matten**: namely / that one indicates to the opponent / with these words / that his King / is under attack [[notes: *Angeſprenget*, literally "galloped at" or charged]] / and that he should withdraw.
