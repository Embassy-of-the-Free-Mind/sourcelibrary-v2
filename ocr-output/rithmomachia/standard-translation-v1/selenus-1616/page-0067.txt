As then of them/ **Nicolaus Trigautius** [[notes: Nicolas Trigault (1577–1628), a Flemish Jesuit missionary to China]], of the Society of Jesus, from the commentaries of **Matthæus Ricij** [[notes: Matteo Ricci (1552–1610), one of the founding figures of the Jesuit China missions]], of the same Society, regarding the Christian Expedition to the Chinese [[notes: *De Christiana expeditione apud Sinas*, a seminal work published in 1615 that introduced China to the European public]], book 1, pages 90 and 91, testifies/ when he writes:

> *The game of dice and of cards, which usage has also brought into these parts, is plebeian and common. More serious men, for passing the time and even for playful gain, employ "little soldiers"* [[notes: *Latrunculos*, a term for an ancient Roman strategy game, here used by the author to describe the Chinese version of chess, Xiangqi]]*, not entirely unlike our own. In these they differ: for the Royal piece never leaves the four cells nearest his station, nor do the two "Literati"* [[notes: The Mandarins or Advisors in Xiangqi]], *the King’s assessors. They lack a Queen. Furthermore, they have two other pieces, quite ingenious, which they themselves call "War-powder Cauldrons"* [[notes: The Cannons or *Pao* in Chinese chess]]; *these precede the two Horses and follow the infantry, who, in these two cells, precede by one cell. This piece moves in almost the same manner as our turreted Elephants* [[notes: The Rooks or Castles]]; *however, it does not attack the Leader of the enemy army, the King, unless between itself and the King being attacked there is another piece, either one’s own or the opponent’s. Hence it happens that the King, being sought, can avoid the blow in three ways: first, by a slight—so to speak—deflection of the body and an honorable flight into the nearest cell or station; secondly, by the opposition of another piece; finally, if he completely bares his side and orders his soldier, by whom he was covered, to depart.* ,,
> 
> *The most serious kind of game among them is of this sort: On a board of three hundred cells* [[notes: This refers to the game of **Go** (known as *Weiqi* in China). A standard Go board has 361 intersections, which the author approximates as 300]], *more than two hundred pieces are played with, some of which are white, others black. With these pieces, one player tries to drive the other’s pieces into the middle of the board so that he may then dominate the remaining cells. In the end, he who has subjected more cells on the board to himself is called the Victor. The Magistrates take up this game most greedily and often spend the greatest part of the day playing; for among those skilled in playing, a single game lasts a full hour. He who is skilled in this game, even if he were distinguished in no other thing, is honored and summoned by all. Indeed, some even choose them as Masters, with their accustomed rituals, so that they may learn the logic of this game accurately from them.* ,, That is: ,,
