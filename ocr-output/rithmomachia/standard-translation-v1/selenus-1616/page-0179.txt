Mention was previously made in the 10th Chapter of the 1st Book that no **soldier** [[notes: *Soldat*; the standard term for a pawn in this period, reflecting the game's metaphor as a battlefield]] may pass by those of the other side without the permission of the other army's leader. Now, if one of the players moves a soldier—with which he should have captured the opponent’s soldier—yet passes by regardless and places it on the next field ahead of him, the other player has the power to take him away on the line where he stands. However, he must move and place his own soldier into the back field where the other soldier, who should have been struck from there, had previously stood [[notes: This is a description of the *en passant* rule, where a pawn moving two squares can be captured by an opposing pawn as if it had only moved one square]]. If, however, he makes another move in the meantime, he no longer has the power to take him away in the future. For it was previously in his power to take him in the manner just described, or to let him stand.

For example: When one of the White Queen's soldiers moves as far as he can towards the border—that is, into his fourth field—and the other moves the Black King's soldier also as far as he can for the first time (namely, into his fourth field), and the former does not wish to take away the Black King’s soldier, but instead moves his soldier forward into the fourth field of the Black Queen: then the latter takes this one, and places his soldier not in the field from which he took him (which is at his side), but rather into the fourth field of the White Queen which is behind him, and from which the former should have struck and attacked him before, as it was not otherwise agreed upon. This, however, is only to be understood regarding the **border-fields** [[notes: *gränz-feldern*; the squares in the center of the board, usually the 4th and 5th ranks, where the opposing pawns first meet]]. For when the soldiers have occupied the first or *respectivè* the fourth field of the enemy, it is then free for them (so far as it is not otherwise agreed) either to strike or to pass by.

A **chess-player** [[notes: *Schach-Zieher*; literally "chess-mover"]] should, however, in the capturing of soldiers, take as much care as possible that he does not move one soldier in front of the other (because the movement of the hindmost soldier is thereby hindered), but rather removes with a **major piece** [[notes: *Haupt-Steine*; literally "head-stones," referring to the more powerful pieces like the Rook, Knight, Bishop, or Queen]] the other's piece which might be an obstacle. He might then obtain a good piece thereby and better arrange the game; or he must do such out of necessity because of a piece he must guard against.
