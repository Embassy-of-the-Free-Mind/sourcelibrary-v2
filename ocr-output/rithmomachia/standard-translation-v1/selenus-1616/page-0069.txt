# ## 2. Chapter: Of the Game of Chess.

> [is] trained / though he / understands little in other matters / he is held in very high regard / and preferred over others. Indeed, some are also accustomed / with special pomp and custom / to choose these same people / just so they may learn this game / correctly and from the ground up / as teachers / and to hold them as such / for the duration of their entire lives / according to their custom / (p. 90).

If one now turns back / toward the North / one encounters / the Muscovites and Russians: of whom / Paulus Oderbornius and P. Laur, in a letter to D. Chytraeus, August 1, 1581, sent from Lithuania, reported the following:

> *The Russians or Muscovites play the battle of the thieves [[notes: A reference to "Ludus Latrunculorum," an ancient Roman strategy game that Renaissance writers often conflated with or used as a Latin name for Chess]] with the greatest skill; so that two Kings, White and Black, opposing one another for praise, contend with two-colored weapons. In this art they truly so excel that I know not whether any nation ought to be compared with them.* That is:

> The Russians / or Muscovites / play the game of Chess / very ingeniously / and with special diligence: and are in the same / so skillful and experienced / that / in my opinion / other nations / cannot easily be compared with them.

The Poles / Finns / and other Northern peoples [[notes: Literally "Midnight peoples," a common early modern term for those living in the North]] / do not yield much to these neighbors of theirs.

Of the Ancient Goths / mention has already been made above / in the First Chapter / regarding the seventh benefit.

What the situation is / in this regard / with us Germans / is / because it is well known / not necessary to write. Otherwise, one sees from the history / of King Pippin’s son [[notes: Charlemagne (Charles the Great), son of Pippin the Short]] / (which we cited above) / that the Germans / have known how to play / this game / for nearly a thousand years. And even to this day / the Chess-pieces are shown / in the French Royal Treasury / at St. Denis [[notes: The Basilica of Saint-Denis near Paris, the traditional burial site of French kings and home to many royal relics]] / with which Carolus Magnus [[notes: Charlemagne]], son of Pippin / and first German Emperor / is said at times / to have passed the time and played.

Likewise / among the English / French / and Italians [[notes: The original term "Welschen" generally referred to speakers of Romance languages, most commonly Italians or French]] / the game of Chess / is not hidden under the bench [[notes: An idiom meaning the game is not neglected, but rather held in high regard]]. But in particular / the Spaniards / as they are by nature inclined toward gaming / are said also to be the best players / (as witnessed concerning them / along with an explanation of the reasons / by the aforementioned Pascasius Justus Ecloviensis, pages 36, 46) / and are very devoted to the game of Chess: they are also held by many / to be the best chess-movers / (as Count Balthasar of Castiglione [[notes: Baldassare Castiglione, the Italian author of "The Book of the Courtier" (1528), a famous guide on etiquette for the nobility]], book 2, *De Curiali sive Aulico*, ascribes to them).
