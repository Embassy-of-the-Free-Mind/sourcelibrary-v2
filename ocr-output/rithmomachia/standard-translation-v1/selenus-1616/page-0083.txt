### Regarding the Game of Chess.

[[margin: Chapter 3:]] to be reckoned; and thus, because he commands all and is their head, he is assigned, as it were, to the middle (just as the Persians and the Ancients gave the middle place to those whom they wished to honor). Therefore, the White King stands in a Black square, and the Black King stands in a White square. [[notes: This describes the traditional rule that the King starts on a square of the opposite color—unlike the Queen, who "stands on her own color."]]

Regarding his gait, it is slow: only from one square into another, and no further (except for the **Jump** into the third square, of which more shall be said shortly), even if **Check** [[notes: *Schach*, the warning that the King is under attack]] is offered to him. He can, however, perform his move in all manners, just as the **Rooks** [[notes: *Rochen*, the tower-shaped pieces]], **Bishops** [[notes: *Schützen*, literally "archers"]], and **Pawns** [[notes: *Soldaten*, literally "soldiers"]] do, regardless of the color of the square. He may step forward or backward, sideways, or toward the corners and points (that is, upon those lines where the squares that follow one another are of the same color—for example, the line that reaches from the White King’s Rook to the Black Queen’s Rook and has white squares; or the way the Pawns capture one another). Finally, he can never be moved closer to the opposing King than the third square, because a square must always remain between them (for reasons which may be found below, in the second book, at the end of the first chapter). [[notes: This refers to the rule that two Kings can never stand on adjacent squares.]] Therefore, from his starting square (at the beginning of the game), he may be moved into five other squares: namely, (1.) that of the Queen; (2.) that of his Bishop; (3.) the Queen’s other square; (4.) his Bishop’s other square; and (5.) his own other square. [[notes: This likely refers to the "King's Leap" options or the immediate surrounding squares on the second rank.]]

The **Jump** [[notes: *Der Sprung*, the "King's Leap," a special move allowed once per game in early modern chess]] is permitted to him not only in the manner of the **Knights** [[notes: *Reutern*, literally "riders"]], but also to the sides and forward, though with certain measures and conditions. Namely: he must not have yet received any **Check** which forced him to yield from his square (of which the first chapter of the second book makes mention); and there must be no piece—whether from the enemy's host or his own—hindering him in the third square to which he jumps. Furthermore, by this jump, no piece belonging to the enemy may be captured if one stands upon those squares; nor may his own **Pawn** be driven out of its square into the next one forward so that he may be placed in its stead. (This is otherwise customary in some places, perhaps intended to suggest that the lesser is obligated to give way to the higher, even at the peril of life and limb.)
