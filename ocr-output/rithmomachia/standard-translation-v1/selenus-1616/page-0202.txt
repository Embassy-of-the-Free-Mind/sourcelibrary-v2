# The Seventh Chapter.
## On the examples in particular / how the same / 
## into the common / and special / and then the 
## common / in turn / into three groups / and 
## their manifold types / are 
## divided.

The division of the following examples (so that we may, at last, proceed to their description) will be understood primarily from the following: for,

First, we wish to record and describe the ways and manners in which one can play and conclude the game in the common manner: Thereafter, when an **advantage** [[notes: *Vortheil*; refers to "odds" or "handicaps" given to a weaker player, such as starting with an extra piece]]—(of which a report shall follow below, in the final Book, from the first to the ninth Chapter)—has been given, how it can be well-played and brought to an end.

Now, as concerns the common way of playing without a given advantage: this will again be found to be threefold: For the First comprises the games which are begun and carried out with the King’s [pawn]; the Second, those with the Queen’s [pawn]; the Third, those with a **Rook’s** [[notes: *Rochen*; the early term for the Rook, derived from the Persian 'rukh']], **Archer’s** [[notes: *Schützen*; the 17th-century German term for the Bishop]], or **Rider’s** [[notes: *Reuter*; the Knight]] **Soldier** [[notes: *Soldaten*; the standard term for Pawns in this era]], or even with a Rider.

Among these, there shall also be sufficient reminder regarding how others, and specifically *Damianus* [[notes: Pedro Damiano (1480–1544), a Portuguese apothecary and author of one of the earliest printed chess manuals]], have erred in them: so that their errors may be avoided and the same be brought onto a proper path.

Among these three, the first, with the King’s [pawn], is the best; the second, with the Queen’s [pawn], is to be tolerated; but the third, with a Rook’s, Archer’s, or Rider’s pawn, or even with a Rider, is to be rejected.

However, the first common type is again distinguished by the first move: for, one can either start with the King’s Soldier on both sides: or (as has been briefly set down in the tenth chapter of the third book) the one who has the first move indeed gives
