# Of the Game of Chess.

...it is all yours: and, acting as if he intended to push his **stake** [[notes: *aufgesetzetes geld* — the money wagered on the game]] toward the **Warden's** pile and mix it therewith, he added at least three times as much of his other money to it. The **Warden**, pretending to pay attention to other words and matters, subtly pulled it toward himself, saying: "Young fellow, I swear to you by the soul of my father, that I have played with many in all my days, but never found one who has given me **checkmate** [[notes: *Schachmat*]] in this game, except for you; likewise, I have never seen a more polite youth."

[[margin: 1. cap:]]
From all this, it is now to be seen how far this game is to be distinguished from all others which are either entirely or partly subject to **luck** [[notes: *Glücke* — referring to games of chance like dice]], and which cannot by far reach its purpose and utility. This distinction was well noted and observed by **Steph: Costa** in *Tract: de Ludo*; **Lamb: Danæus** in his *Tract: de Ludo Aleæ*; and **Dr. Henricus Knaustinus** in his *repetit: de Ludo* [[notes: These are 16th-century legal and theological scholars who wrote on the morality and classification of games]]. At the same time, it should be remembered that the **Number-Game** [[notes: *Zahl-Spiel*, likely referring to Rithmomachia, a complex mathematical board game popular in the Middle Ages and Renaissance]], (of which a separate little treatise has been appended to the end of this description) would be of the same stamp as our game, if only it were just as commonly used.

Regarding this same [game], which has hitherto been described with sufficient and satisfactory reasons concerning the nature and properties of this art: several people—among whom are persons of no small rank and reputation—offer no little opposition. (Such as: according to **Plato** in *lib: 2. de Republ.*: the **King of Great Britain, James**, the first of that name, in the same book which his Majesty wrote to his son, **Henry** [[notes: Prince Henry Frederick, who died young]], of Christ-mild and highly laudable memory, and entitled *A Royal Gift* [[notes: *Basilikon Doron*, a manual on government written by James I, where he famously advised his son to avoid chess because it was "too over-wise and philosophicke a folly"]]; likewise, **Paris de Puteo** in *tractat: de Ludo, n. 11*; **Navarrus** in *Enchir: confess: c. 16. n. 3.*; and **Olaus Magnus**, the Archbishop of Uppsala, in the 13th Chapter of the 15th Book; as also, in part, **Count Baldassare of Castiglione** in the 2nd book of his *Complete Courtier* [[notes: *Il Cortegiano*, which suggests a courtier should be proficient but not too skilled at chess, lest it seem he spends too much time on a mere game]]; further, **Petrus Heigius** in his last *quæst: quæst: 10, n. 51*; and then **Emanuel Soarez**, *de verbo Lydus, n. 197.*). We must nevertheless confront these [opponents] as the necessity of the matter requires before we proceed further.
