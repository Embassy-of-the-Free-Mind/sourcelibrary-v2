# The Third Book / 11. cap:
[[notes: Chapter 11: On the Game of Chess.]]

[[margin: II.]]
Therefore, the **White**—as **Damianus** [[notes: Pedro Damiano (1480–1544), author of a famous early chess manual]] indeed considers it in n. 6—should not take the **S. K. Reuter** [[notes: *Schwarzer Königs-Reuter*, or Black King's Rider (Knight)]] with his **K. Schützen** [[notes: *Königs-Schütze*, or King's Archer (Bishop)]]; rather, he should pay diligent attention to the **Black’s** schemes and move his **Queen** away from the place where she stands, behind the **K. Schützen** (unless he wishes to be deprived of victory). In summary: let the **Black** play as he will; if the **White** is experienced in this game, he will win it much more easily if he does not exchange his **K. Schützen** for the **S. K. Reuter**, but instead moves out into the field with his **Reutern** [[notes: Riders (Knights)]] and places his **King** in a secure location. The **Black**, however, if he intends to use his **Rochen** [[notes: Rooks or Castles]] subsequently, will certainly be threshing empty straw [[notes: A period idiom meaning to engage in a futile or useless effort]]. Therefore, let the **White** (notwithstanding that **Damianus** considers it advisable) give no cause for the exchange of pieces; for it is much more the **Black's** concern to strive for this, so that he might the better extricate himself from the enemy's schemes.
