# The First Book / Chapter 2:

,, *By the drawing of turned straight lines, the sides of squares, equally*
,, *dissecting [one another], it is described:* **That is:**
,, **The Line-Game / which was depicted with straight lines / just /**
,, **as a geometric figure / was played /**
,, **with sixty stones / thirty of which were black / and the**
,, **others white. This game seems / to be equal / to that**
,, **which is played today among us / with eighteen stones / of two colors /**
,, **divided into two heaps. These stones / are placed and laid / in a certain way**
,, **and manner / upon the corners or angles / and midpoints / where**
,, **the straight lines / of the four-cornered figure / meet with the**
,, **drawn-through cross-lines. However / this same figure / is made and**
,, **drawn / from three squares / which / because**
,, **one is larger than the other / are distinct / and their sides /**
,, **are intersected in the middle / with straight cross-lines. By this last game /**
**without doubt / the Mill-Game [[notes: Known today as Nine Men's Morris or Merels; a strategy board game for two players where the goal is to form "mills"—rows of three stones]], is understood by Majolus [[notes: Simon Majolus (1510–1597), an Italian bishop whose encyclopedic work *Dies Caniculares* is a frequent source for early modern writers]].**

->**How the stone-game was played among the Romans / shall be**<-
->**understood from the following.**<-

**Ovidius [[notes: Publius Ovidius Naso (43 BC – 17 AD), a Roman poet famous for his works on love]], mentions this game / in his writings / principally / in three places; Firstly / Book 2 of *The Art of Love* [[notes: *Ars Amatoria*, a series of elegies providing instruction in the crafts of courtship and seduction]],**
**page 409.**

> ,, *Whether the counter moves under the image of robbery,*
> ,, *Let your soldier perish by a glass enemy.*

->**That is:**<-

,, **When you play with your beloved [[notes: The original German "Buhlschafft" refers to a lover or a romantic interest]] / with glass stones /**
,, **upon a Chess-table [[notes: The author uses "Schach-Tabel" broadly here to mean any checkered or lined gaming board, specifically the Roman *Tabula Latrunculorum*]] / and she makes / a secret plan / against your**
,, **stones / then simply permit / (because you should strive**
,, **to be at her service / in all things) your stone /**
,, **to be taken away unhindered / by her stone / and behave**
,, **as if you / had not seen nor noticed it.**

**Secondly / Book 3 of *The Art of Love*, page 440.**

> ,, *Let her play cautiously, and not foolishly, the battles of the Robbers,*
> ,, *One counter perishes with a twin enemy.*
> ,, *The warrior, caught without his comrade, fights on:*
> ,, *An envious rival often retraces the work begun.*

->**That is:**<-

,, **She / (namely / the young lady / who is sought after /**
,, **with honor) shall cautiously / and wisely / play the Chess- or**
**Stone-**
