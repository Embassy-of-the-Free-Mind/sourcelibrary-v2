# On the Battle of Numbers

*...that it was the same rule which the Ancients followed in their playing; from which fact I capture the conjecture [[notes: "Conjecturam capio" – a Latin phrasing meaning "I infer" or "I gather the idea"]] that the development and movement of **Chess** (*Scachorum*) drew its origin from the very same source. From this, it is easy to see how severely and stoically this game was described by certain of the Ancients: who, while content with the mere contemplation of the game, looked down upon that part of its exercise which would have been neither unpleasant nor joyless to the players. These [Ancients] are **Gilbertus Papa** [[notes: Pope Sylvester II (c. 946–1003), a great mathematician and astronomer who was rumored to have brought the game to Europe]], **Hermannus Contractus** [[notes: Hermann of Reichenau (1013–1054), a Benedictine monk and scholar who wrote one of the earliest descriptions of the game]], **Castrensis**, and **Nicolaus Orestinus** [[notes: Nicole Oresme (c. 1320–1382), a significant medieval philosopher and mathematician]]. In our own memory, they have been imitated by **Orontius Finæus Delphinas** [[notes: Oronce Finé (1494–1555), a French mathematician and cartographer]] and **Jacobus Faber Stapulensis** [[notes: Jacques Lefèvre d’Étaples (c. 1455–1536), whose 1496 publication "Rithmimachie ludus" revitalized interest in the game during the Renaissance]]; nor have I, until now, departed from their footsteps. But when I saw that this contemplation, when separated from recreation, was sometimes tedious, I greatly rejoice that I have recovered that which had long ago been cut off and separated—which seemed to contribute most to the perfect completion of this work.*

Finally, because this game encompasses many other games within itself, and increases their charm and perfection rather than diminishing them; and because it is useful and delightful not only to the unlearned and unrefined, but also to the learned and subtle minds: therefore, everyone will surely be pleased by it. And so that one may rouse one's spirit and understanding—through these pleasant and charming "game-matters"—toward much more serious and higher things, and conversely, when one is exhausted by "head-work" [[notes: *Kopf-Arbeit* – mental labor or intense study]], one may again be revived and refreshed by it; let everyone learn it diligently and make themselves familiar with it.
