# To the Reader.

Whenever the **Author** of this following work found himself free from more important affairs, he occasionally—in order to avoid idleness—committed to paper and recorded several rules, examples, and whatever else was useful for the **Game of Chess** (to which he found himself somewhat **inclined**), though without any order, and merely for his own recollection. 

Although it was not his intention to let these thoughts of his become a **Treatise**, much less to have them appear in public print—especially as **Seneca's** [[notes: Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BC – 65 AD), a Roman Stoic philosopher whose works remained highly influential in the 17th century.]] words (as the same were also cited by **Phil: Camerarius** [[notes: Philipp Camerarius (1537–1624), a German jurist and scholar known for his popular collection of historical and philosophical anecdotes titled *Horæ Subcisivæ*.]], in the preface to the 2nd Century of his *Hor: subcil:*) were well-recalled by him and maturely considered: *“Quoties aliquid scripturus es, scito, te, morum tuorum & ingenij, hominibus, chirographum dare”* [[notes: Latin: "As often as you are about to write something, know that you are giving a handwritten testament of your character and talent to men." This suggests that writing publicly exposes the author’s private soul to judgment.]]. 

Yet, at the request of several of his relatives and other sensible persons whom they [the notes] somewhat pleased, he gradually brought them into an order. After the passing of some time, he finally—lest an excessive diligence (which is called ***morosam diligentiam*** [[notes: A Latin term for a "fretful" or "tedious" diligence—the habit of over-working a piece of writing until its original vitality is lost.]]) should be reproached against him by anyone, just as happened to **Protogenes** at the hands of **Apelles** [[notes: A famous anecdote from Pliny the Elder: the Greek painter Apelles praised his rival Protogenes but said his only fault was not knowing when to "take his hand off" the canvas.]], or to **P. Æmilius Veronensis** and **Th. Linacre** the Englishman [[notes: Thomas Linacre (c. 1460–1524) was a celebrated English humanist and physician, noted for his extreme perfectionism which delayed his publications.]] at the hands of **Dr. Erasmus of Rotterdam** (*lib: 6. Apopht: 35.*)—brought it to light...
