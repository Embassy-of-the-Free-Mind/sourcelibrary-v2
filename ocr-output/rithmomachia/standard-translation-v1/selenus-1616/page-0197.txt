# Of the Game of Chess.

[[margin: 5th Chapter:]] ...to seize. Yet he should not easily yield from the **Rook’s square** [[notes: *Rochens felde*; the corner square, such as a1 or h1. This refers to the strategy where a King can force a draw against a Rook-Pawn if he can reach the corner square, as the Pawn cannot promote without the King being stalemated]]. For then the aforementioned **Soldier** [[notes: *Soldat*; the common historical term for a Pawn]] cannot be made into a **Queen**, nor can **Checkmate** be achieved in that manner (since the **King** is merely enclosed [[notes: stalemated]]); thus, the position often helps more than bravery or the enemy’s power.

But when one of the Chess-players—after they have lost many pieces and one has just as many remaining as the other, such that the game is ultimately of equal strength on both sides—picks up the pieces and draws them toward himself: and the other, believing himself wronged thereby (as his pieces were perhaps better positioned than those of the one who cleared the board), says to him: "Have you ended the game? I could still have won it!" and the first player replies by asking what the other would still wager on the game? Whereupon the other offers a **Crown** or **Ducat** [[notes: *Krone* and *Ducaten*; gold coins of significant value in the early modern period]] more, and pledges that he shall win the game: there is then a condition attached, by which he who does not uphold it loses both his first and this final game. Now, the best way to win such a conditional game is this: that one should always take the other’s pieces, exchanging them for his own, to bring the game to a **dead-game** [[notes: *tod-Spiele*; a draw or stalemate]], and thus, by this means, win the staked money (considering the other has obligated and bound himself to achieve something). Therefore, everyone must take care how they accept such a condition. For he who makes such a wager always gives the other an advantage, whether it be at the start of the game or thereafter; it is all the same.

Now, if the game at its end is in such a state that one can perceive and conclude that neither is able to win, and yet one of the players still believes he can prevail, and therefore will not consider the game equal nor begin a new game, but instead moves his pieces back and forth to exhaust the enemy and make him negligent: then, and not before, one should set and decree a term of **fifty counted moves** which the two combatants may still perform. For these moves are sufficient to attempt all means and ways one might have to win. If this term should then prove insufficient to obtain the victory, according to the opinion of distinguished players, one should consider the game equal, take up the pieces, and begin a new one, etc.
