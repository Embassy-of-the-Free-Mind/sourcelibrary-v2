# The Second Book / Chapter 1:

**Checkmate** results in a lost game. However, as can be seen in Chapter 5 below, one can win just as well with a "blind" or unannounced **Checkmate** [[notes: In early modern chess, players often formally announced "Check" or "Checkmate." This passage clarifies that the win is valid even if the player fails to call it out.]], and the same should be held and recognized as a true **Checkmate**. Furthermore, one can deliver **Checkmate** with any piece (except the King). However, when the game reaches its end and only one piece remains alongside the King, one cannot give **Checkmate** (unless it be the **Queen**, a **Soldier** [[notes: "Soldat" refers to a Pawn]] who could be promoted to a **Queen**, or a **Rook** [[notes: The author uses "Roche," an older term for the Rook]]).

Why one only **Checkmates** the King (that is, hunts him and forces him to surrender) but does not capture him like other pieces, may not be easy to explain. We otherwise believe it is not unbelievable that the reason appears to be this: that when men fought in olden times for kingdoms, lands, and people, the heads of state themselves took to the field. This was not so that they themselves should fight (for the military guard was appointed for that; hence *Scipio Africanus*, when he was reproached for not being a good fighter,
[[margin: „]] well replied: *Mater mea, non Bellatorem, sed Imperatorem, me peperit* [[notes: Latin: "My mother did not give birth to a warrior, but to a commander."]]: *Frontinus: lib: 4. stratag: c. 7.* [[notes: Frontinus was a Roman civil engineer and author of a famous book on military stratagems.]]) Why else has
[[margin: „]] Nature denied the **King Bee** a stinger? (*Pliny: lib: 11. c. 7. Natur: hist:*) [[notes: Pliny the Elder was a Roman naturalist. Early naturalists mistakenly believed the Queen Bee was a King.]]) but rather, they were meant only to instill a heart and courage in the entire army to fight knightly against the enemy. Therefore, they are esteemed among their own people just as the soul is in a human body; and upon their person depends the salvation and welfare of the military forces and, at times, the entire country. Now, if it happens that one is attacked by the enemy, a wise regent does not involve himself in the strife at all, but rather—out of love for his soldiers and lands, so that he does not put them in danger—he retreats quite gently and step-by-step into better safety. He continues this for as long as he can, even if the army is almost entirely defeated. Nevertheless, when necessity demands it, he endeavors to do damage to his enemy by cautious striking down—especially if the enemy has pressed in too close without reinforcements or quite thirstily, or if he encounters the enemy on the flanks (for he never offers his head to an enemy for the reasons mentioned).
