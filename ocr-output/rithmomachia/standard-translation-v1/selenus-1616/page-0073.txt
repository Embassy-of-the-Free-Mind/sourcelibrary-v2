### The Second Part / led by Polydore Vergil (Book 2, Chapter 13 of *On the Inventors of Things* [[notes: Polydore Vergil (c. 1470–1555) was an Italian humanist priest who wrote a famous history of "firsts" and origins]]), who believes that this game is called Chess or the "Game of Stones" because of the pieces, which are nowadays called *Scacos*. His words are as follows:

> *Today, these counters, or little stones, or "thieves" with which we play-fight (for it is a contest resembling a battle), are called "Scacos," perhaps named from the word "scandere" [to climb], because the pieces, when moved toward the opponent’s side, seem to climb upward.*

->That is:<-

> The pieces used in this game (which is not unlike a battle) are called Chess-pieces today, perhaps from the Latin word **scandere** [[notes: Latin for "to climb" or "to ascend"]], because the pieces, when they are moved toward the opponent’s side, appear as if they are climbing upward.

This opinion might seem to many to be a bit of a stretch. Therefore, others seek the origin of the name from the way the word is used within the game itself.

However, we believe that the name of a thing should primarily be researched and derived from its nature and manner as much as possible. In this regard, Vitus Amerbach [[notes: Vitus Amerbach (1503–1557) was a German humanist and theologian]], in his *Exposition of Book 2 of Ovid's Tristia*, may have hit the mark best when he writes about this name in this way:

> *The Game of Little Mercenaries [Ludus Latrunculorum] is now called the Game of Chess [Ludus Scachorum], perhaps named from "fugando" [chasing or putting to flight], if we may take the etymology from our own language. For there is, within it, a certain image of a battle.*

->That is:<-

> The game which the Romans called **Ludum Latrunculorum** [[notes: As mentioned on the previous page, this literally means the "Game of Little Mercenaries"]] is now called the Game of Chess; perhaps (if one wishes to take the origin of this word from our German language) named because of the **Schacken**—the driving and chasing away (of which a further report shall follow below) of the opponent's pieces. For hidden within it is the image of a battle and the routing of the enemy.
