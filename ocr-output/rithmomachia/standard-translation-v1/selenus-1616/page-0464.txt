### The Fourth Book / Chapter 9

Yet we cannot leave unmentioned here that in the aforementioned **County of Ascania**, the town of **Aschersleben** bears a **chessboard** in its coat of arms; therefore, a portion of it is also recorded in the coat of arms of the **Princes of Anhalt** (as Counts of Ascania). Presumably, the **game of chess** was in common use and held in no small regard in that place in former times, more so than in the single village of **Ströbeck**. This also lends no small support to our opinion (namely, that the **Germans**, by virtue of Chapter 2 in the 1st Book, pages 39 and 43, must have been good chess players since years immemorial). Accordingly, it is not to be wondered at that their neighbors, the **Slavs** or **Wends** [[notes: The Wends are a historical Slavic population living near Germanic settlement areas]], also showed themselves to be somewhat knowledgeable of the game of chess at that time: in that they may have given the name **Rochlitz** to a town in **Upper Lusatia** [[notes: A historical region in Germany and Poland]] because of it. Indeed, **Matthæus Dresserus**, in *descriptione Urbium Germaniæ* [[notes: "Description of the Cities of Germany," a historical geographical work]], almost conjectures this in the following words:

> *Rochlicium vel a Slavis sic nominatum, quasi Ludus Latruncularius, quo delectata fuerunt plurimæ gentes. Cujus notam in Insigni suo gerit oppidum.* That is: **Rochlitz** was named as such by the **Wends** because of the **game of chess**, which many nations have delighted in using: just as the town still bears a **Rook** [[notes: "Rochen" in German, referring to the chess piece]] in its coat of arms because of it.

However, the **Smaller Game** arose from the aforementioned **Extensive Game** in the following manner:

The board of this **Courier Game**, or **Great Chess**, was turned: the uppermost part of it was shifted to the side, and thus the outermost **white square** (as it is still in use today, and perhaps still observed because of this change) was placed on the **right hand**. Four cross-lines, or **thirty-two squares**, were separated from it and brought into a square space. Sixteen pieces (namely **eight Soldiers**, **four Couriers**, **two Men**, and **two Sneakers**) were removed, and the entire game was drawn closer together in the number of its squares and pieces.

This game has been described by **Fr. Jacobus de Casallis** [[notes: Likely referring to Jacobus de Cessolis, a 13th-century monk whose chess sermon was a medieval bestseller]], **Fr. Conradus von Ammenhusen**, and **Dr. Jacob Mennel**. It is called the **Old Chess Game** by the inhabitants of **Ströbeck**, and without the sixteen named pieces, it is played just like the extensive **Courier Game**.
