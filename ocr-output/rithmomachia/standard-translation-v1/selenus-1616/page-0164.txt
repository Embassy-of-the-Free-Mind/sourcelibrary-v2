Header: The Second Book / 2. cap:

...as will be heard in more detail in the following third, as well as from the sixth Chapter, of this book.

There are, however, only two players. Since there are only two Kings, one requires no more than two leaders who shall represent them. He who leads the White King with the White pieces [[notes: The German text uses "Steinen" (stones), which was the common term for chess pieces]] is called the White player; he who leads the Black King with his set [[notes: "Zeuge," meaning his equipment or array of pieces]] is called the Black player. They alone rule these two hosts: unless one should undertake to play against two or more, or allow the victor [[notes: "Segener," referring here to the person currently winning or the person whose turn it is]] to seek counsel from another.

Here it is to be remembered that none of the spectators should be of counsel to one or the other player with words or winks (since they, through the mere act of watching, already gain no small benefit and can learn to be on their guard), but should rather attend the game in silence and—so that they might later, if any *disputat* [[notes: A formal or informal dispute/argument regarding the rules]] should arise among the players, be able to intervene more fittingly and with less suspicion—conduct themselves in all things as neutral and impartial. For otherwise, they would not be permitted as judges; rather, one would much more rightfully demand compensation from them for any damage arising therefrom. If a game occurs for pleasure, however, and not for the sake of profit [[notes: Referring to games played for stakes or money]], then a spectator may certainly share good advice with the one who is the least skilled (so that he might become more experienced, and the other, because he has so many players against him, might observe his own moves all the better and more diligently): considering that a spectator can commonly perceive an error much sooner

->than one of the players<-
->can perceive it.<-
