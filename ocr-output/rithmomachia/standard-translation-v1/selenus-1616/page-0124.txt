### The First Book / Chapter 10:

» [to] the decorators and the mint-masters) [who build] walls around the city, towers, cellars, chambers [[notes: *Kematen*, from the Latin *caminata*, referring to heated living quarters or private rooms]], treasures, and many other things that are useful and good for the human race: and since men of all kinds—Kings, Princes, Nobles, and Counts, Knights and Servants, citizens and peasants—are in some way subject to these [workers], they therefore require truly faithful people. For whoever loses his loyalty [[notes: *Trew*, encompassing faithfulness, integrity, and reliability]] has nothing more that he can lose. He cannot dare to speak joyfully with any man, for no one can or should believe him. Loyalty is the most holy good that the heart possesses, which no necessity can force to deceive, and which neither wealth nor sorrow can separate. And it is a great foolishness [[notes: *kintheit*, literally "childishness"]] that you should trust someone in the same hour that you have already been deceived by them.

» The **Craftsmen** [[notes: *Werckleut*, referring here to the second Pawn in the allegory, which includes smiths, carpenters, and other builders]] are good for the world and very useful; just as everything that grows and springs from the earth exists for the sake of man, and grows for man’s sake, it is arranged so that here one helps the other. Thus, we should follow Nature, so that we are diligent in the common good above all things. The first seed of justice is that one promotes the common good; for there is a proverb: It concerns you when your neighbor’s wall is burning [[notes: A vernacular version of the Latin proverb *tua res agitur, paries cum proximus ardet*, meaning that a crisis nearby will soon become your own problem]].

» 2. These same **Craftsmen** should possess wisdom and truth, so that one does not envy the other, nor harbor suspicions that are not true. God created the world such that it desires two things: holiness and wisdom; and one cannot exist without the other. He deceives himself who says he is spiritual and holy without wisdom, or that he is wise without being holy. For it properly belongs to a man who is wise that he does nothing for which he must repent, nor should he be a hypocrite. His deeds should be deliberate and not hasty, pleasing and honorable; and he should possess none of these things: envy, nor hate, nor suspicion. For whoever envies another is small; but whoever envies no one is great. When the envious man speaks, he talks of his neighbor’s luck: saying his crops are more beautiful, his pigs are fatter, and his cattle are more useful. They should also not be suspicious, for whoever is suspicious thinks everyone is as unfaithful as he is, and would rage against everyone, for he himself is senseless. They should also have undaunted strength, and especially the **Sailors** [[notes: *Schiffleut*; the allegory often groups sailors and merchants with the third or fourth pawns, but here the text emphasizes the shared virtue of "strength" or "fortitude"]], so that they...
