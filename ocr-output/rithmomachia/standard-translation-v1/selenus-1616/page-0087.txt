# Of the Game of Chess.

[[margin: Chapter 3.]] [[notes: Although the previous page was marked as Chapter 5, the margin here notes Chapter 3; this may refer to a specific subsection of the "First Book" or a numbering error in the original manuscript.]] ...[brave]ry: the Knight; victory: the Administrator [[notes: *Verweser*. In this treatise, the Rook is often depicted as an Administrator or Governor who maintains the King’s lands.]]; power and strength: the Queen; chastity and the unity of all the people: all this is given to honor the King. When he first rises from his place, he goes to the third field [[notes: This refers to the "King’s Leap," an ancient chess rule that allowed the King to move two squares on his first move to speed up the game's opening.]]; this signifies that the first perfection begins with three, for one and two make three, and doubling three makes six. This signifies the six persons who are named highly upon the chessboard [[notes: *SchachZabel* refers to the board itself.]], who create a true perfection: these are the King, the Queen, the Judge [[notes: *Richter*, the piece we now call the Bishop.]], the Knight, the Administrator, and the Commoners. The King moves in the manner of them all, and thus he should possess the perfection of them all. And when the King moves from his place, he may lead the Queen with him; this signifies that women should not make vows or promises without the husband's will—neither for church-festivals nor for pilgrimages. If it happens that she makes a vow or promise and the husband objects, she may not fulfill it without his will. But the husband may well make a vow and promise, and he may also do and fulfill it without the wife's will. However, if she does not wish to be without him, she may travel with him. This is because the wife shall be subject to the husband's command, and because the husband is a head of the wife, but the wife is not the husband's head. Yet, according to the debt of marriage [[notes: This refers to the *debitum coniugale*, a medieval theological and legal concept where spouses had mutual rights and obligations to one another's bodies.]], each of them has power over their own body; rather, the wife’s body belongs to the husband, and the husband’s body belongs to the wife. And for this reason, he shall lead her with him and give her what is hers if she demands it of him. And when the King goes forward, the Queen may go with him; but if the Queen goes forward, the King remains if it pleases him. For his following move, he goes only to the nearest field; this signifies that as long as he is in his realm, he is safe. But when he comes out of it, he should move safely, for he alone is more useful than a thousand others; and if he alone were captured or slain, the power of the army would be entirely destroyed. And for this reason, the Kings do not dare to go to one another; thus they cannot harm one another [[notes: In chess, two Kings can never stand on adjacent squares, meaning one King can never "capture" the other.]]. And when all his people are captured, he has no one with whom he might save himself to keep the victory. For what would a King rejoice in, who has no one over whom he is King, and who is subject and obedient to him? It is a vain boast...
