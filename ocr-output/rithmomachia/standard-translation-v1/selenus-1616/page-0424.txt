### The Fourth Book / Chapter 3:

...takes the same with the **King**, or with his **Rook**: **White** moves the **King’s Knight** (out of danger) to fourteen [[notes: f2]].

[[margin: 24.]]
If **White**, however, places the **Queen** (after **Black** has moved the **King’s Knight** to 52 [[notes: d7]]) on 24 [[notes: h3]]: (no. 9) then **Black** moves the **King’s Knight** to the **White King’s Soldier** [[notes: the pawn]] on thirty-five [[notes: c5]]: **White** (to protect it) moves the **Queen** to six [[notes: f1]]: **Black** gives **Check** with the **Queen** from twenty-five [[notes: a4]]: and thus causes a bad game for **White**, which he can easily lose if he does not know how to take sufficient care.

It is therefore not useful for **White**, although it may have the appearance of a good game, to play in such a manner, ra-
[[margin: 25.]]
-ther, * he should let the **Black King’s Knight** remain on his square (no. 5) and (in order to bring his pieces into the field) give **Check** with the **King’s Bishop** [[notes: literally "Schützen" or marksman]] from thirty-nine [[notes: g5]]: If **Black** then covers the **King** with the (aforementioned) **King’s Knight** on fifty-three [[notes: e7]] or forty-six [[notes: f6]]: then **White** (to protect the **King’s Soldier** [[notes: pawn]]) moves the **King’s Knight** to twelve [[notes: d2]]: and will thus give no cause for the exchange of both **Queens** (as happened in no. 2), but will rather prevent it through other useful moves.
