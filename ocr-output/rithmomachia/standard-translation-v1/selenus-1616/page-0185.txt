# ->On the Game of Chess<-

[[margin: 3 cap:]] ...[the opponent should not be considered to have] lost the game. Although it appears impolite in such a skillful and serious game not to better guard one's **position** [[notes: *schantze*; originally a military term for a breastwork or entrenchment, here referring to one's tactical advantage or safety]], and to use such thoughtless words, or to boast of **triumph** even before victory is achieved: it is nonetheless contrary to the nature of this game. It should be considered the same whether he says **Check** or **Checkmate**, provided that he properly observes the customs of the game in his moves. For one is not so strictly bound to the words if one is certain of the matter itself: which will clearly tell the **King** whether he can move forward or not. Just as this miserable, trifling, and **"mate"** syllable [[notes: *matte sylbe*; the author is making a pun. *Matt* means "mate" in chess, but in German it also means "weak," "faint," or "exhausted." He is arguing that the spoken word has no power if the chess piece itself doesn't actually provide the threat]] can little frighten him or cause him harm, if the **Piece** [[notes: *Stein*; literally "stone"]] itself is not found capable of it. Thus, the player is free to use this little word or—without any fear of losing the victory—to omit it. One must not draw or formulate judgment from slippery words, but much more from the lawful matter itself.

(2.) That one should take care toward the end to always leave the **King** his freedom, so that he may be moved and not **shut in** [[notes: *eingesperret*; this refers to stalemate. In the 17th century, the rules for stalemate varied by region; in some places, it was a draw, while in others, it was a win for the player who was stalemated or a "lesser" victory for the one who caused it. The author prefers a clean Checkmate]]: so that he can be given **Checkmate** according to the custom of the game, and victory can thus be achieved. Further information on this is to be found in the first Chapter of this book, in the section where the confinement of the King is discussed.

(3.) That some are of the opinion that this game should be played out in fifty moves at the longest, and **Checkmate** given, or failing that, the pieces should be picked up and the game abandoned and surrendered. However, they are to be informed that while it is true that one can deliver **Checkmate** not only in that many moves, but even in fewer: they should consider that it is the same situation (as when a **King**, who remains in a strong **fortress**, sends word from the fortress to the camp of another **King** who has besieged him for 10, 20, 30, 40, or 50 days: that since he has besieged him for so and so long and could not overcome him, he should simply retreat and depart. The other King outside, however, does not accept this, but if he cannot win the fortress in 50 days, he intends to spend 50 weeks or 50 months, etc., until he has it in his power) as in the Game of Chess. For if one cannot mate the other on the fiftieth move, he may well try for a hundred or more moves, and is not bound to any certain number. Sometimes players spend a whole day on one game; sometimes they also take five...
