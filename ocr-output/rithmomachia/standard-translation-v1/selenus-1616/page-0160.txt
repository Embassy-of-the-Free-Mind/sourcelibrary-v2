It consists, however, in this: that the place where the **King** is positioned be well-fortified and, as it were, entrenched [[notes: "beschantzet," referring to the construction of "Schantzen" or earthwork fortifications]] with both the major and the lesser **pieces** [[notes: "Steine," literally "stones," the standard term for chess pieces at the time]] (all of which must be prepared and ready for this purpose). This is done so that the **King** may be as far as possible from the enemy's incursions, and also so that his own **pieces** may perform their movements more freely and boldly, coming to his aid more quickly and holding off the enemy with better advantage and opportunity to do him harm. Without doubt, the inventor intended to indicate by this how a **Potentate** [[notes: A monarch or ruler with great power]] who wishes to invade another's land must first protect his own country and fortresses and garrison them as necessity requires. How these fortifications of ours can be made is explained further at the beginning of the 10th Chapter in the final Book.

The **defense** of the **King** rests upon this: that when he is charged and given **Check** [[notes: "geschachet"]] by the enemy, he either gives way to an empty square or to the same square where the opponent's **piece**, which he has cleared away, previously stood; or he may move one of his own **pieces** forward—provided they cannot capture the piece giving the **Check**—and thus prevent the **Check**. This moving forward [[notes: "fürzihen," or interposing a piece]], however, cannot always happen: for when someone unexpectedly presents himself with his **Knight** [[notes: "Reuter," literally "Rider"]], or gives **Check** with a **Pawn** [[notes: "Soldat," literally "Soldier"]] that is protected by another **piece**, one cannot place anything between that piece and the **King**, but must instead either move the **King** away or capture this **Knight** or **Pawn**. But when a **Double Check** [[notes: "doppelter Schach"]] is given to the **King**, he cannot be protected or guarded against them in any other way—since he is being attacked by two enemies at one time and from different places—than by either taking flight and seeking another place where he can obtain help and assistance, or by slaying one of them with his own hand, if he is able, and thus moving out of the way of the other. In former times [[notes: "Vorzeiten," referring to older medieval rules]], if it was his first move, the **King** could well **Leap** [[notes: The "King's Leap" was a precursor to modern castling, allowing the King to move two or three squares on his first move]] into a third square (even if various **Checks** had been given to him before, which he had covered with his pieces, and he had not yet moved from his original place).
