and [he also] wished to set [the authors’] own words, both domestic and **Foreign**, into this **Treatise** where it was necessary and fitting, as his own—though not without reason.

He has particularly cited the words of the **Old Germans** [[notes: Referring to Medieval or Middle High German texts, which would have looked archaic and quaint to a 17th-century reader.]] for the reader’s pleasure (should the reader perhaps take delight in such a language), and further, to make understood the diligent industry of the same [ancestors], and how highly they endeavored to apply this **Game** to serious matters and to common civic and courtly life. At the same time, he also wished to incidentally remind [the reader]: that should these words sound too harsh to perhaps subtle ears, or should someone else wish to apply himself only to the main work without loss of time: the same person may well omit what is marked with this sign (**»**) from the 6th Chapter to the 11th of the First Book of this **Treatise**, after the conclusion of each **Chapter**, and thus proceed more swiftly.

He felt it necessary to primarily use the own words of **Foreign Authors** in the first two chapters of the 1st Book, so that (1) no one would have cause to doubt them; and (2) in searching through the **Authors**, if their words had not been placed here, the reader would not be led too early, and contrary to his intent, from this entertaining art into serious study. Finally, (3) to accommodate those who might be in a foreign land or otherwise could not look them up due to pressing business, or because they did not possess the books or did not understand them in their language (for which reason most of the cited texts have been translated into **German**), and yet wished to draw water from the right **Fountainhead** [[notes: A metaphor for original sources or primary texts.]] but were unable to look them up.
