# On the Game of Chess.

->E iij<-

[[margin: 5.cap:]] ...has **soldiers** [[notes: *Soldaten*; pawns]], then one should strive and endeavor to give a **Knight** [[notes: *Reuter*; literally "Rider"]] or **Bishop** [[notes: *Schütze*; literally "Archer" or "Shooter"]] for both **soldiers**, or even just for one (whereby the game then becomes equal). One could then, perhaps with opportunity and advantage at the right time, exchange the pieces, and should not put oneself in any danger on account of this trade. For although one is not able (by virtue of the 7th Chapter in the First Book, and the 1st Chapter of this book) to give a **checkmate** with only one piece alongside the **King**—excepting the **Queen** and **Rooks** [[notes: *Rochen*; the traditional term for Rooks, derived from the Persian 'rukh']]—this game can nevertheless, as long as only one **soldier** is taken, sometimes still be won. Namely: when one side takes the **soldier** that stands on the flank, blocks the way of the other **soldier** with his primary piece, and thus, without hindrance, makes a **Queen** out of his own **soldier** and gives **checkmate** with her.

If, then, one of the **chess-players** [[notes: *Schach-Zieher*; literally "chess-puller," a common period term for a player]] cannot win the game with his pieces, he should at the very least endeavor to delay the other and ward off the **checkmate**. For example: when one has only a **Rook**, but the other still has a **Rook** and a **Knight**: the former (so that the latter may not win, or ever be able to trap his **King**) should proceed in such a way that he places his **Rook**, as far as he can, in the middle of the **chess-board**, and keeps the other's **King** away with constant **checking**. Now, if that player covers his **King** with his **Knight**, the first player must keep the **King** and **Knight** enclosed and captured as long as he can. But if the **King** emerges from behind the aforementioned **Knight**, he should continue to pursue him with **checking**. Otherwise, the one with the **Rook** and **Knight**, provided he still intends to win, must take good care that he is not himself enclosed, does not lose the **Rook**, or that the other **Rook** is not traded for the other **King** [[notes: This likely refers to a forced exchange of Rooks]]. For when the **Rooks** are exchanged, one cannot give a **checkmate** with the **Knight** alone; rather, the game remains equal, and according to the custom of some places, either nothing is won, or, as is common in Spain, only half of the stake [[notes: *Aufsatzes*; refers to the wager or pot of money placed on the game's outcome]] is won.

Likewise, when one has only a **Rook**, but the other has a **Rook** and a **Bishop**: the latter can only with difficulty win against the former, provided the one with the single **Rook** plays sharply.
