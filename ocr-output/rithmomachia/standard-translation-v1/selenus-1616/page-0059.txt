# On the Game of Chess.

[[margin: 2nd Chapter:]]
—whoever was meant to throw aimed at that same stone [Penelope] to see if he could hit it. If he then hit or touched this **Penelope** and drove her forward from her place, then his stone was placed back upon the space of the one he had cast out. Once he had replaced **Penelope** in the spot where she stood before, he threw his stone a second time; and if he hit her once more, and touched none of the stones belonging to his companions or opponents (which stood all around her), then he won, and this gave him great hope for marriage with **Penelope**. This happened quite often to **Eurymachus** [[notes: Eurymachus was one of the two leading suitors of Penelope in Homer's *Odyssey*, portrayed as arrogant and manipulative]]; therefore, he entertained the notion that he would one day be the one to marry **Penelope**.

To this subject also belongs the game called **πόλεις** [[notes: *Poleis*, meaning "cities." In this game, the spaces on the board were called "cities" and the pieces were "dogs"]], which is mentioned by **Cratinus** [[notes: An Athenian comic poet]] in *Fugit*, and by **Plato** in Book 4 of the *Republic*; as was already mentioned above in the first chapter from **Erasmus of Rotterdam** [[notes: A famous Renaissance humanist and scholar]], and which **Julius Pollux** [[notes: A Greek grammarian and sophist from the 2nd century AD]] in Book 7 calls *Ludum Latrunculorum* [[notes: The "Game of Mercenaries" or "Little Soldiers," the most famous Roman strategy game, often compared to chess or draughts]]; and it is this very game which corresponds with our Chess—or rather, our Battle-Game.

Furthermore, there was a game called *Ludum quinque Linearum* [[notes: The "Game of Five Lines"]], or more properly **πεττείαν** (*petteia*), as if it were **πεντείαν** (*penteia*) [[notes: The author is making a linguistic connection between the game *petteia* (from *pessos*, a pebble) and the word *pente* (five), suggesting it was named for its five lines]]. (Although this little word is applied to all other games of this kind by **Hesychius** [[notes: A Greek lexicographer from Alexandria]] and others, setting aside the previous allusion, not without reason). One may read of this in the aforementioned book by **Pollux**, and in the works of **Simon Majolus, Bishop of Volturara** [[notes: An Italian bishop and author known for his "Dog Days" (*Diebus Canicularibus*), an encyclopedic work of the late 16th century]], in his *Diebus Canicularibus*, page 576, under the heading **πεσσοί** (*pessoi*).

**Eustathius** [[notes: A 12th-century archbishop and scholar who wrote extensive commentaries on Homer's *Iliad*]], in his commentary on the *Iliad*, folio 490, as well as **Coelius Rhodiginus** [[notes: An Italian scholar of the Renaissance]], Book 20, Chapter 27, mentions yet another game (for which sixty stones were used, namely thirty white and as many black); and this game (as **Julius Pollux** testifies in the aforementioned Chapter 7, page 440, line 25) comes much closer to our Game of Chess. Regardless of the fact that the aforementioned Bishop **Majolus**, on page 576, wishes to attribute it to another game, as follows:

> "But also **Διαγραμμισμός** (*Diagrammismos*), the Game of Lines, or of 60 counters, was played on a diagram described by straight lines; it utilized sixty counters, of which thirty were black and the rest white, moved by placement and shifting. Similar to this seems to be that game played among us with eighteen counters of two colors, arranged in two rows: these are skillfully placed at the corners and the mutual intersections of the straight lines of a square diagram, which consists of three squares of different sizes, described in three parts and transver—" [[notes: The author is quoting a Latin description of a game that sounds like an expanded version of Nine Men's Morris or a similar alignment game. The text cuts off mid-word "transversa" and continues on the next page.]]
