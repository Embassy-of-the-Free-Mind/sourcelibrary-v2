### The First Book / Chapter 10:

...and their rights / so that they take no more / than they should: For no man / who has won through excessive luck / has long joy from it.

The **eighth pawn** [[notes: the text uses *Wend*, a term for the pawn, which here represents the lowest social class]] on the **chessboard** [[notes: *SchachZabel*, from the Latin *tabula*, was the standard German name for the game at this time]] shall rightly stand before the **Rook** that stands at the King’s left hand, for he signifies **gamblers**, **scolders**, and **scoundrels**. These shall make known to the land whatever may bring shame and harm to a **governor** or **administrator** [[notes: *Vistum* or Vicedom was a high-ranking administrative official, similar to a deputy or governor]]. When these **gamblers** have gambled until they are bare and naked, they are then quickly ready to carry **messages** through the land and seek out the property of others. He shall also be depicted on the chessboard in such a way that he has **broad red hair** [[notes: in medieval symbolism, red hair was often associated with a deceptive or unstable nature]], and shall have in his right hand a few **pennies**, signifying the **greedy**, and in the left hand **three dice**, signifying **players** and **scoundrels**. Under his belt, he shall have a **letter-case** or **box**, which signifies **runners** and **couriers**. 

The first of these, who are called **wastrels** [[notes: *gusar*, likely referring to spendthrifts or those who "guzzle" their inheritance]] and destroyers of their own property—their lord and guardian should take charge over their estate and make them powerless over their goods. For when it happens that they have squandered and consumed their inheritance, they cannot work; thus they become **murderers**, **robbers**, **arsonists**, and **evildoers**. **Greed** is a vice and a great misdeed, for although it may benefit one’s friends for a time, it ultimately harms one’s neighbor. It is therefore better to keep what one has than to strive for what one does not have. And regarding this, there is a common **proverb**: "He who spends more than he has, becomes poor—of that there is no doubt." For he who does not manage his expenses will perish before he even notices it. 

After these are certain **gamblers** and **scoundrels** who wickedly consume their property, and they then become robbers, murderers, and traitors. It is foolish to covet another's goods once one has consumed one's own; such people are not fit to be **judges** or among the **wise men** of the community. Next are the **couriers**, who should be diligent in delivering their messages by the time their lords have sent them and commanded them to return. This is so that no other messenger arrives before them to present a counter-case and displace them in their business.
