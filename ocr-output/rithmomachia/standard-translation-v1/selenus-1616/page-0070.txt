## The First Book / 2. Chapter:

As for what concerns other / individual persons / who have distinguished themselves / in this game / above others: one will find / in Homer’s *Odyssey*, Book 1 [[notes: Homer describes the suitors of Penelope playing a game with stones, often interpreted by Renaissance scholars as an early form of chess]], the aforementioned suitors / or wooers: In Euripides’ *Iphigenia in Aulis*, Protesilaus and the Ajaces, who / occupied themselves / with the *pellsis* [[notes: Likely a printer's error or variation of *pessoi*, the Greek term for game pieces or "stones"]], or chess-pieces. Likewise / the mentioned Justus Ecloviensis testifies / of Augustus and Verus, both Roman Emperors: Maecenas, and Q. Galba, that they / were good chess-players. Scaevola is mentioned by Valerius Maximus, Book 8, Chapter 8. Mention was made / shortly before / of Charlemagne.

Of Pope Leo the Tenth / Paulus Jovius, Bishop of Nocera, writes in Book 4 of his biography, page 86, as follows:

> „ *He always condemned gambling as a plague to domestic affairs and character; however, at Chess [[notes: The Latin text uses "Latrunculis," an ancient Roman game often used as the Latin name for Chess in this period]], he played so subtly and sharply, even with the most practiced players, that no one seemed able to equal him, whether in skill or speed, in joining or concluding a battle.* That is:
> „ Pope Leo / the tenth of that name / always hated and rejected / the game of dice / as being / to a person / in his conduct and livelihood / very harmful / and, as it were, a pestilential poison: But Chess / he favored / [playing] very shrewdly / even with the most practiced masters / and indeed was far superior to almost all / in experience and skill / in conducting this battle of the stones / and in obtaining victory / with swift haste.
