### Chapter 7: On the Game of Chess.

the Black player (to put the White player to the test) moves the King’s Bishop’s Soldier [[note: *Schützens-Soldat*, literally "Shooter’s Soldier," the Pawn in front of the Bishop.]] to forty-six: The White player takes the Black King’s Soldier with his King’s Knight [[note: *Reuter*, literally "Rider."]]: The Black player moves the Queen against him to fifty-two: The White player moves the same [[the Knight]] toward the Black King’s Rook at fifty-one: The Black player takes the White King’s Soldier with the Queen, offers check, and moves her onto the [square of] the White King’s Bishop [[note: Likely square f2, if following the standard numbering of the era.]]: The White player (so that he does not lose the same [[pawn or position]]) covers the King with the Queen on twelve: The Black player takes her with his Queen: The White player takes the same with the King: The Black player moves the King’s Soldier to the White King’s Bishop’s [square] at thirty-seven: The White player takes it with the same [[his own pawn]] and protects his King’s Knight once again: The Black player takes the same [[the protecting pawn]] with the King’s Bishop’s Soldier: The White player [takes] the Black King’s Rook with the King’s Knight: The Black player (since this White Knight cannot be released from captivity [[note: *auß der haft*, a colorful way to describe a piece that is trapped and cannot escape the corner.]] again) wins a piece to his advantage, and victory will surely fall to him, provided he is well-instructed in the moves.

But if the White player plays in another manner: then the Black player must always take good heed of his game and proceed in good order (though it [[the position]] cannot be made so free and unhindered by this path as in the other manner: therefore they are not to be compared with one another) and show himself [ready].
