# On the Game of Chess. 13

[[margin: Chapter 1]]
...witnessed/ been/ and whose language, as **David Chytræus** [[notes: David Chytræus (1530–1600) was a prominent German Lutheran theologian and historian]], in *Chronicon Saxoniae*, Book 1, page 3, and **Reinerus Reineccius** [[notes: Reinerus Reineccius (1541–1595) was a German historian specializing in biblical and classical genealogies]], in his little treatise on the early origin of the people of Meissen, page 38, state (have used), was well understood: as can be gathered from them, and from the following words of **Olaus Magnus** [[notes: Olaus Magnus (1490–1557) was a Swedish archbishop and writer, famous for his *Historia de Gentibus Septentrionalibus*, which described the customs of Northern Europe]], the Goth, Archbishop of Uppsala (*On the Diverse Exercises of Men*, Book 15, Chapters 12 and 13):

> Since nothing is more difficult in human affairs than to understand the minds and affections of men, by what judgment they are led, or to look insightfully into how those immersed in all kinds of pleasures or sorrows are able to moderate their passions: it is therefore the custom among the more illustrious Goths and Swedes, when about to place their daughters in honorable marriage, to examine the minds of the suitors with wondrous skill and passion, especially in the **Game of Little Soldiers**, or **Chess** [[notes: *Ludo Latrunculorum* was an ancient Roman board game, but by the Renaissance, the term was frequently used by scholars as a Latin name for Chess]]. For in that game, anger, love, petulance, greed, sloth, laziness, and many other foolish passions and movements of the mind, as well as the forces and properties of fortune, are accustomed to be demonstrated: namely, whether the suitor is of a rustic mind, such that he suddenly exults indiscreetly in triumph, or whether he knows how to endure injuries inflicted more cautiously, or to turn them away more modestly, etc. And having seen these signs, provident parents moderate the marriages of their dear children, to be more discreetly embraced or rejected. *That is;*

> Because the spirits, senses, and thoughts of men—and what they carry in their shields [[notes: "To carry something in one's shield" (*etwas im Schilde führen*) is a German idiom meaning to have secret intentions or a hidden agenda]]—can be most difficultly noted and felt in this human life; also, how they know to dispose and behave themselves in excessive good or bad fortune, and to be precisely observed: therefore, the most noble families among the Goths and Swedes have had the custom, when they intended to marry off their daughters, to explore and put to the test the spirits of those who have wooed them in many ways and manners, but especially through the **Stone-** or **Chess-Game**. For, in the same, anger, love, lightheadedness, greed, negligence, and laziness: also other more foolish movements of the spirit, alongside the power and properties of Fortune, allow themselves to be seen and heard.
