### The First Book. Chapter 1.

**Muhammad**, the Prince of the **Saracens**, in his **Quran** [[notes: *Alcoran*]], or Book of Laws, in the 3rd and 13th **Suras** [[notes: *Azoarâ* — the chapters of the Quran]], decries this game as a sin. Yet the **Turks** pay as little heed to this command as their Emperor **Suleiman** [[notes: Suleiman the Magnificent, r. 1520–1566]] did to the law by which the making of images or portraits was forbidden. When he took a desire to have his likeness painted by a Netherlandish master, **Pieter Coecke van Aelst** (as **Matthias Quad**, etc., records at the end of the 94th chapter of *The Glory of the German Nation*), he ignored the ban. It is much the same as how, in former times, the **Mamluks** [[notes: *Mammelucken* — a ruling caste of Muslim knights]] viewed the prohibition of their prophet **Muhammad** against the drinking of wine (as **Peter Martyr d'Anghiera** testifies in Book 3 of *The Babylonian Embassy*, page 409).

Others bring forward the laws of the Emperor **Justinian** from the 123rd *Novel*, chapter 10, and *Auth: interdicimus, C. de Episc: & Cler:* [[notes: These are citations to the *Corpus Juris Civilis*, the foundational body of Roman law]]. They cite the word ταβλίζειν (*tablizein*), or *ad tabulas ludere*, which is to say: to play at a table [[notes: *Tafel* — specifically referring to board games played with dice, like backgammon]]. By this, it seems the game of **Chess**—or the **Chess-Board Game** [[notes: *Schach-Zabel-Spiel*]]—is likewise meant. However, as to what it actually signifies, **Jacques Cujas** [[notes: *Cujac:* — a famous 16th-century French legal scholar]] writes in his exposition of said *Novel 123*. Moreover (to say nothing of other excuses), it is manifest from the same Emperor's law and constitution—contained in *l. 34. §. 1. C. de Episc: aud:*—that our game is not meant at all. For while a similar prohibition is found there, the reasons given for it do not rhyme with the game of **Chess** in any way, nor can they be twisted to apply to it. We have already mentioned above, in the discussion of the second property of the game, what [[gloss: the Gloss]] [[notes: *die Gl:* — the standard medieval legal commentary on Roman law]] and other noble jurists hold regarding **Chess**.

Thirdly, some wish to "knock the bottom right out of the barrel" [[notes: *dem Fas den Boden gar außschlagen* — a German idiom meaning to settle an argument decisively or go to extremes]] by pretending that the intended delight and refreshment of the mind cannot be found in this game. On the contrary, they claim that besides the loss of noble and precious time, which must be spent in learning the game but could be more usefully applied to other things, one finds only burden and over-exertion. But these critics do not look upon the **Art**, or the accepted and established manner of the Master, who, without any annoyance and according to his heart's desire, always finds his joy and frequently-mentioned, magnificent delight within it. They deserve to hear in reply what **Henricus Portius**, [[term: J. C.]] [[notes: *Juris Consultus* — a legal consultant or counselor]], says in his 2nd Book of Epigrams:

> ” *Inscius Artis erit, qui veram spreverit Artem,*
> ” *Nam quae nota minus, sunt quoq; grata minus.* That is:
> ” What one has not rightly experienced,
> ” One seldom gives good counsel upon.
