# The First Book / Chapter 1:

> » Now after **Sadoc**, an Arab (the chief warden [[notes: *Wacht-Meister* refers to the Castellan or Captain of the Guard]] of the tower in which the beautiful **Bianco-fiore** was held prisoner), had gone under the vault of the tower with **Florio** (who was the son of King **Filicis** in Spain and the lover of the imprisoned maiden) or **Philocopo** (as he called himself then), and they held many and various conversations with one another, **Philocopus**, seeing an exquisite **Chess-Board** hanging on the wall in a corner, said to the warden: whether he liked to play chess [[notes: *den Schach zöge* — literally "to move/draw at chess"]], since he saw such a beautiful board hanging there? The **Sadoc** answered that it was indeed so: For he amused himself with it at times, and desired to know from him if he also understood how to play? Whereupon **Philocopus** gave him his answer: He knew a little bit about it. "Well then," said **Sadoc**, "so shall we play with one another until the heat passes by [[notes: *biß die hitze... für über schleiche* — literally "until the heat creeps past," referring to the midday Mediterranean sun]] and you can ride back into the city." "That pleases me quite well," answered **Philocopus**. Hereupon, as **Sadoc** spread out several tapestries in a cool, fresh vault and had the chess-board brought, the one on this side and the other on that side sat down opposite each other. The **Chess-Pieces** [[notes: *Schach-Steine* — literally "chess stones"]] were arranged on both sides: The game began, and from both parties—so that it would not look altogether too childish—a considerable number of certain **golden pennies** [[notes: *guldener Pfenninge* — these refer to gold bezants, the currency used for the stakes]] were laid down, which were to go to the winner for his profit and share. Accordingly, these two players sat down, as mentioned, with differing thoughts: for the one wished to win the staked money, but the other desired to gain the friendship and intimacy of the warden (to better advance his plan to enter the tower) and thus intended to lose willingly. **Philocopus** soon noticed in the first moves that he understood the game better than his opponent, the warden. He then pressed the warden’s **Chess-King** sharply with one of his **Rooks** [[notes: *Rochen* — the Rook or Castle, from the Persian *rukh*]] and **Knights** [[notes: *Reuter* — literally "Riders"]], practically cornering him; and the King had, on the left side, a **Bishop** [[notes: *Schützen oder Alten* — In early German chess, the Bishop was called either the "Archer" (*Schütze*) or "Old Man" (*Alte*), a translation of the Arabic *Al-fil* or elephant.]] or Al-
