[[meta: This page continues a prefatory Latin poem addressed to the author (writing under the pseudonym Selenus). It transitions from the previous page's discussion of the general history of games to a personal tribute to the author’s multifaceted skills in both physical horsemanship and intellectual pursuits like chess.]]

Either you urge on the courses of swift horses,
Or guide them, turning with an easy rein;
Whether you once contended with javelins, sword, and spear,
Or now, from a crown you won,
Carry the triumph of the hanging ring [[notes: A reference to "tilting at the ring," a popular Renaissance tournament sport where a rider at full gallop attempts to catch a small suspended ring on the tip of their lance.]];
Or, free from care, you now play the battles
Of the more playful *Latrunculorum* [[notes: A Latin name for Chess, literally meaning the "Game of Little Soldiers." While it originally referred to an ancient Roman board game, Renaissance scholars used the term for Chess.]];
Or you yourself attempt to explain these things
More clearly to **US** in this charming, new little book,
Just now polished with dry pumice [[notes: A classical literary allusion to the final editing and smoothing of a manuscript; in antiquity, pumice was used to smooth the rough edges of parchment rolls.]],
In this **QUIETER LIFE** which you enjoy.
From this it is clear enough, that in a **HARDER TIME**
(If by chance Erynnis [[notes: In Greek and Roman mythology, the Furies (Erynyes) were deities of vengeance; here, she represents the personification of the madness of war.]], that cruel **GODDESS**,
Should infest our native shores with a fiercer battle),
You show how well-prepared you are,
With a martial mind and a sharp heart,
O bravest of the descendants of **Tuisto**,
Joined to **Mannus** by an ancient lineage [[notes: According to the Roman historian Tacitus, Tuisto and his son Mannus were the mythical progenitors of the Germanic peoples. The poet is emphasizing the author's noble German heritage.]].

->*Matthias Börtius, Licentiate of Both Laws*<-
