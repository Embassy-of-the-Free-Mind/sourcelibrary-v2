# Chapter 4: On the Game of Chess.

**Black**—if the **White** moves the **King’s Knight’s Pawn** [[notes: *K. Reuters Soldaten*—the author refers to pawns as "soldiers" belonging to a specific piece.]] to **thirty-four** and then sets it on **thirty-seven**: [[notes: The author uses a numbering system for the board (1–64) common in 17th-century manuals like that of Gustavus Selenus, rather than modern algebraic notation (e.g., e4, d5).]] the **White** now can take the **Black King’s Pawn** either with the **Queen** or with the **King’s Bishop** [[notes: *Schütze* or "Archer," the standard period term for the Bishop.]]. Although it is much better to take it with the **Queen**, he nevertheless sets down (no. 10) that it should be performed with the **King’s Bishop**, from which, however, the most harmful moves that could possibly exist do follow.

[[margin: 22.]] But if the **White**, according to our opinion, takes the **Black King’s Pawn** not with the **Bishop** (as in no. 10 and no. 21 at the end) but rather with the **Queen** to greater benefit: then the **Black** robs the **White King’s Pawn** with his **King’s Bishop** and moves it against the **White Queen**. The **White** takes the **Black Queen** with the same and offers **Check**. The **Black** takes the same with the **King’s Knight** (for otherwise the **White** would take the same with the **King’s Bishop**). The **White** then moves the **King’s Bishop** to the **Black King’s Knight’s Pawn** at their **Rook’s** [[notes: *Rochen*—an old term for the Rook, derived from the Persian 'rukh'.]] square, wins a **Pawn** as an advantage, and thus obtains a good game (notwithstanding that **Damianus** [[notes: Pedro Damiano, the 16th-century author being critiqued.]] opines that there is no help for him at all).

[[margin: 23.]] Now suppose that the **Black Pawn** is not taken with the **White Queen** (no. 22) but rather with the **White Bishop** (as in no. 10): then the **Black** plays the **King’s Knight** against the **White Queen** on **twenty-seven**. The **White** moves the same against him on **nineteen**. The **Black** takes the **White King’s Pawn** with the **King’s Bishop**.

[[margin: 24. *]] The **White** (no. 11) takes the **Black King’s Knight** with the **Queen** (notwithstanding that her **Pawn** might have been moved to **twenty-one** with more benefit, both to defend the **King’s Bishop** and to open the path for the **King’s Bishop** against the mentioned **Black Knight**). The **Black** takes the **White King’s Bishop** with the **King’s Bishop** and moves it to the **White King’s Rook**. The **White** moves the same to **two**, onto a black square (for reasons mentioned in no. 11). The **Black** offers a **Discovered Check** [[notes: *Ab-Schach*—a move where one piece moves out of the way to unmask a check from a piece behind it.]] from the **Queen’s Bishop** on **twenty-one**. The **White** places the **King** on **eleven**. The **Black** offers **Check** with the **Queen** from **twelve**. The **White** moves the **King** to **eighteen**. The **Black** moves the **Queen** to **forty-four**. The **White** moves his **Queen** to **twenty-six** (no. 13) to prevent that which was indicated in no. 13. The **Black** offers **Check** with the **King’s Bishop** from **forty-five**. The **White** (no. 15) moves the **King** to **twenty-five**. The **Black** moves the **King’s Bishop** against the **White Queen** on **thirty-five**.
