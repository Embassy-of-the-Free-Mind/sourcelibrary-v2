Nor will she have fear, though it were Jove or Dis [[notes: Dis Pater was a Roman god of the underworld, often synonymous with Pluto or Hades. The poet suggests the Queen piece is so powerful she would not fear the gods of heaven or hell.]].
If her Queen, powerful and strong,
  Proudly emerges with her armed ranks,
  To which enemy will she not bring death?
The Horse [[notes: *Caval*, the Knight.]] will avail little, even less the Archer [[notes: *Arciere*, an early term for the Bishop piece, which was often depicted as an archer or *sagittario* in 16th-century Italian chess sets.]]:
  Soon you shall see my Captains
  With cuts and thrusts make your men fall.
Not even the fierce and valiant son of Peleus [[notes: Achilles, the Greek hero of the Iliad.]] 
  Caused such slaughter among the Trojans,
  While Hector’s final cries were heard,
As much as I will cause, if I now engage with you,
  If I set my ranks against yours:
  Ah, what slaughter! Ah, what turmoil!
Therefore, if you wish to fight, make ready
  Your Black folk, arrange everything:
  Make it so your field now faces mine.
I am here gathered with eight Pawns,
  Two Towers [[notes: *Tor*, the Rooks.]], two Archers, and two Steeds,
  And the White King, prepared with his Lady [[notes: *Donna*, the Queen.]].
With these bold and White Warriors,
  I will come into your borders, into your enclosures,
  And I mean to strike down Infantry and Horsemen.
You shall see then, Bombene, your soldiers
  Defeated and dead, and how your King shall suffer
  And beg pardon for past errors.
Do you laugh at this? Do you believe I let these
  Thoughts flee like smoke? That they are fables or dreams
  Which the clear dawn or the Sun might nullify and destroy?
More than a hundred champions have I taken, and every
  Piece of equipment, every weapon they left me when defeated;
  I know not if you rave now, or if you still dream.
Let Spaniards come, or dark-tinted Ethiopians [[notes: In Renaissance chess poetry, such as Marco Girolamo Vida’s *Scacchia Ludus*, the black pieces were often poetically described as Ethiopians or Moors.]],
  Even if they led the entire army of Darius [[notes: Darius III, the Persian King whose vast armies were famously defeated by Alexander the Great.]]...
