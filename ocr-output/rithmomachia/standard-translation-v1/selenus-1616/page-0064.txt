### The First Book / Chapter 2

>> ...departed. Regarding the **manner of playing**, they encountered one another in a formal battle-order, just like those who intend to deliver and maintain a battle in the open field. They also had their goals and boundaries, defining how far they were permitted to go. Thus, one also had to take such heed and precaution that, when the enemy was to be attacked, one did not rush toward him too hastily, thereby exposing oneself and, through imprudence, placing oneself in peril. These stones [[notes: The playing pieces]] were also fashioned from precious gemstones, and both women and men played with them.

From this, it is now to be seen that the game which the Latins call *Ludum Latronum* [[notes: Literally the "Game of Mercenaries" or "Game of Robbers," a Roman strategy game]] is one and the same as our **Game of Chess**. For it is directed just as much toward the delight and exercise of the mind and the art of war as ours is: as can be gathered from the phrases of **Ovid** and **Martial**: *sub imagine Latrocinij* ["under the image of a battle"], *prælia Latronum ludere* ["to play the battles of mercenaries"], *ludere bella Latronum* ["to play the wars of mercenaries"], etc.

Although **Bartholomaeus Merula** [[notes: An Italian humanist scholar and editor of Ovid]], in his commentary on the second and third books of Ovid’s *The Art of Love*, may not have quite hit the mark, **Jacobus Micyllus** [[notes: A German Renaissance humanist and teacher (1503–1558)]], in his explanation of Ovid, has likewise missed the right path. He assumes that this our game is something completely and utterly different from the *Ludo Latrunculorum*. For even granted that among the ancients the stones may have been captured in a different manner than is customary in our times (as can be seen from the writings of Ovid and Pollux), it does not immediately follow from this that it must be an entirely different game. After all, even today, this game is not played with the same moves in every place. For although the Chinese, Persians, and Moors play Chess, it nevertheless happens in a different way than we play it (as can be seen from what we shall soon cite from **Garcia de Orta** and the Jesuit **Nicolas Trigault**). Is there not a difference to be perceived between the **Courier-Game** [[notes: *Currier-Spiel* or Courier Chess, a popular variant played on a 12x8 board]]—or the way the inhabitants of **Stropeke** [[notes: This refers to Ströbeck, a German village famous for its centuries-old tradition of chess-playing]] play it in that place—and that which is otherwise customary? But who would say that those in Stropeke do not play Chess just as much as others who play in the common way?

Within the last three or four hundred years, several people have published various little treatises on the Game of Chess. Among these, the oldest will likely be **Fr. Jacobus de Casallis** [[notes: Better known as Jacobus de Cessolis, a 13th-century Dominican friar whose sermon on chess became one of the most famous books of the Middle Ages]], whom we have already cited above, and who lived around the year 1200 after Christ...
