When then (3.) the White player begins with the **King’s Bishop’s Soldier** [[notes: "Soldaten" or Soldiers refer to the Pawns. The King's Bishop's Soldier is the pawn originally at f2.]], and moves the same to **thirty** [[notes: Gustavus Selenus uses a 1–64 numbering system starting from White's bottom left (a1 is 1, h1 is 8, a8 is 57, and h8 is 64). Square 30 corresponds to f4 in modern notation.]]: then the Black player advances the **King’s Soldier** to **thirty-six** [[notes: Square 36 is d5.]]: The White player, in opposition to him, moves his **King’s Soldier** to **twenty-eight** [[notes: Square 28 is d4.]]: The Black player moves the **King’s Bishop** to **thirty-eight** [[notes: Square 38 is f5.]]: and has thus arranged his game not unfavorably, because he can move his **King’s Knight** [[notes: "Reuter" (Rider) refers to the Knight.]] to **twenty-nine** [[notes: Square 29 is e4.]] without hindrance, and the path for the **King’s Bishop** to harm the enemy is quite free: considering that the White player has moved no Soldier who can easily stop or prevent him: regarding this, one may also see the 3rd Chapter, no. 4 of the fourth Book.

If, however, (4.) the White player begins with the **King’s Bishop’s Soldier** (according to the teaching of the old Chess Masters: *If you wish to keep the game, then move the first for the Old*) [[notes: This appears to be a period maxim suggesting that moving the most important central pawns—the "first" priority—is better than clinging to "old" or passive defensive positions.]] and pushes it to **twenty-seven** [[notes: Square 27 is c4, marking the English Opening. While the author calls it the "King's Bishop's" pawn, square 27 is on the Queen's side; he likely uses the term generally for a Bishop's pawn.]]: then the Black player plays the **King’s Soldier** to **thirty-seven** [[notes: Square 37 is e5.]]: The White player moves the **King’s Soldier** to **twenty-nine** [[notes: Square 29 is e4.]]: The Black player moves the **King’s Bishop** to **thirty-five** [[notes: Square 35 is c5.]], and if he follows through in this manner, as the opportunity is given to him, he will have a much better chance than the White player to arrange his pieces for attack: Likewise, he can move with the **King’s Knight** to **twenty-eight** [[notes: Square 28 is d4.]] freely and without hindrance.

Now if (5.) the White player begins his game with a **Knight’s Soldier** (in order to block the path of the Black player’s Bishop): then the Black player should strive for nothing else but to place the King’s or the King’s Soldiers toward the side where the White player is advancing, and thus strengthen his game.

When then (6.) the White player starts his game with the **King’s Rook’s Soldier**, and moves the same to **thirty-two** [[notes: Square 32 is h4.]] (in order to move the **King’s Rook** [[notes: "Rochen" refers to the Rook or Castle.]] to **twenty-four** [[notes: Square 24 is h3, a maneuver known today as a "rook lift."]]): then the Black player should move the **King’s Soldier** to **thirty-six** [[notes: Square 36 is d5.]], and open the **shooting-hole** [[notes: "Schieß-loch" is a metaphorical term for a clear diagonal or file that allows a long-range piece like a Bishop to "shoot" across the board.]] for his Bishop toward 24 [[notes: h3.]]: But if the White player moves the **King’s Rook’s Soldier** to **funf und zwantzig** [[notes: Square 25 is a4.
