**On the Game of Chess.**

[[margin: 10th Chapter:]]
7. But if White does not take the Black Pawn with his Pawn (as in n. 4), but instead first advances his King’s Pawn to **thirty-six**, against the Black King’s Knight: * then Black plays it [the Knight] to **twenty-eight**: White moves his King’s Bishop [[notes: *Schützen*, or "Archer."] to **thirty-seven** against the same: Black then takes White’s King’s Bishop’s Pawn [[notes: *Schützen-Soldaten* – the Bishop’s Pawn.]] with the same [Knight] (if he wishes to risk it in a sacrifice [[notes: *In die Schantze schlagen* is an idiomatic expression for risking or sacrificing a piece in a gambit.]]); or otherwise (since the Knight is to be valued higher than this Bishop’s Pawn, especially at the start of the game), he moves his King’s Bishop’s Pawn to **thirty-five** for its protection: White takes it regardless with his King’s Bishop: Black takes the same with the King’s Bishop’s Pawn: White takes the Black King’s Pawn with the King’s Bishop’s Pawn and moves the same once more against the Black King’s Bishop, and, provided he understands the game, he arranges his play well in this manner.

8. But if Black does not move the Knight to **28** (as in n. 8), but rather his Queen to **fifty-two** (to keep the White King’s Pawn pinned [[notes: *gefangen zu halten* – literally "to hold captive" or "to pin," preventing the pawn from taking the aforementioned Knight]]), then White takes the Black King’s Pawn with his King’s Bishop’s Pawn, moves against the Black King’s Bishop, and defends his King’s Pawn against the Black Queen: Black then offers Check [[notes: *Schach*]] from **thirty-one** with the same Bishop: White covers the King with his King’s Knight at **thirteen**: Black (in order to ruin White’s game) moves the King’s Bishop’s Pawn onto White’s King’s Bishop’s Pawn at **thirty-eight**: White (so that his King need not fear anything from the Black Queen, and also so that his Pawn may have protection) moves the King’s Knight to **twelve**: Black moves the King’s Knight to **twenty-eight** (so that White’s freed Pawn cannot take it), and in this way will bring the enemy’s game into disorder. Now, if White, before he is put in Check (perhaps if Black should take his King’s Knight with the King’s Bishop), jumps with his King to **two**, he will organize his game much better and prepare himself against the enemy’s incursion. It should also not deter him that it may appear as though the King’s Rook [[notes: *Roche*, the traditional term for the Rook/Castle.]] would be enclosed by this jump and unable to be used; for after a few moves, he can free it again and obtain a great advantage over the other player.
