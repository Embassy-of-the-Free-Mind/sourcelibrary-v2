...before he may follow in the **Queen's** footsteps [[notes: A metaphor for a Pawn reaching the final rank to be promoted to a Queen]], if he is missing such an opportunity and stands upon the **Knight's** [[notes: *Reuter*; literally "Rider"]] or **Rook's** [[notes: *Rochen*; the archaic term for the Rook]] line; while the other player still has their **Queen** and places her, or the **King**, onto the square where the first player intended to make his **Pawn** [[notes: *Soldat*; literally "Soldier"]] into a **Queen**: then that player (provided he is familiar with this game) must take care not to place his **King** on the **Rook's** line. This holds true even if the opponent tries to force him there with the **Queen**, intending to give him **Checkmate** within perhaps two more moves alongside the **King**. For such an attempt is in vain and for nothing, because he can always move the King along such a line in such a way that the opponent’s **Queen**, together with her **King**, must move more toward the corners or angles rather than straight ahead or diagonally to reach him. If this is done, the other player can never win that same square or—unless the first player is altogether too negligent and careless—inflict harm or take it by force.

However, if the **Pawn** does not stand upon the **Knight's** or **Rook's** line, but rather upon the lines of the other pieces [[notes: The central files: Bishop, Queen, or King files]], and the opponent's **King** or **Queen** has moved in front of him, then the **King** can be driven by force to such a place where one may deliver **Checkmate**. It should be noted here that in some places, particularly in **Spain**, it is customary in this case to be able to give **Checkmate** even if all pieces have been taken from the **King** [[notes: This refers to a historical rule variation where "bare king" did not automatically end the game or result in a draw, depending on the region]]. For the **King** cannot prevent the opposing **Queen** from either taking the **Pawn** or driving it so far until she, with her **King**, comes close enough to those places where she can deliver **Checkmate**. 

But if the **Pawn** stands on the outermost lines and must not be taken, then one cannot give **Checkmate**; instead, the **King's** movement is merely blocked [[notes: This describes a stalemate, which in many historical rulesets was treated differently than a modern draw]]. In such a case, one should strive to draw the **King** into a **Rook's** square and thus give cause and occasion for this "locking in," since no other protection can be hoped for.

When one player, alongside the **King**, has a **Pawn** intending to make it a **Queen**, but the other has only the bare **King** (the movement of his other pieces having been hindered) and can still move: then this player must see to it that he moves his **King** in front of that **Pawn** in good time.
