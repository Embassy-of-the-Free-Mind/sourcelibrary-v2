# 8th Chapter: On the Game of Chess.

<image-desc>A decorative horizontal woodcut banner featuring foliage and two circular portraits at the ends, framing the start of the chapter.</image-desc>

## The Eighth Chapter.

### Concerning the Archers / both of the Kings / and of the Queens : where they should be placed / how they are moved / and why they are esteemed better / than the Knights / in this game.

<image-desc>A framed woodcut illustration of a chess piece. The piece consists of a decorative, chalice-like pedestal topped with a bust of an archer wearing a cap and holding a bow with an arrow. Beneath the frame is the caption "Ein Schütze."</image-desc>

->An Archer.<-

After the Rooks / those are held to be the most noble stones [[notes: *Steine*, literally "stones," was the common term for chess pieces]] / which were called in former times / *Viros Consulares*, Council-Men : Otherwise also *delphinos* (that is / Princes or Lords / as the Spaniards interpret it : or lovers of mankind : as *Actius, quæst: 6. n. 5.* contends) and then Priests [[notes: *Pfaffen*; while this word later became derogatory, here it refers to the clerical identity of the piece, which survives in the English name "Bishop"]] : (perhaps because / among the common loudmouths [[notes: *Scharhänsen*, a term for boastful or talkative people]] / Councilors / and the like / are cried out as Priests / or Elders / just as in our own times / that certain great Potentate called all [such] figures / Poets : or else / because one has observed the custom / wherein the Clergy allowed themselves to be used extensively / not only in political affairs / but especially also / in matters of war / and they were therefore included / in this game of conflict [[notes: *Streits-Spiel*, an allegorical term for chess as a simulation of war]] : so that the proverb / remains true here as well:

> *Quicquid agit Mundus, Monachus vult esse Secundus :*
> No matter what anyone may undertake
> The Priest wants to be the second man)

or Ensigns [[notes: *Fendriche*, or standard-bearers]]: Wherefore the Italians [call them] / *Alfieri*, as if *Alferios, i. e.* [[notes: The author is correctly identifying the etymology of the Italian *alfiere*, which stems from the Arabic *al-fil* (the elephant), though here it is being linked to military rank.]]
