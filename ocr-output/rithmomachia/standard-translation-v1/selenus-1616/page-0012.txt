Furthermore, the **Author** has directed his manner of writing—according to his intended **Work** and **Subject matter**—as much as was possible: In doing so, He has wished to be of use to both the **Beginning** and the **Experienced Chess-Player** [[notes: Translated from *Schach-Zieher*, literally "chess-mover"]]. However, insofar as He might be seen as **Obscure** and **Incomprehensible** in certain places: those who are beginning and still inexperienced should not, for that reason, lose heart; considering that through diligence they may finally grasp his meaning in due order and form an understanding of it. Yet, should He appear **too clear and plain**, the experienced **Chess-Players** should nevertheless consider that some thought must still be applied to it, and that He wished to accommodate the beginners in order to keep them encouraged.

If one should also wish to accuse the **Author** of unnecessary, practiced **Wordiness** [[notes: Translated from *weitleuffigkeit*, meaning prolixity or lengthy digression]] in a matter that is not quite so important: He has primarily followed the advice of **Pliny the Younger** [[notes: Gaius Plinius Caecilius Secundus (61–c. 113 AD), a Roman lawyer and author known for his many letters]], who in Book 5, Epistle 6, expressed the following words (as they were cited by **Luis de Alcázar** [[notes: A Spanish Jesuit theologian (1554–1613). The author is using a heavy theological commentary to justify the length of his chess book—a common scholarly flourish of the 1600s]], from Seville, of the Society of Jesus, Doctor of Theology, in his *Investigation into the Secret Sense of the Apocalypse*, Volume 1, page 22):

» *First, I consider it the duty of a **Writer** to read his **Title** and repeatedly ask himself what he began to write, and to know that if he dwells on the **Subject Matter**, it is not long; but it is very long if he fetches and drags in something else. He is brief who does what he set out to do. For this is not a **Digression**, but the **Work** itself. If nothing is introduced that is, so to speak, **Off the Path**, then it is not the **Letter** that describes it, but the **Subject** being described that is **Great**.*

**Martial** [[notes: A Roman poet famous for his short, witty epigrams]] has composed this same opinion in the following short verse:

» *Those things are not long from which there is nothing you could take away.*

[[summary: The author defends his writing style, explaining that he balances complexity for experts with clarity for beginners, and justifies the book's length by arguing that staying on-topic is never "too long."]]
