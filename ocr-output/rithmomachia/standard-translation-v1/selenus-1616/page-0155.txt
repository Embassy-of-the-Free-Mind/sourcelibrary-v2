# 1. cap: Of the Game of Chess.

...should, and that the German language actually suggests this usage in the words **Schach** [[notes: Check]] and **Mat** [[notes: Mate]]: For when one announces a **Schach** to the King, it means as much as "be hunted" or "yield from your place." Which the King must then also do: unless He can still bring one of his pieces forward, or clear the "checker" [[notes: The piece giving check]] from the **Tabel** [[notes: The game board]] with the same. But when one says **Schach-Mat**, it signifies: you shall be hunted and made so faint and powerless [[notes: The author is connecting the word *Mat* to the German *matt*, meaning exhausted or faint]] by this attack that you cannot proceed further, but must surrender. From this it follows that it will not be necessary to seek the *derivationes* [[notes: etymological origins]] from other ancient or far-flung peoples' languages. And although these words could be drawn from them based on the meaning of the aforementioned usage, they certainly do not allow themselves to be so easily *appliciren* [[notes: applied]] or twisted to that end. And even granted that they brought something similar with them (for, as **Lutherus** [[notes: Martin Luther (1483–1546), the German reformer and translator of the Bible]], in *Genesis*, page 617.b. states: *Multæ dictiones, compluribus linguis, communes ſunt. Non enim ſic diſtortæ ſunt linguæ, ut non ſit aliqua vocum interdum ſimilitudo & congruentia.*: That is: There are many words that are common to many languages. For languages are not so entirely torn apart from one another that at times some should not be similar to each other and rhyme with one another) so we might be searching for such *derivationes* too far and too painstakingly, much like those who trace the *Sileſios* [[notes: Silesians]] from the *Eliſijs* [[notes: Elysium]], and the *Vliſſinger* in Zeeland from *Vlyſſe* [[notes: Ulysses/Odysseus]] (as **Matth: Quad** [[notes: Matthias Quad (1557–1613), a noted geographer and engraver]] states on pages 177 and 358).

But as to why these words are used alone for the Kings, and not for the other pieces as well (since it is common to all pieces that they are hunted from the board; as we also concluded in the 2nd Chapter of the previous Book, that the name of the **Schach-Spiel** [[notes: Chess game]] might perhaps have come from this), the reason for it seems to be this: that the other pieces, after a hostile attack, can be robbed [[notes: captured]], but Kings are only hunted, and finally compelled to surrender. Regarding the former, when one has made a plan and an attack against the enemy, it seems absurd that one should also warn him that his pieces might be robbed: Therefore, the word **Schach** is rightly omitted [for other pieces], and he may keep a careful watch over his own chances himself.

[[summary: The author argues that the terms "Schach" and "Mat" have natural meanings in German related to hunting and exhaustion, making complex foreign etymologies unnecessary. He also explains that "Check" is only called for the King because he is meant to be pursued and captured, whereas other pieces are simply taken without warning.]]
