[[margin: Chapter 8:]] ...for the other pieces and the **advantage** [[notes: *Vorzug*, referring here to the "odds" or extra moves given to a weaker player]] to be taken with more benefit than they were given; it is advisable for a player to act in this manner. He must, however, know how to arrange the game so that he may again fill the gap left by the **Soldier** [[notes: *Soldat*, the common term for a Pawn]] and close it in haste. Likewise, the **Rook** [[notes: *Roche*, the Rook]] is a noble and valuable piece upon which one can well rely at all times, but especially when the game becomes somewhat dangerous toward the end and threatens a loss.

Inexperienced players may (5.) consider it an equal trade when they take a **Knight** [[notes: *Reuter*, literally "Rider," the standard term for the Knight in this period]] from an opponent to whom they have given a **Rook** as an advantage, imagining that they can make better use of their Knight than the Rook. However, they do not calculate the account correctly. For it is a greater advantage to give away a **Rook** for a **Knight** than to give away the **King’s-Archer’s-Soldier** [[notes: The King's Bishop's Pawn; the "Archer" or *Schütze* refers to the Bishop]] or the initial advantage. This is even more dangerous for those who do not know how to create profit and benefit for themselves with that same Soldier and advantage, considering that they will subsequently commit one error after another.

If (6.) one is given the choice, along with the **Knight**, of either taking the **King’s-Archer’s-Soldier** and two moves, or placing the **King** in a corner and taking two moves [[notes: This refers to a "King's Leap" or early form of castling where the King is moved to a safe corner square in one turn]]: in that case, he takes the **Knight** and the **Soldier** together with both advantages with more profit than taking the King so placed along with the two moves. For in the former case, all the pieces are more capable and fit to begin the struggle with greater freedom and to capture the opponent’s pieces; likewise, the opponent must take great care of his **King** due to the lack of his **Archer’s-Soldier**.

Furthermore, there are (7.) some who are accustomed to giving the opponent the advantage that the **Queen**, beyond her usual movement, can be moved upon the chess-board like a **Knight** [[notes: This describes a powerful "fairy chess" piece often called the Amazon, which combines the moves of a Queen and a Knight]]. In return, they receive a **Knight**, as if they had gained in the trade and "fished very well" [[notes: *sehr wol fischeten*, a period idiom for making a lucky or profitable deal]]. But they err greatly: for the **Queen** is such a piece that can do more and be moved further than all the others; and if the power to wander about like a **Knight** is given to her besides, she is very difficult and troublesome to resist.
