## The Third Chapter.

### Of the third variety, when White plays without the King’s Bishop’s Soldier and has the first move, but may only use one piece.

Furthermore, when White does not take the two moves (of which we spoke in the preceding chapter) but instead only takes the lead- or first-move for the aforementioned King’s Bishop’s Soldier [[notes: *Schützens-Soldat* refers to the pawn standing in front of the Bishop, which was commonly called the "Archer" or "Marksman" in 17th-century Germany]]: then from this arises the third variety, in the following manner:

[[margin: 1.]] If White then moves the King’s Soldier [[notes: *Soldat* (Soldier) is the author's term for a Pawn]] to twenty [[notes: Selenus uses a coordinate system numbering the board from 1 to 64, starting from White's bottom-left (a1) to Black's top-right (h8)]]: then Black moves his King’s Soldier to thirty-six: White [moves] the same King’s Soldier to [[margin: 2.]] twenty-nine: * Black takes him with the same: White [takes] the same with the King’s Soldier: Black declares **Check** with the **Queen** from twenty-five: White moves between them and against her the King’s Knight’s Soldier [[notes: *Reuters-Soldat*]] to eighteen: Black declares **Check** once again with the same from twenty-eight, and moves her to the White King’s Rook: White (so that he does not lose the same) covers the **King** with the **Queen** at twelve: and the **Queens** on both sides are taken by force: in consideration that they, for the sake of their Kings (whom they must not allow to fall into **Check** or remain uncovered), cannot be moved forward. Thus, the removed Soldier brings a very poor disadvantage if the game is played without the Queens.

[[margin: 3.]] But if Black does not take the White Soldier (no. 2), but instead moves the King’s Soldier to twenty-eight: then White plays the King’s Bishop’s Soldier to thirty, arranges his game very well in this way, and can move his pieces further into the field with deliberation.
