# The Second Book / 5. cap:

(as can be seen from the previous chapter) runs contrary to the custom of this **game**: moving backward and forward [[notes: This refers to the "touch-move" rule or the prohibition against retracting a move to correct an oversight]]. It would appear as if someone wished to replace and improve upon that which had been neglected or ignored, against their own knowledge and conscience (which, in all fairness, one should strive to uphold). Now, it does sometimes happen that a person plays with **two pieces** [[notes: *Steinen*; literally "stones"]] at once: if this occurs from the beginning without any premeditation or notice, it is fair to let it be and not change it back. However, if it is an attempt to compensate for a previous oversight, it can rightly be prevented and not permitted. Accordingly, whenever a piece is moved that can reach the **King** (to finish him off [[notes: *den garausß zu machen*; an idiom for "to end" or "to kill"]] on the next move, provided He does not move away or otherwise protect Himself), one should always say **Check** [[notes: *Schach*]]. For, if the other player makes the same move, or acts as if He did not notice it, the damage caused by the neglect and oversight is to be blamed on no one other than the one who failed to heed the announcement of the Check.

Furthermore, when a player covers his King against a Check with one of his pieces, and thus fends off the offered Check: but subsequently moves that same piece to another square before the King has been moved out of the covered Check (because the opponent’s piece, by which He was placed in Check, is still present on the same line), thereby leaving the King openly exposed and uncovered; and if He realizes that this move was made unlawfully: then it shall rest upon the **discretion of the opponent** whether he will allow that move to stand or not. If the opponent is not satisfied with this move, then the King—who must pay the penalty—shall be moved and played in place of the piece that was unlawfully moved [[notes: This describes an illegal move where a player moves a pinned piece, exposing their own King to "discovered check." In this era, the penalty for an illegal move often involved being forced to move the King instead]].

Finally, when one has the opponent's King in flight, he should continually press and pursue him by offering Checks, so that the King—if given any respite—does not fortify himself again with his pieces and ultimately cause damage to the opponent's game.

Regarding **Checkmating** [[notes: *Schach-Matten*]], the following should further be carefully noted:

That (1.) a **Chess-player** [[notes: *Schach-Zieher*; literally "Chess-mover"]] should not easily or rashly blurt out the word **Checkmate** [[notes: *Schach-Mat*]] when victory is not yet certain. In some places, the custom is indeed that the one who says Checkmate (believing that the opponent’s King can neither retreat nor be helped), should that King unexpectedly find a loophole or assistance, in that case, the same game/ [[notes: The sentence breaks off here and continues on the next page.]]
