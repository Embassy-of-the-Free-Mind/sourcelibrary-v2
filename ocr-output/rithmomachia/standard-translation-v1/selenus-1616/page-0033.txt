### 3.
-> ### Of the Game of Chess. <-
-> A iij <-

[[margin: Chapter 1]]
[...moderation], when it is played without any greed or agitation of the
spirit, at the right time and with a certain measure—perhaps by melan-
choly people to delight and refresh themselves: just as one is ac-
customed to delighting oneself with string-music, or to use 
leisure, sleep, or other rest, when one has done justice to 
more important and distinguished matters. For it is written that the 
first invention of play was not intended for any evil or vice-ridden 
end: considering that the Lydians, for the sake of 
variety, so that they could better endure hunger, are said to 
have invented all sorts of games [[notes: This refers to a famous story by the Greek historian Herodotus, who claimed the Lydians invented games like dice to distract themselves during an 18-year famine, eating only every other day]]. Thus also, the Stone-Game (which is 
commonly called Chess [[notes: "Stone-game" or *Steinspiel* was a general term for board games played with pieces or "stones"]]) was invented to the end that soldiers, 
in a long-lasting siege, could better dispel and pass their 
idleness. Thus also writes the poet *Horace*: that not the playing, 
but rather not knowing how to give measure to the game, is a shame. 
Moreover, distinguished people have also played, such as *Cato*, 
*Portius*, *Domitian*, *Claudian* (who wrote a book on the Art of Play), 
the Emperors *Verus* and *Augustus*; *Maecenas* 
however, and *Q. Galba*, also moved the Chess pieces. *Caesar*, 
*Scaevola*, and *Ticinius* were excellent ball-players.

Therefore, this should be well and diligently noted: 
that firstly, with this entire game, nothing else than 
the amusement, delight, and liberation of the spirit 
must be viewed and sought as the primary, ultimate, and true purpose. 
And secondly, that the game is the very work which this art, 
in its place, just like **Music**, should present and leave behind: 
just as otherwise the building and other arts leave a house or 
work behind them after the labor is finished [[notes: The author is distinguishing between "mechanical" arts like architecture, which result in a physical object, and "active" arts like music or chess, where the "work" is the performance or the play itself]]. From which it further 
appears that the Art of Chess belongs among those arts 
which are called in Latin *disciplinas activas* [active disciplines], 
or properly, in a single word, *Artes* [Arts], and whose 
practice rests upon a certain form and experience.

Now, as concerns the form and shape here: it has the 
same, in part, from **Mathematics**, from which (as *Plato* 
testifies) all arts flow: namely from **Geometry**...
