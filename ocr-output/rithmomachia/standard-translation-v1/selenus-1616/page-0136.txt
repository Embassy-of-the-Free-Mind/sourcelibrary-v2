### The First Book / 11. cap:

How one must set the pieces upon the **Chess-Table** [[notes: *Schach-Tabel*, an older term for the board]] shall be understood from the following report and figures:

The **Sixteen Main Pieces** take their places opposite one another on the outermost two lines: the common **Soldiers** [[notes: *Soldaten*, referring to the pawns]] occupy the next two **transverse lines** [[notes: *zwerch-linien*, or ranks]] in front of them. (Therefore, six squares or ranks remain between the main pieces, and four squares remain between the soldiers.) They are placed, one after the other, into different squares according to this order: The **White King** to the right side, in a black square; his **Archer** [[notes: *Schütze*, the historical German name for the Bishop]] in a white; his **Rider** [[notes: *Reuter*, the Knight]] in a black; his **Rook** [[notes: *Roche*]] in a white. To the left side, the **White Queen**, next to the King, in a white square; her **Archer** in a black; her **Rider** in a white; and her **Rook** in a black square. In the opposing play, the pieces of the **Black host** are placed opposite on the other color. Accordingly, one sets the pieces not only on opposing squares, but also on opposing sides and corners: for he with the white pieces arranges and sets his **King**, with his seven pieces, on the right; and the **Queen**, with her company, on the left; while he with the black pieces sets his **King** and his company on the left, and the **Queen** with her seven pieces on the right hand. Thus, both **Kings**, as well as both **Queens**, and the other main pieces—namely, the four **Rooks**, **Archers**, **Riders**, and sixteen **Soldiers**—stand opposite one another according to the division of the following figures.

Now, when the **field-camps** [[notes: *Feld-Läger*, a military metaphor for the starting setup]] of both sides are thus established, then because of this stationing, one piece has protection and defense from another in the following manner: For the **King** keeps watch over or protects, from his square, the following five pieces: (1.) his **Queen**; (2.) his **Archer**; (3.) his own **Soldier**, (4.) his **Queen's Soldier**, and (5.) his **Archer’s Soldier**. The **Queen** defends (1.) her **King**; (2.) her **Archer**; (3.) her own **Soldier**, (4.) her **King’s Soldier**, and (5.) her **Archer’s Soldier**. The **King’s Rook** keeps watch (1.) over his **Soldier**; and (2.) over the **King’s Rider**; the **Queen’s Rook** has equal power. The **King’s Archer** pays heed to (1.) the **King’s Soldier** and (2.) that **Rider’s Soldier**. The **Queen’s Archer** defends (1.) her own **Soldier** and (2.) her **Rider’s Soldier**. The **Riders** keep watch over three squares, such as (1.) the **King’s** or **Queen’s Soldier**; and (2. 3.) the third square in front of the **Archers** and **Rooks**. The **King’s Soldier** keeps watch (1.) over the third square of the **Queen**; and (2.) that of the **King’s Archer**.
