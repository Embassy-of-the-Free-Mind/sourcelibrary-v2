# P R E F A C E .

[[meta: Catchword: the (It. *lo*)]]

[[image-desc: A large decorative woodcut initial 'R' at the beginning of the text. Inside the frame of the letter, a bearded figure (likely a philosopher) is seated on the right, gesturing with his hand. A standing figure and a landscape with buildings are visible in the background. A small dog sits at the feet of the seated man.]]

*The ancient Philosophers discovered many most beautiful games to uplift and recreate human minds weary and exhausted by the study of the sciences; the greater part of these—and nearly all of them—due to the long passage of time, wars, and the lack of those who cared to restore them, have been lost. Thus, in our current age, we have no knowledge of such games, except for one discovered by the most wise Pythagoras, named **Rythmomachia**,* [[notes: Rythmomachia, often called "The Philosophers' Game," is a complex mathematical board game. The name is derived from the Greek words for "harmony" or "proportion" (*rythmos*) and "battle" (*mache*).]] *which means nothing other than a battle of the harmony of numbers. This game, in the common judgment of all men of spirit, is deemed most ingenious, most useful, and most delightful, and truly worthy of such a famous Author. For as it is founded upon numbers and musical consonances caused by those numbers, and filled with many clever devices, it cannot help but greatly delight and benefit the minds of students, keeping them—while playing and joking—practiced in **Arithmetic** and **Music**, most noble and useful sciences.* [[notes: In the Renaissance, Music and Arithmetic were two of the four "Quadrivium" arts. Music was understood as the study of number in time, or proportions.]] *This game is found written in the Latin language as a very brief dialogue by **Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples**;* [[notes: Known in the text as Iacomo Fabro Stapulense (c. 1455–1536), a French humanist who published a version of these rules in 1496.]] *and later illustrated and expanded by **Claude de Boissière** of Dauphiné:* [[notes: Known in the text as Claudio Ruxero Delfinate, a French mathematician who published a detailed work on the game in 1554.]] *But the true original composed by Pythagoras in the Greek language is not to be found (so far as I know) at this hour. Therefore, wishing in this little treatise of mine to translate this game into the vernacular,* [[notes: "Vernacular" here refers to Italian, making the text accessible to those who did not read scholarly Latin.]] *it is my intention to make use of the labor of the two aforementioned authors, though not binding myself to translate it word for word in the manner in which it was written by them, but rather in the manner of a Paraphrase, so as to be able to expand some things said by them too briefly, and likewise to condense others treated by them at too great a length, and finally to make easier everything that was written by them somewhat obscurely. But so that I may proceed in an orderly fashion, I shall divide this entire present treatise into six principal parts. In the first, I shall describe the form of the **Board**, and of the pieces, that upon that* [[notes: The text ends mid-sentence. The catchword "lo" at the bottom of the page indicates the word would be "quello" (that), referring back to the board/table.]]

[[summary: Francesco Barozzi introduces Rythmomachia as a noble Pythagorean game that combines mathematical and musical theory. He explains his plan to synthesize and clarify earlier Latin works by Lefèvre d'Étaples and Claude de Boissière into a more accessible Italian guide.]]
